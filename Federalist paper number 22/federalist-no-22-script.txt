At the end of the last episode, Hamilton was standing over a patient. For three papers before that, he and Madison had walked us through a graveyard of dead confederacies, ancient Greece, the Holy Roman Empire, the Swiss cantons, the Dutch republic. And then, last time, he turned the lamp around and aimed it at the living government in the room, the Articles of Confederation, the rickety system the thirteen states were actually struggling along under in the winter of seventeen eighty-seven. He examined it like a doctor, and he named three things wrong with it. A government that could not enforce its own laws. A government that could not protect one of its own states from being overthrown by force. And a government that could not even raise money fairly, because it split its bills among the states by a measurement of wealth that does not actually exist.

Three defects. And underneath all three, one single disease. A government that tries to act on states instead of on people. A government that can only ask a province to obey, instead of commanding a citizen.

That was Federalist Number Twenty-One. And Hamilton was not finished. He told us so himself, right at the end. There was more wrong with the Articles, and the next batch of defects were big ones.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-Two. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. Published in the New York Packet on December fourteenth, seventeen eighty-seven, just two days after the last one. And here is what makes this paper different from the one before it. Episode Twenty-One was a doctor listing symptoms. This one starts the same way, with more symptoms, more defects, and then, in its very last breath, it stops being a list at all. It turns into a verdict. Hamilton stops saying here is another thing that is broken, and starts saying this patient cannot be cured, only replaced. And then, in the final lines, he names the one thing the replacement has to be built on. We will get there. But first, the defects. And the first one is the biggest of them all.

The first defect Hamilton names is that the government had no power to regulate commerce. No power over trade.

Now, that can sound dry and technical, like a footnote for economists. It is not. It may be the single most consequential failure in the entire list, because it is, more than any other, the thing that actually dragged the country into the room where the Constitution got written. So let me make it concrete.

Under the Articles, each of the thirteen states ran its own trade policy. Each state set its own tariffs, its own import duties, its own rules about whose ships could dock and whose goods could pass. There was no national hand on the wheel. And the results were exactly what you would expect when you give thirteen neighbors thirteen different sets of rules and no referee.

Two things went wrong, one facing outward, one facing inward.

Facing outward, toward the rest of the world, America was helpless. Picture trying to negotiate a trade deal with Britain or France when you are not one country but thirteen. A foreign nation had no reason to give the United States anything, because why would they? They could already sell into every American market they wanted, and if they made a promise to one Congress, any single state could turn around and break it the next month. Hamilton points out that Britain, in particular, had figured this out completely. British ministers openly said, in so many words, that there was no need to offer America a fair commercial treaty, because the American government was too weak and too divided to do anything about it. Britain could flood American markets with British goods, keep American ships out of British ports, and pay no price for it, because there was no single American authority strong enough to push back. Several states tried to retaliate on their own, slapping their own restrictions on British trade. But because thirteen states could never agree to do it together, every one of those efforts collapsed. A patchwork of thirteen separate trade policies is not a negotiating position. It is an invitation to be played.

But it was the inward-facing problem that was the truly dangerous one. Because the states had started doing it to each other.

Here is the thing about trade in the seventeen eighties. The states were not just trading with Europe. They were trading with each other, constantly. And the ones that happened to control the great ports, New York above all, realized they could tax that trade as it flowed through. So New York taxed goods coming through New York harbor, even when those goods were really bound for a customer in New Jersey or Connecticut. The neighbor paid the tax, and New York pocketed the money. And the neighbors, naturally, were furious. New Jersey, squeezed between the big ports of New York and Philadelphia and taxed by both, was described at the time as a cask tapped at both ends. So New Jersey retaliated. When New York ran a lighthouse out on a spit of New Jersey land, New Jersey turned around and slapped a punishing tax on it. This was happening, neighbor against neighbor, inside the same union, in the same years Hamilton was writing.

And Hamilton saw exactly where it was heading. He says that these interfering, unneighborly regulations, if no national authority steps in to stop them, would multiply and spread until they became serious sources of animosity and discord. To show his readers what that road looks like at the end, he reaches across the ocean for an example. He quotes a description of the German empire, the patchwork of hundreds of little German principalities, where every petty prince along a river charged his own toll on every boat that passed. The result, the description says, was that the fine streams and navigable rivers with which Germany is so happily watered are rendered almost useless. Picture that. Some of the best rivers in Europe, natural highways for trade, choked into uselessness, not by any natural barrier, but purely by the greed of dozens of little governments each demanding a cut at its own stretch of the bank. A boat going downriver might be stopped and taxed twenty times before it reached the sea.

And then Hamilton brings it home, and this is the line that should land. He says that if the states keep going this way, the citizens of each state would eventually come to be considered and treated by the others, in his words, "in no better light than that of foreigners and aliens."

Foreigners and aliens. Think about what that means. A merchant from Connecticut, arriving in New York, treated not as a fellow American but as a foreigner, taxed at the border, viewed with suspicion, subject to a separate set of hostile rules. Hamilton is warning that without a single national authority over trade, the United States would slowly stop feeling like one country at all, not because of any dramatic war, but because of the steady, grinding accumulation of trade barriers, each state walling itself off from its neighbors one tariff at a time.

At this point you might be wondering why the founders had not simply fixed this already, if it was so obvious and so dangerous. And the answer is the reason this defect matters so much. They had tried. The mess over commerce was so bad that in seventeen eighty-six, the states sent delegates to a meeting at Annapolis specifically to deal with trade. That meeting failed to fix anything on its own, too few states showed up, but the delegates who did come issued a call for a bigger convention the following year, in Philadelphia, to deal with the deeper problems of the union. That bigger meeting was the Constitutional Convention. So the failure to manage commerce is not just one item on Hamilton's list. It is, in a real sense, the spark that lit the whole fire. The reason there was a Constitution to argue about at all traces directly back to the chaos this defect created.

And the fix, when it came, was decisive. The new Constitution would hand the national government the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations and among the several states. That one clause is the reason that today you can ship a truckload of goods from Connecticut to New York to New Jersey without stopping at a single border, without paying a single internal tariff, without anyone treating you as a foreigner. The enormous free internal market that the American economy is built on exists because Hamilton and the others looked at this defect and decided to kill it at the root. If you want to see the same experiment run again in modern times, look at how hard the nations of Europe worked, two centuries later, to build a single market where goods move freely across borders. They were solving the exact problem Hamilton is describing here, the problem of neighbors strangling each other's trade.

So that is the first and biggest defect. No power over commerce.

Now Hamilton turns to a second defect, and here I am going to move quickly, because we have actually already met its twin. Last episode, remember, we spent a long time on the way the old government raised money, the quota system, where the national government could not tax anybody, so it just split the bill among the states and mailed out requests and waited, usually in vain. Well, it turns out they did the very same thing with soldiers.

When the country needed an army, during the Revolution itself, the national government could not raise troops directly. So it did exactly what it did with money. It assigned each state a quota of men and asked them to fill it. And you might be thinking, all right, but surely raising soldiers worked better than raising money. It did not. It produced its own special kind of mess. Hamilton says it gave birth to a competition between the states that turned into, in his wonderful phrase, "a kind of auction for men." Picture it. Each state, desperate to fill its quota, offering bigger and bigger cash bounties to get men to sign up. So the states started bidding against each other for the same pool of recruits, driving the price of a soldier higher and higher. And the men, no fools, learned to wait, holding out for a better offer, because tomorrow's bounty would be bigger than today's. The result was slow, thin, expensive enlistments at the exact moments the country could least afford them, during the most dangerous stretches of a war for survival.

And just like with money, the burden fell unfairly. The states with a war actually raging on their soil scrambled to fill their quotas, while the states far from the fighting shrugged and dragged their feet. Hamilton's verdict on the whole quota approach, whether you apply it to dollars or to soldiers, is brutal and worth quoting, because it is the through-line of these two episodes. He calls it "a system of imbecility in the Union, and of inequality and injustice among the members." The same rotten machinery, the same disease from last time, just pointed at a different target.

That is the second defect, and we can leave it there, because it is really the old quota sickness wearing a uniform.

The third defect is where Hamilton gets into something deeper, something about the basic math of how the old Congress made decisions. And it comes in two parts, because there were two separate things wrong with the voting, and the second is far more dangerous than the first.

The first part is the one people remember. Under the Articles, every state got one vote. One state, one vote. And that meant tiny Rhode Island had exactly the same weight in the national councils as Massachusetts, or New York, or Virginia. Delaware, with a fraction of the population, had an equal voice with Pennsylvania. Hamilton says this violates the most fundamental rule of any government that calls itself republican, the rule that the sense of the majority should prevail.

Now, a fair question here is: so what? Rhode Island is small. Why does it matter if a little state gets a vote equal to a big one? Isn't that almost charming, the small standing equal to the great?

Hamilton's answer is that it is not charming at all, because it means a minority can rule the majority. He points out that you could assemble a group of the smallest states, states that together hold only a small fraction of the American people, and that little group could outvote, and impose its will on, states representing the vast majority of the population. A handful of people in the small states could dictate to everyone else. And he warns that the big states would not put up with this forever. He says the larger states would, in time, "revolt from the idea of receiving the law from the smaller." You cannot, over the long run, ask the majority of a free people to take orders from the minority. Something will eventually break.

That is the first part of the voting problem, the unfairness of one state, one vote. But now Hamilton turns to the second part, and this is the one that genuinely alarms him, because it takes that flaw and weaponizes it.

It was not enough, under the old system, to get a simple majority of states to agree. For the most important decisions, you needed a supermajority, the agreement of nine of the thirteen states. And that sounds responsible, doesn't it? It sounds careful. Surely requiring a big consensus before the government acts is a safeguard, a protection against rash decisions. That is certainly what its designers thought.

Hamilton says it is the opposite. He says it is a disaster disguised as a safeguard. And he puts it in one of the sharpest lines in the paper. He says that this rule, "what at first sight may seem a remedy, is, in reality, a poison."

Here is why. When you require far more than a majority to get anything done, you have not empowered the majority. You have empowered the minority to block. Think it through. If you need nine states to act, then five states, just five, by refusing, can stop the entire government in its tracks. A minority gets a veto. And a small, stubborn, or even bribed minority can hold the whole country hostage, forcing the majority to either give up on acting at all, or to buy off the holdouts with concessions. Hamilton runs through what this produces in practice: endless delay, constant backroom negotiation and intrigue, and what he calls contemptible compromises of the public good. And in the worst case, when no deal can be struck at all, the government simply seizes up and does nothing, frozen, at the very moments it most needs to move.

And to show his readers exactly where this road ends, Hamilton points to a country they all knew was dying of precisely this disease. He says Congress had repeatedly found itself in the situation of a Polish diet, where a single vote was enough to put a stop to all its movements.

Let me tell you about the Polish diet, because it is the most chilling example in the paper, and Hamilton's readers would have felt it in their bones. Poland in the eighteenth century had a rule in its parliament so extreme it is almost hard to believe. It was called the free veto. And it meant that any single member of the assembly, any one nobleman, could stand up, object to a measure, and not only kill that measure but dissolve the entire session and wipe out everything it had passed. One man. One voice. Total paralysis. The whole national legislature, undone by a single objection.

You can imagine what this did to Poland. The government could almost never act, because there was always someone, somewhere, who could be persuaded, or paid, to stand up and say no. And here is the part that would have chilled every American reading this in seventeen eighty-seven. Poland's neighbors had figured this out. The great powers surrounding it, Russia, Prussia, Austria, learned that they did not need to conquer Poland. They just needed to keep it paralyzed. They could bribe a single Polish nobleman to veto anything that might make Poland stronger, and so keep it permanently weak, permanently helpless. And then, in seventeen seventy-two, just fifteen years before Hamilton sat down to write, those neighbors had simply begun helping themselves, carving off great slices of Polish territory while the paralyzed Polish government could do nothing to stop them. Hamilton's readers were watching, in real time, a once-great nation being eaten alive, and the thing that had made it edible was exactly the rule Hamilton is warning about. The need for too much agreement. The minority's power to freeze the whole. For modern listeners, I will add the ending Hamilton could not yet know: within eight years of this essay, Poland would be carved up completely and vanish from the map of Europe entirely, erased as an independent country for over a century. The paralysis did not just weaken Poland. It killed it.

That is what Hamilton means when he calls the supermajority rule a poison. It looks like caution. It functions like a slow death.

So why does that matter so much? Here is where Hamilton connects it to something even more frightening, and it is the next defect on his list. He says a system where a minority can block everything is not just slow. It is dangerously easy to corrupt from the outside.

Think about it from the perspective of a hostile foreign power. If you want to stop the American government from doing something, how many people do you have to get to? In a system run by simple majority, you would have to bribe or pressure a majority, a lot of people, very hard to do. But in a system where a small minority can veto anything, you only have to get to that small minority. You only have to buy a few. Hamilton points out that this makes a weak, divided republic a bargain for any foreign government looking to control it. You do not have to win the argument. You just have to purchase the handful of holdouts who can freeze the rest.

And then Hamilton makes a broader, darker observation about republics in general, one that clearly worried him. He says that republics have one particular weakness that monarchies do not. He says they "afford too easy an inlet to foreign corruption." His reasoning is uncomfortable but sharp. A hereditary king, he says, has so much personal stake in his own kingdom, his crown, his glory, his children's inheritance, that it is genuinely hard for a foreign power to offer him enough to make betrayal worth it. What could you give a king that is worth more than being king? But in a republic, power is held by ordinary citizens, lifted up from the crowd by election, holding office for just a few years. For such a person, the temptation is real. A foreign bribe might easily look bigger than the modest, temporary stake they have in the public good. The very thing that makes a republic admirable, that power is held by ordinary people rather than by an untouchable royal family, is also a soft spot that foreign money can press on.

And then he gives the example that makes it real, and it is a genuinely alarming one. He points to Sweden. For much of the eighteenth century, Sweden had been one of the freest countries in Europe, a monarchy where the real power sat not with the king but with the parliament. And what happened? Foreign powers, France on one side, Russia and Britain on the others, simply bought the factions in that parliament. Openly. Notoriously. The political parties of Sweden were, in Hamilton's blunt word, alternately purchased by foreign governments, so brazenly that it disgusted the whole nation. And that disgust, that exhaustion with a parliament that had been turned into a marketplace for foreign gold, is what let the king step in. In seventeen seventy-two, the Swedish king seized back near-absolute power in a single stroke. Hamilton describes it with real force: the most limited monarch in Europe, in a single day, without tumult, violence, or opposition, became one of the most absolute and uncontrolled. A free country had its freedom collapse, not because it was conquered from outside, but because its corrupt, purchasable legislature had so disgusted its own people that they welcomed a strongman as a relief. Foreign corruption did not just weaken Sweden. It handed the country to a king.

That is the fourth defect, the way the old system left America wide open to being bought.

And then Hamilton names one last crack, the one he says crowns all the others. The old government had no courts. No national judiciary at all. And he gives the reason in a line so clean it has echoed through American law ever since. He says, "Laws are a dead letter without courts to expound and define their true meaning and operation."

A dead letter. A law that no court can interpret or enforce is just ink on a page. And under the Articles, that is what national law largely was. There was no national court system to say what the law of the union actually meant. So when a treaty signed by the United States came into question, or when one state's law clashed with the agreements of the whole, who decided? Thirteen separate state court systems, each free to interpret things its own way, each leaning toward its own local interests. Hamilton's point is that you cannot have one country with thirteen different final answers to the same legal question. The treaties and laws of the whole union were left at the mercy of thirteen different sets of courts, any of which could twist or ignore them. You need, he says, one supreme tribunal, one court at the top whose word is final, so the law means the same thing everywhere. That idea, sitting here as a complaint about a missing piece, is the direct seed of the Supreme Court of the United States.

So now stack them up. Add these to last episode's three, and look at the whole pile. A government that cannot enforce its laws. Cannot protect its own states. Cannot fund itself fairly. Cannot manage trade, at home or abroad. Cannot raise an army without an absurd auction. Cannot make decisions, because a tiny minority can freeze everything. Lies wide open to foreign bribery. And has no courts to make its laws mean anything at all. That is not a government with a few problems. That is a government that fails at very nearly everything a government is for.

And this is the moment the paper turns. Because Hamilton now does something he has not done in all the papers leading up to this one. He stops listing symptoms and delivers the verdict.

He says the Articles are not a system you can repair. You might think, looking at this long list of defects, that the sensible thing would be to go in and fix them one by one. Amend the document. Patch the holes. Add the missing powers. And here you might fairly ask, as many people did at the time: why not? Why not just amend the Articles instead of throwing them out and starting over?

Hamilton's answer is unflinching. He says the thing is broken too deep for patching. He calls it a system "so radically vicious and unsound, as to admit not of amendment but by an entire change in its leading features and characters." Radically unsound. Meaning the rot is not in the details, it is in the foundation. You cannot renovate a house whose problem is the ground it stands on. You have to rebuild.

And that brings Hamilton to the deepest point in the entire paper, the thing that lies underneath every single defect we have talked about across both of these episodes. He asks: why is the old government so weak, so easily defied, so easily ignored by the states? And his answer is that the Articles were never really grounded in the right thing. They were never ratified by the people. The old union was just a deal among thirteen state legislatures, a treaty, an agreement between governments. And the trouble with an agreement between governments is that the governments who signed it can always claim the right to walk away from it. If your union is just a contract that thirteen state legislatures agreed to, then any state legislature can argue that it has the right to un-agree. The whole thing rests on the permission of the states, which means it can never really command them.

The fix, Hamilton says, is to build the new government on a completely different foundation. Not on the consent of thirteen state governments, but on the consent of the people themselves. And he says it in a sentence that is, quite simply, one of the most important lines in the whole Federalist Papers. He writes: "The fabric of American empire ought to rest on the solid basis of the consent of the people. The streams of national power ought to flow immediately from that pure, original fountain of all legitimate authority."

Let that land. The fabric of American empire, the whole structure, the whole country, ought to rest on the consent of the people. Not on a treaty between state governments. On the people. Power should flow directly up from the citizens themselves, that pure, original fountain, and not be filtered through, and held hostage by, the thirteen state legislatures.

This is the heart of everything. Remember the disease we have been chasing across both episodes, the one defect underneath all the others, a government that acts on states instead of on people. Here, in these final lines, Hamilton names the cure for it at the level of the foundation itself. A real national government cannot be a club of states. It has to belong directly to the people, draw its power directly from the people, and act directly on the people. That is the difference between a treaty and a country.

And here is the payoff that Hamilton's readers were about to see, and that you already know. When the Constitution was finished, it was deliberately not sent to the thirteen state legislatures for approval. It was sent out to special conventions, elected by the people of each state, on purpose, precisely so that the new government could say its authority came straight from the people and not from the state governments. And when the framers needed the very first words of that new Constitution, the words that would announce whose document it was and where its power came from, they did not write "We the states." They wrote, "We the People." Those three words, the most famous opening in all of American government, are the direct answer to the defect Hamilton is naming right here, in the last paragraph of Federalist Number Twenty-Two. He is, in a real sense, writing the argument that the Preamble would later put into three immortal words.

So look at the arc of these two episodes together. Hamilton walked us through the failures of the old government, eight defects across the two papers, from the inability to enforce a law to the inability to interpret one, from the chaos of trade to the poison of the minority veto. And then he showed us that all of it traces back to a single original mistake, building a nation on a treaty between governments instead of on the will of the people. The diagnosis is now complete. The patient, the Articles of Confederation, has been declared beyond saving. And the foundation for the replacement, the consent of the people, has been named.

What comes next is the construction. Hamilton and Madison are about to stop tearing down and start building. Federalist Number Twenty-Three opens a whole new stretch of the series, where Publius turns from everything that was wrong with the old government to laying out what the new one needs to be, beginning with the most basic and most controversial requirement of all: that a government responsible for the safety of a nation must be given powers equal to that enormous job, without timid limits that leave it unable to do the very thing it exists to do. The autopsy is over. The blueprint begins.

This has been The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Twenty-Two. Next time: Federalist Number Twenty-Three, still Alexander Hamilton, turning from the wreckage of the old government to the first principle of the new one, that a government meant to defend a nation must be built strong enough to actually do it.
