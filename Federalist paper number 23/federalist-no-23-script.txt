At the end of the last episode, the autopsy was finished. Across Federalist Number Twenty-One and Number Twenty-Two, Hamilton had laid the old American government out on the table and named everything wrong with it. Eight defects in all. A government that could not enforce its own laws. Could not protect its own states. Could not raise money fairly. Could not manage trade. Could not raise an army without a bidding war. Could not make a decision, because a tiny minority could freeze everything. Lay wide open to foreign bribery. And had no courts to make its laws mean anything at all. Then, in the very last breath of Number Twenty-Two, Hamilton stopped listing symptoms and delivered the verdict. This patient, the Articles of Confederation, could not be cured. It had to be replaced. And the replacement had to stand on a new foundation, not on a treaty between thirteen state governments, but on the people themselves. The consent of the people. The idea that would become the first three words of the Constitution. We the People.

That was the end of the demolition. And it is worth pausing to notice what the whole series has been up to this point. For twenty-two papers, the writer signing himself Publius has mostly been swinging a wrecking ball. Why a divided America would be prey to foreign powers. Why neighboring states drift into war with each other. Why the old confederation was too weak to enforce, to tax, to protect, to act. Twenty-two papers of what is broken, what is dangerous, what cannot hold.

Today that changes. Federalist Number Twenty-Three is where Publius puts the wrecking ball down and picks up a blueprint. From here forward, for a long stretch of the series, the question is no longer what was wrong with the old government. The question is what the new one has to be. The demolition is over. The construction begins.

And here is the thing about construction. Before an architect decides anything about the shape of the rooms, or where the windows go, or what the building will look like, there is a cruder, heavier question that comes first. How much weight does this structure have to carry? Get that wrong, and nothing else matters, because the whole thing comes down. Hamilton starts the building phase with exactly that question. Before we talk about how the new government is shaped, how its powers are divided, who checks whom, we have to answer the rawest question of all. How much power does this government need? And his answer, in this paper, is going to be more than a lot of his readers were ready to hear.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-Three. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. Published in the New York papers on the eighteenth of December, seventeen eighty-seven, just four days after the last one. And it is built around a single word that Hamilton loved, a word he comes back to again and again across these papers. The word is energetic. The title of the paper is, in plain terms, the necessity of a government as energetic as the one being proposed.

So let me start there, because the word matters. At this point you might be wondering what Hamilton even means by an energetic government. He does not mean a busy one, or a loud one, or a government always passing new laws. By energy, Hamilton means strength that can actually act. A government with the capacity to do the things a government exists to do, vigorously, decisively, on its own authority, instead of standing around hoping someone else will cooperate. The old confederation, remember, was the opposite of energetic. It could ask. It could request. It could mail a polite demand to thirteen state capitals and wait. What it could not do was act. That is the deadness Hamilton is reacting against. And the cure he is about to prescribe is a dose of energy that will alarm a lot of people. This is going to be the word that defines everything Hamilton wants. Hold onto it.

Now, instead of walking you through Hamilton's argument in the order he happens to lay it out, let me start with the idea at the very center of this paper, because once you have it, everything else clicks into place. It is an idea so simple that Hamilton himself says it can be obscured but never really made plainer by argument. Here it is. If you decide that something must get done, then you have to give the people responsible for doing it the tools to actually do it. You cannot will the goal and starve the means.

Hamilton puts it as a kind of axiom, a self-evident rule. The means, he says, ought to be proportioned to the end. The people from whom you expect a result ought to possess the means by which that result is reached. That sounds abstract, so let me make it concrete.

Imagine you hire a night watchman to protect your house. And imagine that when you hire him, you hand him a sealed list, written in advance, that spells out exactly which doors he is allowed to lock, which rooms he is allowed to enter, and which specific threats he is permitted to respond to. You wrote that list before you had any idea what was actually coming. Now picture a burglar climbing through a window you forgot to put on the list. Your watchman, by the rules you gave him, has to stand there and watch. He sees the danger. He is the man you hired precisely to stop it. And he is forbidden, by a piece of paper you wrote in advance, from doing the one thing you hired him for.

That is the trap Hamilton is warning about. And it leads him to the most striking claim in the entire paper, the line that made his opponents sit bolt upright. When it comes to the common defense, to protecting the nation from danger, Hamilton says the powers ought to exist without limitation. Without limitation. And he gives the reason in a single sentence that is the beating heart of this essay. Those powers cannot be limited, he says, because it is impossible to foresee or define the extent and variety of national emergencies, and the matching extent and variety of the means that might be needed to meet them.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the whole argument in one stroke. The circumstances that endanger a nation, Hamilton says, are infinite. You cannot make a list of them ahead of time. You do not know whether the next threat is an invasion, a rebellion, a blockade, a famine, a foreign fleet, a plot you have not imagined yet. And because you cannot predict the danger, you cannot pre-write the list of powers needed to meet it. So, he says, no constitutional shackles can wisely be imposed on the power that is charged with the nation's safety. No constitutional shackles. The power to defend the country has to be as large as the dangers to the country, and the dangers have no fixed size.

And here the modern parallel almost writes itself, because history has tested Hamilton's claim about as hard as a claim can be tested. Think about what the men arguing in seventeen eighty-seven could actually picture when they imagined a threat to the nation. They pictured muskets. They pictured a line of redcoats. They pictured warships made of wood, with sails, firing cannonballs. That was the edge of the possible. Not one of them, not the most imaginative person in that entire generation, could have pictured an airplane dropping bombs from the sky. A submarine beneath the ocean. A single weapon that could erase a city in a flash of light. An attack that crosses a border not by land or sea but through wires and screens, shutting down a power grid from the other side of the world. A virus stepping off an airplane and spreading through a country in weeks. Every one of those became a genuine danger to the United States. And every one of them has had to be met under the authority of a Constitution written by men who were thinking about wooden ships. The only reason that was even possible is that Hamilton, right here, refused to freeze the list of dangers. He said the power to defend the nation must stretch to fit whatever comes, precisely because nobody can know what is coming. Two hundred and forty years of unimaginable threats have proven him exactly right.

So that is the principle. Power to defend the nation cannot be capped in advance, because danger cannot be capped in advance.

Now, a fair question here, and it is the question that should be forming in your mind right now, is this. Where does Hamilton get the nerve to be so sure? It is a bold thing to tell a country that just fought a revolution against too much power that the new government needs power without limits. What makes him so confident he is right?

The answer is that Hamilton was not theorizing from a library. He had watched the alternative nearly kill the country, up close, with his own eyes. To understand this paper, you have to understand where Hamilton spent the war.

For most of the Revolution, Alexander Hamilton was not off in a legislature making speeches. He was at George Washington's side. From early in seventeen seventy-seven, he served as one of Washington's closest aides, the young officer who wrote the general's letters, sat in on his councils, and carried his orders. Which means that when the Continental Army nearly came apart, Hamilton was standing right there watching it happen. And here is the part that burned itself into him. The army that won American independence almost did not die from British bullets. It almost died from its own government's inability to feed it.

Picture the winter of seventeen seventy-seven into seventeen seventy-eight. Washington's army is encamped at Valley Forge, in Pennsylvania, about twenty miles from a Philadelphia that the British comfortably occupy. It is brutally cold. And the soldiers of the army are starving. Many of them are barefoot. Washington himself wrote that you could trace his army's marches by the blood their bare feet left on the frozen ground. They have no coats, no blankets, often no food for days at a stretch. Disease tears through the camp, and over that single winter an estimated seventeen hundred to two thousand of them die, not in battle, but of hunger, cold, and sickness, in their own camp, on their own soil.

And now ask the question that haunted Hamilton. Why? Was it because America had no food? No. There were full barns and fat livestock within a day's ride. Was it because the country had no cloth, no shoes, no money? No. The resources existed. So why was the army that was fighting for the country's survival freezing and starving in the snow? Part of it was an ordinary, miserable mess. The army's supply system had collapsed into incompetence, the winter roads were impassable, and the national paper money had sunk so low in value that farmers would not take it, preferring to sell their grain for hard coin, which often enough meant selling to the British over in Philadelphia. But underneath all of that was the deeper problem Hamilton kept pointing at his whole life. When the supply system broke down, there was no national authority strong enough to reach in and force it back together. The central government could not tax. It could not seize what it needed. It could not command. All it could do was send polite requests to thirteen separate state governments, and print paper money that was turning to nothing in people's hands. A government that can only ask, and never command, will watch its own soldiers freeze with full barns a day's ride away. The whole thing ran on please and thank you, and please and thank you does not keep men alive through a Pennsylvania winter.

That is the experience underneath this entire paper. When Hamilton says, in his careful, lawyerly way, that the experiment has demonstrated that the old expectation was, in his words, ill-founded and illusory, that dry phrase is sitting on top of a memory of frozen, barefoot men. He is not making a debating point. He is telling you what he saw.

And that memory drives him straight to the prescription, which is the same cure we met at the end of last episode, now stated as a hard rule about power. If you are serious, Hamilton says, about giving the union real strength and a real future, you have to stop doing the one thing the old government did. You have to, in his words, abandon the vain project of legislating upon the states in their collective capacities. Stop making laws aimed at state governments and hoping they obey. Instead, he says, we must extend the laws of the federal government to the individual citizens of America. Reach past the state capitals. Let the national government make law that touches the actual person, the individual citizen, directly.

This is the same disease and the same cure from Number Twenty-Two, wearing different clothes. Last episode, the deepest defect of the old order was that it acted on states instead of on people, which is why it was always weak, always begging, always ignored. The fix, we said, was to ground the whole government in the people. We the People. Here Hamilton takes that exact idea and turns it into a rule about how power has to work. A government that defends a nation cannot operate by leaning on thirteen middlemen. It has to reach the citizen directly. And while he is at it, Hamilton throws out the old machinery by name. The whole scheme of quotas and requisitions, he says, splitting the bill or the draft among the states and waiting, must be thrown out. He calls it a fallacious scheme, and brands it impracticable and unjust. Remember the auction for men from last episode, the states bidding against each other for soldiers because the national government could not raise an army directly? That. That entire approach, Hamilton says, into the bin. A real government raises its own troops, builds its own fleets, and collects its own revenue, the way every other functioning government on earth does.

Now, having felt the force of why power matters, you are ready for the part of the paper Hamilton actually opens with, and which I deliberately saved. Because right near the start of Number Twenty-Three, Hamilton hands his readers a map. He says the whole inquiry ahead of us, this entire next phase of the argument, naturally divides into three questions. And those three questions are going to organize a big chunk of everything that follows in the series, so they are worth holding in your head.

First question. What are the objects? Meaning, what are the actual jobs the federal government has to do? What is it for? Second question. What quantity of power does each of those jobs require? Once you know the job, how much authority does doing it actually take? And third question. Who does that power operate on? On the states, or on the individual citizen? Objects, quantity, persons. The jobs, the power each job needs, and who the power reaches.

Notice that Hamilton has, in a sense, already answered the third question in this very paper. Who does the power operate on? The citizen, directly, not the state. That is settled. And the objects, the jobs, he sketches quickly. There are, he says, a handful of great purposes the union exists to serve. First and foremost, the common defense, protecting the members from attack. Second, keeping the peace at home, against rebellion and convulsion from within, as much as against enemies from without. Third, regulating trade, both with foreign nations and among the states. And fourth, managing the country's whole relationship with the outside world, its diplomacy and its commerce abroad. Those are the objects. And in this paper, Hamilton zeroes in on the first and the heaviest of them. The common defense. Because if he can win the argument about power there, on the question of national survival, the rest follows easily.

But there is a fair objection here, and it is not a small one, and to Hamilton's great credit he does not pretend it away. You might be thinking it right now. Wait. Power without limitation? No constitutional shackles? Did these people not just fight an eight-year war against a government that had too much power? Is unlimited authority not the exact thing the Revolution was against?

That was not a fringe worry in seventeen eighty-seven. It was the central fear of a whole movement, the people we now call the Anti-Federalists, who looked at the proposed Constitution and saw, in language like Hamilton's, the seeds of a new tyranny. Power without limit. A national government that could raise armies and taxes at will and reach right past the states into every citizen's life. To people who had just risked everything to throw off a distant, overbearing power, that sounded less like security and more like the thing they had bled to escape. And they were not fools for thinking it. That fear is one of the most reasonable fears in the whole founding.

Hamilton's answer is not to soften the claim. It is to sharpen the logic. The powers, he insists, are not too large for the jobs. They are sized to the objects, no bigger. A power to defend the nation is only as large as the nation's dangers, and we did not choose those dangers. And then he springs a trap on his opponents that is genuinely clever. All right, he says. Suppose you are right. Suppose it is truly impossible to safely trust any government with the powers needed to defend a country this big and this varied. If you actually believe that, then be honest about where your own argument leads. It does not lead to a weak national government. It leads to no national government at all. It means we should give up on a single united country and break ourselves back into small, separate confederacies, each one little enough to be governed without such frightening powers. You cannot have it both ways. You cannot hand a government the job of keeping the whole nation alive and then refuse it the tools to do the job and call that caution. That is not caution. That is setting the thing up to fail and pretending you have been careful. In one of his cleaner lines, Hamilton says, let us not attempt to reconcile contradictions, but firmly embrace a rational alternative. Pick one. Either a union strong enough to defend itself, or no union. But not a union deliberately built too weak to survive.

And this is the moment to step back, because the argument Hamilton opens here does not close in seventeen eighty-seven. It is, in many ways, the argument that has never closed. Hand the defender of the nation whatever power the danger requires, because you cannot foresee the danger. That single idea is the headwater of two hundred and forty years of the hardest argument in American life. How much power does national security justify, and what does it cost in freedom? The standing army Hamilton wanted has become the most powerful military the world has ever seen. The logic of no shackles, of powers that stretch to meet any emergency, runs straight through every great fight over a president's war powers, every debate about surveillance and secrecy, everything that later critics would call the imperial presidency. Hamilton lit that fuse right here, in a quiet paragraph about means and ends.

So how did the founders eventually answer the Anti-Federalists' fear, if not by weakening the powers? This is the part worth understanding, because it is the resolution of the whole tension. They did not shrink the government's strength. Hamilton won that argument. What they did instead, a couple of years later, was add a different kind of protection. The Bill of Rights, the first ten amendments, spelling out things the government may not do to a person no matter how much power it has. And the whole machinery of checks and balances, the rival branches watching and blocking one another. In other words, the founders agreed with Hamilton that you should give the government real strength, and then they chained that strength with rights and rivalries, rather than by starving it in advance. Energy first, then limits on how the energy may be used. That is the bargain the Constitution finally struck. And it starts with Hamilton, in this paper, insisting on the energy.

Which brings us to the way Hamilton ends, and it is one of the most satisfying turns in the series so far, because he takes his opponents' single best argument and flips it completely upside down. The strongest card the other side held was the sheer size of the country. The United States, they said, is simply too big. Thirteen states strung along a vast coastline, stretching back over the mountains toward a continent. No single energetic government, they argued, could ever manage something that large. It is too much territory, too many people, too many competing interests. The bigness itself makes a strong national government impossible.

Hamilton looks at that argument and says, you have it exactly backwards. The bigness is not the reason to fear a strong government. The bigness is the strongest reason of all to build one. Because here is the truth that should be obvious, he says. A small, weak government might just barely hold a small country together. But, in his own words, the very difficulty itself, drawn from the extent of the country, is the strongest argument in favor of an energetic government; for any other can certainly never preserve the Union of so large an empire. Read that again in plain terms. The harder the thing is to hold together, the stronger the hands holding it have to be. A vast country is not an argument for a weak government. It is the single best argument for a strong one. If America is going to be big, then America had better be strong, because nothing weak could ever keep something that large from flying apart.

And that is where Number Twenty-Three lands. The construction phase has begun, and Hamilton has laid the cornerstone, and the cornerstone is power. A government meant to defend a nation must be given strength equal to that enormous job, with no shackles written in advance, because nobody can foresee what defending a nation will someday require. The means must match the end. The power must reach the citizen, not just the state. And the bigger and more important the job, the more power it takes, not less.

It is, by the way, the most aggressive case for central power that Hamilton makes in the entire series, and he makes it first, on purpose, on the ground of national survival, where it is hardest to argue against. Everything else he wants to build, he is building on top of this.

Next time, Hamilton takes this big principle and aims it at the single most frightening specific his readers could imagine. Because it is one thing to say a government needs power to defend the nation in the abstract. It is another thing entirely to say it out loud. The new government will be allowed to keep a standing army. Soldiers under arms, paid by the government, in peacetime, when no war is being fought. To Americans in seventeen eighty-seven, a standing army in a time of peace was very nearly the definition of tyranny. It was the instrument every would-be despot in history had used to crush a free people. And the Constitution does not ban it. Hamilton is about to explain why.
