For four papers now, Hamilton has been talking Americans down off their oldest fear. The fear of a standing army. And in the last one, Number Twenty-Seven, he made the most reassuring move of the whole sequence. He argued that a government which is good at its job, and which touches the lives of ordinary people directly and fairly, earns the kind of obedience that no army could ever manufacture. Force, he said, is not the tool of a strong government. It is the crutch of a failed one. Build the union well, run it through courts instead of camps, and the sword will barely be drawn at all.

It is a warm and hopeful note. And Hamilton is far too clear-eyed a man to end there.

Because there is an honest exception buried inside that hopeful picture, and today he turns around and faces it directly. Yes, a well-run government is obeyed out of habit most of the time. But most of the time is not all of the time. Once in a great while, in any country that has ever existed, something breaks. A rebellion. An insurrection. A violent uprising that no amount of good administration could have talked out of happening. And when that day comes, the gentle machinery of courts and clerks is not enough. The government has to be able to answer force with force.

That is the admission at the center of Federalist Number Twenty-Eight. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. It ran in the Independent Journal in the closing days of December, seventeen eighty-seven. And here is what makes this paper so interesting. Having conceded the scary thing, that the government will sometimes have to use force, Hamilton then turns the concession into the single most powerful argument for the union in the entire debate. He is going to show that the very thing the critics feared, a government strong enough to use force, is also, when it is built the American way, a government that can never use that force to enslave the people who created it. The paper admits the danger with one hand and dissolves it with the other.

Let me start where Hamilton starts, with the admission itself, because he does not hedge it. He says plainly that there may happen cases in which the national government may be necessitated to resort to force, and that this cannot be denied. Then he goes further, into a piece of hard wisdom about human societies that is worth hearing in his own words. Seditions and insurrections, he writes, are, unhappily, maladies as inseparable from the body politic as tumors and eruptions from the natural body.

Sit with that image, because it tells you exactly how Hamilton sees the world. A political community is like a human body. And a human body, no matter how healthy, will now and then develop something that has to be cut out or drained or burned away. It is not a sign that the body is dying. It is simply what living bodies do. In the same way, Hamilton is saying, every society that has ever existed, no matter how free, no matter how well governed, will occasionally throw up a violent disorder that has to be dealt with. To design a government on the assumption that this will never happen is not idealism. It is fantasy.

And Hamilton has a name for that fantasy. Some of the Constitution's critics had insisted that a proper republic should govern purely by what they called the simple force of law, that a free government should never need anything sharper than a court order and a sense of shared obligation. Hamilton thought that idea was beautiful and completely useless. Governing at all times by the simple force of law, he writes, has no place but in the reveries of those political doctors whose sagacity disdains the admonitions of experimental instruction. In plain English, that dream lives only in the daydreams of theorists so pleased with their own cleverness that they refuse to learn anything from actual experience. Hamilton is not interested in how government works in a seminar. He is interested in how it works when a mob is marching on an arsenal.

And notice that this is not some quirk of the seventeen eighties that we have since outgrown. Every stable, free country in the world today still keeps exactly this power somewhere in reserve. They all have laws for emergencies, for insurrections, for the rare moment when ordinary policing is not enough and organized force has to answer organized violence. No functioning democracy has ever managed to delete that power, because the need for it is not a flaw in any one constitution. It is a fact about human beings living together. Hamilton is simply refusing to pretend otherwise. He would rather look the ugly necessity in the face and build the safeguards around it than wish it away and be caught defenseless on the day it arrives.

At this point you might be thinking that Hamilton has just handed the Anti-Federalists their whole case. They warned that this new government would reach for force, and here is Hamilton openly agreeing that it will. Has he not just conceded the argument? He has conceded a fact. He has not conceded the argument. And the way he pulls those two apart is the hinge of the paper.

Here is the move. Hamilton asks a simple question that the critics had somehow skipped. This need to use force, once in a while, against a violent uprising. Is that a special defect of the new national government? Or is it just a fact of governing anything at all? Because if it is a fact of governing anything at all, then it is no more an argument against the union than it is an argument against the states, or against any government the critics might prefer instead.

And he shows that it is exactly that. A fact of governing anything at all. He walks it up a ladder of size. If the trouble is small, he says, a slight commotion in a small part of a state, then the ordinary militia of the rest of that state is plenty to handle it, and you can presume the citizens will turn out to do their duty. But if the trouble is bigger, if a rebellion swallows a whole state or a large part of one, then honest people have to admit that the militia alone might not be enough, and a more organized, more permanent kind of force becomes unavoidable.

Then Hamilton does something shrewd. He does not leave that as a hypothetical. He points at things his readers had just lived through. Massachusetts, he reminds them, had recently found it necessary to raise actual troops to put down the disorders inside its own borders. That is Shays and his rebellion of desperate farmers, the uprising we walked through back in Numbers Six and Seven, the one that terrified the whole political class less than a year before Hamilton sat down to write. Pennsylvania, he adds, raised a force too, and not even in response to an actual revolt, but from the mere apprehension that one might be coming. And then he turns to his own state. Suppose New York had wanted to reassert its authority over the settlers in the disputed lands of Vermont, the quarrel we met in Number Seven. Could New York have managed that with a weekend militia? Of course not. It would have needed a real and lasting military force to do it.

Do you see what he has done? Every single example of a government needing force beyond a casual militia is an example of a state doing it. The states already live with this necessity. They have raised troops against their own citizens, in living memory, more than once. So the need for organized force in a genuine emergency is not some sinister novelty the Constitution smuggles in. It is a plain feature of government as such, one the states themselves cannot escape.

A fair question here is why, if that is true, the critics aimed the complaint only at the national government and never at their own beloved states. And Hamilton's answer is close to a taunt. He points out that the men making this objection claimed to love the Union in the abstract while proposing to replace it with a looser arrangement, separate confederacies, a handful of smaller unions instead of one. And every worry they raised about force under the national government, he says, applies with tenfold weight to the very plan they were pushing instead. Break America into small pieces, and you do not get less turmoil. You get more. Small jealous republics packed close together are the most turbulent, coup-prone, war-torn governments in history. Hamilton had spent whole papers on this. He lets one phrase carry it here. Who, he asks, would not prefer the occasional emergency under a large steady union to the unceasing agitations and frequent revolutions which are the continual scourges of petty republics? The objection does not just miss the union. It boomerangs, and it hits the critics harder than the thing they were aiming at.

So Hamilton has cleared the ground. He has admitted that force will sometimes be needed, and he has shown that this admission is no more an objection to the Constitution than it is to any other way of organizing a country. Now he can get to the real question, the one that actually keeps people awake. It is not, will this government ever use force? Every government will. The real question is, what happens if the government turns that force against the very people it is supposed to serve? What is the safeguard when the guardian becomes the threat?

Hamilton gives two answers, and they build on each other. The first is the ordinary one, the one that operates every single day. The whole power of the proposed government, he writes, is to be in the hands of the representatives of the people. And that, he says, is the essential, and, after all, only efficacious security for the rights and privileges of the people, which is attainable in civil society. Read that carefully, because it is doing quiet work. Hamilton is reminding his readers that the army the critics were so afraid of does not belong to some separate ruling caste. It is commanded, funded, and controlled by a Congress that the people themselves elect, and can throw out. The force of the government is not a thing standing over the people. It is a thing held in trust by the people's own hired agents. In the normal course of life, that is the whole safeguard. You control the men who control the sword.

But Hamilton knows that is not the case anyone actually worries about. The nightmare is not the normal course of life. The nightmare is the day the trust is broken. What happens if the representatives of the people betray the people? If the men elected to hold that power decide to keep it, to turn the government into an instrument against the citizens who built it? Hamilton does not wave this away. He walks straight into it. And what he finds there is the deepest idea in the paper.

If the representatives betray their constituents, he says, then there is no resource left but in the exertion of that original right of self-defense which is paramount to all positive forms of government. That is a heavy phrase, so let me unpack it. The original right of self-defense is the right that comes before any constitution, before any law, before any government at all. It is the bedrock right of a people to resist a power that has turned tyrannical. Every government, in Hamilton's view, sits on top of that right, and none can erase it. When every legal channel has been captured and corrupted, the people keep the ancient right to defend themselves against their own rulers. So far, that is a principle any friend of liberty would nod along to. But a principle is not a plan. And here Hamilton asks the practical question almost nobody bothers to ask. When that terrible day comes, can the people actually win?

And this is where the paper opens up into its most important passage, because Hamilton answers by comparing two very different kinds of country. Picture the first one. Picture a single, consolidated state, one government over the whole territory and nothing underneath it, no smaller governments inside, just the central power on top and scattered individuals below. Now suppose the rulers of that state become usurpers. What can the people do? Hamilton's picture is bleak, and deliberately so. The citizens, he writes, must rush tumultuously to arms, without concert, without system, without resource; except in their courage and despair. Feel how those words land. There is no plan. There is no organization. There is no treasury to draw on, no structure to rally around, no one authorized to lead. Just frightened people grabbing whatever weapons they can find and running into the street, hoping numbers and desperation will be enough. And usually they are not enough. Because the usurpers, Hamilton says, clothed with the forms of legal authority, can too often crush the opposition in embryo. The tyrant still holds the machinery of the state. He looks legitimate. He learns of the plot before it matures, and he strikes it down while it is still a rumor and a huddle of angry men. In a small, undivided country, Hamilton is saying, a rising against tyranny is pitifully easy to smother. There is nothing standing between the lone citizen and the whole weight of the government.

Now hold that grim image in your mind, and let Hamilton show you the other kind of country. The American kind. Because the whole point of what he is about to say is that an American is never that lone citizen in the street. An American already lives, every day, under a second complete government. His state. And the state is not a mood or a sentiment. It is a real, standing apparatus of power. It has a legislature that meets and deliberates. It has a treasury and the ability to tax. It has its own courts, its own officers, its own militia, its own established channels of communication, and above all it already holds the confidence and the loyalty of its people. Which means that on the day a national government reaches for tyranny, the people of America do not have to improvise resistance from nothing. They have a government of their own, already built, already trusted, ready to lead.

Think about what that changes. In Hamilton's own words, the state legislatures can discover the danger at a distance. They are watching. They have better information than scattered private citizens ever could, and they have every motive to be jealous of a grasping national power. So they see the usurpation coming while it is still forming. And once they see it, they do not have to run tumultuously to arms. They can, Hamilton says, at once adopt a regular plan of opposition, and combine all the resources of the community, and communicate with each other across the different states, and unite their common forces. Read that against the earlier picture and the contrast is total. Where the citizen of the consolidated state had no concert, no system, no resource, the American has thirteen organized governments able to coordinate, to fund, to arm, and to lead a continent-wide resistance under leaders the people already trust. The state governments turn a leaderless mob into an organized nation in arms. That, Hamilton says, is why the state governments stand, in every possible contingency, as a complete security against any invasion of the people's liberty by the national power. They are the standing insurance policy against federal tyranny.

And there is a subtle reason the state governments make such good sentinels, one Hamilton is careful to point out. A would-be tyrant rarely announces himself. He advances behind pretenses, behind reassuring words and plausible cover stories, and a large, scattered public is exactly the audience those cover stories are designed to fool. You might be wondering, then, what good it does to have the people on your side if the people can be misled. The answer is that the states are not a scattered public. They are select bodies of experienced men whose full-time business is to watch power and to distrust it. That is why, Hamilton says, a project of usurpation cannot be dressed up in pretenses cleverly enough to slip past a watchful legislature the way it can slip past the people at large. A cover story that might fool millions of busy private citizens has a much harder time fooling a body that is paid to pay attention. So the state governments do not just supply the muscle of resistance. They supply the early warning, the eyes that catch the danger while it is still small enough to stop.

And now Hamilton reaches the idea that makes the whole architecture click, the one that I think is the most quietly brilliant sentence in the paper. It works in both directions. It is not only that the states guard the people against the national government. The national government also guards the people against the states. Power, he writes, being almost always the rival of power, the general government will at all times stand ready to check the usurpations of the state governments, and these will have the same disposition towards the general government. Two levels of government, each jealous of the other, each watching the other, each ready to sound the alarm the moment the other overreaches. And where does that leave the people? In the best possible place. Listen to how Hamilton puts it. The people, by throwing themselves into either scale, will infallibly make it preponderate.

Picture an old balance scale, the kind with two pans. On one side, the national government. On the other, the states. And the people are the weight in the hand, free to drop into whichever pan they choose. If the national government grows dangerous, the people throw their weight to the states, and the states, backed by the people, prevail. If a state government grows dangerous, the people throw their weight to the national government, and it prevails. Neither side can ever win a lasting victory over the people, because the people are never trapped inside one pan. They are the deciding weight, and they can always shift. That is why Hamilton can say something that would sound like empty flattery from a lesser writer but is here a hard structural fact. In a confederacy like this one, the people, without exaggeration, may be said to be entirely the masters of their own fate. Not because someone promised them liberty on parchment. Because the machine is built so that no single part of it can ever close its grip on them without another part, and the people themselves, prying it open.

So why does that matter so much? Because it completely rearranges the fear of a standing army. The critics had pictured a federal army as a hammer poised over a helpless people. Hamilton is showing that the people are not helpless and never were. And he closes the argument by looking at the raw arithmetic of it, which is almost comforting in its bluntness. An army large enough to actually enslave a whole continent, he points out, is an army that, for a very long time, simply cannot be built or paid for. A government can only field the force its resources allow. And here is the beautiful part. As the country grows rich enough to afford a bigger army, it is also growing more populous, which means the natural strength of the people is growing at least as fast as the artificial strength of the government. The people never fall behind. And even if some future federal army did march, Hamilton says, and even if it managed to crush the resistance of one state, the distant states would still have it in their power to make head with fresh forces. Every gain the army made in one place it would have to abandon to fight somewhere else, and the moment it left, the resistance behind it would spring back to life. You cannot occupy a continent that is organized against you. There are not enough soldiers in the world.

Now, there is one condition Hamilton attaches to all of this, and it is easy to race past, but it is the whole game. Every bit of this protection, he says, holds provided the citizens understand their rights and are disposed to defend them. That little clause is the fine print on the entire safeguard. The two-scale machine, the watchful state governments, the people as masters of their own fate, none of it runs on autopilot. It runs on a citizenry that knows what its rights are and is willing to stand up for them. Give it that, and no army the government could ever afford is a real threat. Take that away, let the people grow ignorant of their rights or too comfortable to defend them, and the finest structure ever drawn on paper will not save them. Hamilton is handing his readers an immense reassurance and, in the same breath, quietly handing them the bill. The safety is real, but you have to stay awake to keep it.

And you can watch this whole design running, right now, in American life, though we almost never call it by Hamilton's name. This is what people are looking at when they see a state government take the federal government to court, or the federal courts strike down a state law, or a governor and a president square off over who has the authority to do what. That endless, noisy tug-of-war between the states and Washington is not a malfunction. It is the two scales, exactly as Hamilton drew them, still swinging. Each level of government still jealous of the other, still checking the other, still dragging the other into court instead of into the street. Most Americans could not name the paper this came from, but they live inside its mechanism every single day. The double government Hamilton was defending in seventeen eighty-seven is not a museum piece. It is the working architecture of the country, and it is still doing the exact job he built it to do, keeping any single center of power from ever closing its hand completely.

So step back and look at what these five papers, Numbers Twenty-Four through Twenty-Eight, have accomplished together, because they really are one sustained argument about force and freedom. The first three took the fear of the army apart from the inside. They showed that the states cannot safely hold the nation's soldiers alone, that a paper ban on peacetime armies is either empty or dangerous, and that the true leash on any army is the recurring, public, on-the-record vote that Number Twenty-Six described. Then Number Twenty-Seven pulled back and made the reassuring point underneath it all, that a well-run union woven into people's daily lives will rarely need to draw the sword in the first place. And now Number Twenty-Eight faces the honest exception, the rare day when force truly is necessary, and instead of flinching from it, Hamilton uses it to reveal the deepest safeguard of the whole system.

And that safeguard, in the end, is not a clause. It is not a sentence in the Constitution promising that the government will behave. Hamilton had nothing but contempt for promises like that. The real safeguard is structural, and it is human. It is two governments built to check each other, resting on one people who can always tip the balance, in a country too large and too well organized for any army it could afford to conquer. The final guard against tyranny is not words on parchment. It is the people themselves, awake to their rights, holding a weight they can drop into either scale. That is why Hamilton can end the paper with such startling calm. The fear that this government will one day enslave its own citizens, he says, may be considered as a disease, for which there can be found no cure in the resources of argument and reasoning. He means it almost gently. Some fears cannot be argued away, because they were never built on the real balance of forces in the first place. Show a frightened man the machine, show him where he himself stands inside it, and if he still trembles, no further reasoning will help him. The strength was always in his own hands.

Next time, Hamilton stays with force and freedom, but he zooms all the way in on one piece of the picture he has been leaning on this whole time. The militia. The armed citizenry itself. In Federalist Number Twenty-Nine, still Alexander Hamilton, the question becomes who trains and organizes those citizen-soldiers, how a militia is supposed to work without becoming either useless or dangerous, and why the ordinary people under arms are, in Hamilton's mind, both the union's first line of defense and one of liberty's surest guardians.
