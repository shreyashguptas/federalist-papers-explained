At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had laid the cornerstone of the whole new government, and the cornerstone was power. Across Federalist Number Twenty-Three, he made the case for what he kept calling an energetic government — one strong enough to actually act, not just politely ask. And his central rule was almost brutally simple. If you hand a government a job, you have to hand it the tools to do that job. The means must match the end. You cannot will the goal and then starve the means.

And when the job is defending the nation, Hamilton said, the power cannot be shackled in advance. Because nobody can predict what defending a nation will someday require. You cannot make a complete list of every danger the future holds, so you cannot make a complete list of every power that meeting those dangers might take. Leave the list open, he argued, because the world will not check with you first before inventing a new threat.

That was a bold thing to say to a country that had just spent eight years fighting a war against a government it considered far too powerful. And Hamilton knew it was bold. He also knew that the moment he said the new government would be allowed to keep an army, he would be pressing directly on the single rawest nerve in all of American political life.

Today he presses on it on purpose.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-Four. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. Written for the Independent Journal in the closing weeks of seventeen eighty-seven. And it takes up the one power that Americans of that generation feared more than almost any other. A standing army. Soldiers kept under arms, paid by the government, in a time of peace, when no war is actually being fought.

Before we get anywhere near Hamilton's argument, you have to feel the fear he was standing against. Because it was not a small fear, and it was not a silly one. At this point you might be wondering how a single word — standing — could carry that much dread. So let me tell you where the dread came from, because it has a long history, and Hamilton's readers carried all of it in their bones.

Start in England, a little more than a century before this paper. In the sixteen forties, England fell into civil war, and Parliament built a new kind of force to win it — the New Model Army, a professional, permanent, full-time fighting force. That army won the war. And then that army did not go home. It became a power in its own right. It was the muscle behind the execution of a king, Charles the First. And out of its ranks rose Oliver Cromwell, who ended up ruling England as a military strongman, at one point carving the country into districts, each one run by an army general. For the English, that was the first hard lesson. An army that outlives the war it was raised to fight can swallow the government that created it.

Then it happened again, in a different key. In the sixteen eighties, King James the Second kept a standing army in peacetime and used it to overawe his own people, and he packed its officer corps with fellow Catholics, in open defiance of the law, men who owed their place to him. That was one of the grievances that drove the English to push him off the throne entirely in sixteen eighty-eight. And when the dust settled, the English wrote a Bill of Rights, in sixteen eighty-nine, that said something very specific. Raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace, unless Parliament said so, was against the law. Notice the shape of that fix. They did not say no army ever. They said not without the consent of the people's representatives. Hold onto that idea, because it comes back.

Now bring it across the ocean, and much closer to home. In seventeen seventy, British soldiers — a standing army, stationed in Boston in a time of peace — fired into a crowd of colonists and killed five men. We call it the Boston Massacre. Those troops had been sent to a peaceful city to keep the colonists in line, and to the people of Boston they were living proof of the old nightmare. Soldiers in your streets, answering to a distant government, not to you. When the colonies finally broke with Britain and wrote the Declaration of Independence, one of the specific charges they leveled against King George the Third was exactly this. He has kept among us, in times of peace, standing armies without the consent of our legislatures.

So when the men of seventeen eighty-seven read a new Constitution that let the government raise and keep an army, this whole history came roaring up in them. Cromwell. James the Second. The redcoats in Boston. To a great many Americans, a standing army in peacetime was not one risk among many. It was very nearly the definition of tyranny itself. It was the tool that every would-be despot in history had reached for to crush a free people.

That is the wall of fear Hamilton walks up to in Federalist Number Twenty-Four. And here is the first thing worth noticing about how he handles it. He does not pretend the fear is imaginary. He takes the objection completely seriously as the thing standing between him and ratification. But he also thinks the specific complaint being made against the Constitution is, to put it bluntly, not honest. And a good chunk of this paper is Hamilton trying to prove that.

So let me set the stage the way he does. Hamilton says that of all the attacks on the military powers in the new plan, he has really only run into one specific objection. Just one. It is the claim that the Constitution failed to guard against standing armies in time of peace. There is no clause that flatly forbids them, and to the critics, that silence is damning.

Hamilton's response comes in two moves. First, he wants to show that the objection is close to a fraud — that the people making it are counting on you not to check the facts. And second, even setting all of that aside, he wants to show that a flat ban would have been a genuine mistake anyway. Let us take those in turn, because the first one is where Hamilton does something clever and almost novelistic.

To make his case that the objection is dishonest, Hamilton invents a character. He asks you to picture a stranger to American politics — a foreigner who knows nothing about the Constitution yet, who arrives and simply starts reading the newspapers. And the newspapers, at that moment, are full of furious warnings about standing armies. So this stranger, reading all that alarm, would naturally conclude one of two things. Either the new Constitution must positively command that standing armies be kept up in peacetime. Or it must hand the President — one man — the entire power to raise troops whenever he likes, with no one to check him. Why else, the stranger thinks, would everyone be this frightened?

Then, Hamilton says, imagine the stranger actually sits down and reads the plan itself. And he is astonished. Because neither of those things is true. The power to raise armies is not given to the President at all. It is given to the legislature — to Congress. And not some remote, unaccountable Congress, but a popular body, made up of representatives that the people themselves elect, again and again, on a short clock. The soldiers of this new republic would exist only because the people's own elected representatives voted to create and pay for them.

And then the stranger finds something he was not expecting at all. Not a green light for armies, but an extra brake. There is a clause — Hamilton is pointing at what becomes Article One of the Constitution — that forbids the appropriation of money for the support of an army for any longer period than two years. Say that plainly. Congress cannot fund the army once and walk away. Every two years at most, the money runs out, and the people's representatives have to vote, out in the open, to fund it all over again, or the army simply withers for lack of pay. A standing army in this system is a thing that must be re-approved, on the record, by elected officials, on a two-year heartbeat, forever. There is no autopilot. There is no permanent, self-sustaining military that runs itself once it is switched on. Every couple of years, somebody accountable to the voters has to stand up and say yes, again.

That, Hamilton points out, is a real safeguard. It is, in fact, more protection than most Americans had ever had before. And this is where his imaginary stranger keeps digging, and the case for dishonesty starts to build.

Because the stranger reasons like this. All right — the new plan does not ban armies, but it does add this money brake. Still, people are furious. So surely, he thinks, the state constitutions that Americans already live under must contain strict, ironclad prohibitions on standing armies. That must be the standard these critics are measuring against. That must be why the new plan feels like such a betrayal to them.

So the stranger goes and reads the state constitutions, all thirteen. And Hamilton says he would be amazed by what he finds. Out of thirteen states, only two even mention a ban on peacetime armies at all. And even those two — Pennsylvania and North Carolina — do not really forbid it. Their words, quoted in Hamilton's own footnote, run like this. As standing armies in time of peace are dangerous to liberty, they ought not to be kept up. Hamilton's dry comment on that is worth hearing. This, he says, is in truth rather a caution than a prohibition. Ought not is not the same as shall not. It is advice, not a law. And the other eleven states? They either say nothing about the subject at all, or they openly admit that their legislatures have the right to raise armies if they choose. So the strict, universal American standard the critics keep implying exists — it simply does not exist.

The stranger has one hope left. Fine, he thinks. Maybe the states are loose about it, but surely the Articles of Confederation — the national government Americans are living under right now, the one this new Constitution would replace — surely that document banned peacetime armies. And here Hamilton delivers the punchline, and it is a genuine reversal. The stranger reads the Articles, and his surprise curdles into something like indignation. Because the Articles of Confederation restrained the states on this point — but placed no restraint at all on the national government. None. Under the government Americans already had, Congress could keep standing armies with zero written limit. The brand-new Constitution these people are calling a doorway to tyranny actually adds a limit — that two-year money rule — that the current government completely lacks.

So step back and see what Hamilton has done. He has taken his critics' loudest claim — that the Constitution recklessly opens the door to military tyranny — and shown that the new plan is more careful about standing armies than the state constitutions, and more careful than the Articles of Confederation that came before it. The critics are attacking the plan for lacking a safeguard that hardly exists anywhere else, while ignoring the brand-new safeguard the plan actually invented.

That is why Hamilton gets sharp here, sharper than almost anywhere else in these papers. He says the whole outcry starts to look like nothing more than an experiment upon the public credulity — a test of how much a frightened public will swallow without checking. And he offers his readers a choice about what is really going on. Either the critics are being deliberately dishonest, or they are so carried away by their own zeal that they have stopped being fair. And then he lands the charge in a line that could be written about a hundred political fights since. A campaign like this, he says, has too much the appearance of an intention to mislead the people by alarming their passions, rather than to convince them by arguments addressed to their understandings.

Sit with that sentence, because it is timeless. There are two ways to move a crowd. You can reason with people — give them arguments aimed at their understanding, trust them to think it through. Or you can frighten them — reach past their reason straight to their fear, and let the fear do your work for you. Hamilton is accusing the anti-army campaigners of choosing the second road. Of trying to win not by convincing anyone, but by scaring everyone. And whatever you think of that particular fight in seventeen eighty-seven, the two roads he names are still the two roads. We watch politics travel both of them every single day.

Now, Hamilton could have stopped right there, having more or less accused the opposition of running a scare campaign. But he does not, and this is what makes the paper more than a debating trick. Because a fair question here is obvious. Okay, Hamilton — maybe no one else banned peacetime armies either. But that just tells us everyone was equally careless. It does not tell us a ban would have been wrong. Maybe all of them should have banned it.

So Hamilton takes off the lawyer's hat and makes the real-world case. And his thesis is blunt. He says that restraints on the legislature's power over peacetime military establishments would be improper to impose — and that, even if you imposed them, the necessities of society would make them unlikely to be observed. Read that second half again, because it is the shrewder half. Hamilton is saying that a flat ban on peacetime troops would not just be unwise. It would be a lie you tell yourself. Because the day real danger arrives, the country will raise troops anyway, ban or no ban, and all you will have accomplished is teaching everyone that the Constitution is a document you break when things get serious. A noble rule that everyone quietly ignores is worse than an honest rule you actually keep. Better to admit the need out loud and govern it, than to forbid it on paper and wink at it in practice.

But why is the need real at all? This is where Hamilton stops arguing about documents and asks his readers to look out the window at the actual map of America in seventeen eighty-seven. And this is the part of the paper worth slowing down for, because the picture he paints is not the safe, ocean-shielded America we tend to imagine.

Here is where the young United States actually sat. To the north, stretching down behind the states and off into the interior, was British territory — Canada, and a string of frontier forts the British were still garrisoning, some of them on land that was supposed to be American now. To the south and to the west sat Spain — Florida, the vast Louisiana country, the city of New Orleans, and with it control of the mouth of the Mississippi River, the drain-pipe for everything the western settlers grew. And out in the Caribbean, the rich sugar islands of the West Indies belonged to those same two powers. So the picture is not an America floating safely alone. It is an America boxed in, north and south and west, by the two greatest naval empires on earth.

And Hamilton makes a point here that is easy to skip past but is genuinely ahead of its time. People comfort themselves, he says, with that wide Atlantic Ocean — three thousand miles of water between America and Europe. But look, he says, at what is happening to distance itself. The improvements in the art of navigation have, in his words, rendered distant nations, in a great measure, neighbors. Ships were getting faster and better. The ocean that felt like a moat was shrinking, in practical terms, every decade. A power an ocean away was no longer really an ocean away. If you have ever heard someone say that modern technology has made the whole world smaller, that jet travel or the internet turned faraway countries into neighbors — Hamilton is making that exact argument, in seventeen eighty-seven, about the sailing ship. Distance is not the fixed shield people think it is. Technology keeps eating it.

He adds one more worry to the pile, and it is a subtle one about not trusting your luck. For a long time, France and Spain had been bound together by what was called a family compact — an alliance between the two royal houses, who happened to be cousins, both branches of the Bourbon family. Americans had leaned on that. France was our Revolutionary ally, and as long as France and Spain were family, Spain could be partly kept in check through France. But Hamilton warns that this family feeling is fading with each generation, as the blood ties grow more distant. And politicians, he says, have always had good reason to treat ties of blood as feeble and precarious links of political connection. In plain terms: do not bet your national security on two royal families staying fond of each other. Kinship is a weak glue between countries. It dries out. Base your safety on your own strength, not on someone else's family loyalty holding up.

So put the map together. Britain to the north, Spain to the south and west, both of them great sea powers, the protective ocean quietly shrinking, and the diplomatic arrangement Americans had been relying on slowly coming apart. That, Hamilton says, is why we cannot be too confident that we are safely out of reach of danger. And it leads him straight to the concrete need. On that long western frontier, he argues, the country will simply have to keep small garrisons — little permanent posts of soldiers — both before and after any war, as an ordinary standing fact of life.

Now here is where somebody was sure to raise a hand, and Hamilton knows it. You might be asking the obvious question. Why does that have to be a professional standing army at all? America already has the militia — ordinary citizens, farmers and tradesmen, who keep a musket at home and turn out to fight when they are needed. That was the beloved American ideal. Not a caste of paid soldiers, but the armed citizen who defends his own country and then goes back to his plow. Why not just use them to man the frontier forts, and avoid the whole dangerous business of a standing army entirely?

Hamilton's answer is one of the most down-to-earth passages in the paper, and it is really an argument about human beings, not about strategy. Using the militia for this, he says, is impracticable — and even if you could pull it off, it would be pernicious. Picture what it would actually mean. You take a farmer away from his farm, away from his family and his fields and his livelihood, and you send him off to sit in a lonely fort on the far frontier, month after month, in a time of peace, when there is no war and no glory and nothing to do but stand watch against boredom. He will not stand for it for long. Nobody would. It is the most disagreeable duty imaginable, and it falls in peacetime, when the sense of emergency that makes men volunteer is completely absent. And if you try to solve that by rotating fresh farmers through constantly, so no one man is stuck too long, you have created a different disaster. The endless shuffling is ruinously expensive, and every rotation yanks another working person out of his ordinary productive life. Hamilton's verdict is that it would be as burdensome to the public as it is ruinous to the private citizen. It simply does not work.

So the frontier forts, he concludes, have to be manned by permanent troops in the pay of the government. Full-time soldiers whose actual job is to be there. And then Hamilton does the honest thing, the thing that makes you trust him even when you are nervous about where he is going. He does not hide the ball. He says plainly that a permanent paid corps like that amounts to a standing army in time of peace. His exact words: it amounts to a standing army in time of peace, a small one, indeed, but not the less real for being small. He is not going to pretend it is something cuddlier. Yes, it is the very thing you are afraid of. It is just small, and it is necessary, and pretending otherwise helps no one.

He then piles on two more concrete needs, quickly. First, certain frontier posts are not just forts — they are keys. Whoever holds them commands huge stretches of surrounding territory and controls the trade with the Native nations of the interior. To leave posts like that empty, sitting there to be grabbed at any instant by Britain or Spain, would mean throwing away every ordinary rule of prudence and good sense a nation is supposed to live by. And second, there is the sea. If America ever wants to be a trading nation, or even just to be safe along its own Atlantic coast, Hamilton says it needs to build itself a navy, and the sooner the better. And a navy is not just ships. It means dockyards to build them, arsenals to arm them, forts to protect all of that — and, at least until the fleet is strong enough to guard itself, soldiers stationed to defend the shipyards from a surprise raid. Build a navy, and you have quietly signed up for permanent military installations on your own soil. There is no version of a strong, commercial, secure America that has zero full-time soldiers in it. Hamilton is asking his readers to look that fact in the eye.

So that is the whole shape of Federalist Number Twenty-Four. On one side, Hamilton argues the anti-army objection is close to dishonest, because the new plan is actually more careful about standing armies than anything that came before it. On the other, he argues that a flat ban would be foolish anyway, because the real map of America, with its shrinking ocean and its powerful neighbors, means the country genuinely needs frontier troops and a navy — and no honest person who looks at that map can pretend otherwise.

Now let me pull back, because there is a real question hanging over all of this, and it is worth asking out loud. Was Hamilton simply explaining away a genuine danger? The fear of standing armies was not paranoia. Armies really had toppled governments and crushed freedoms, over and over, throughout history. So how did the American system actually keep the thing Hamilton wanted from becoming the thing Americans feared?

The answer is the piece of this paper that turned out to matter most, and it is that little two-year money clause. Because notice what the founders did not do. They did not ban the army — Hamilton won that argument. Instead, they put the army on a leash held by the people's elected representatives, and they made that leash short. The soldiers exist only because Congress votes to create them. They get paid only because Congress votes the money. And that money vote expires, forcing Congress to publicly choose, over and over, to keep them. The army can never quietly detach itself from the voters and float free, because its own paycheck depends, every two years, on people who have to face election.

That is the same fix the English reached for back in sixteen eighty-nine — no standing army without the consent of the people's representatives. And it connects directly to something we teased at the end of the last episode. Remember, the founders answered the fear of too much power not by starving the government, but by chaining the power once it existed — with a Bill of Rights spelling out what the government may never do, and with rival branches watching each other. The two-year army rule is a perfect example of that whole philosophy in miniature. Give the government the strength it truly needs. Then wrap that strength in democratic control so tight that it can never turn against the people who granted it.

And this is not a museum piece. It is the living bones of how the American military still works. To this day, the armed forces answer to elected civilians, not the other way around. To this day, Congress has to vote on the military on a regular clock — you may have heard of the defense bills lawmakers fight over every year, the ones that set what the armed forces are allowed to do and what money they are allowed to spend. That yearly ritual, the recurring public vote that keeps the armed forces going, is the direct descendant of the clause Hamilton is defending in this paper. Civilian control of the military, funded on a short and public leash, is one of the quiet reasons the United States has kept a very large standing army for a very long time without that army ever seizing the government. Hamilton would say: that is exactly the design. Not no army. An army the voters can starve at will.

Now, before we close, there is one part of this paper that a modern listener should not glide past, and I do not want to. When Hamilton describes the dangers on the western frontier, he refers to the Native nations there as savage tribes, and he calls them our natural enemies, and the natural allies of Britain and Spain against us. That language, and the whole way of seeing built into it, deserves an honest word. Hamilton frames the tribes as a threat that American soldiers must be posted to guard against. But turn the map around for a moment. From the point of view of those nations, it was the United States that was the expanding power, pushing settlers relentlessly westward onto lands that were already lived on. The reason Britain and Spain could make allies on the frontier at all was precisely that Native peoples had, as Hamilton himself half admits, the most to fear from American expansion. So the frontier danger Hamilton treats as a simple fact of nature was, in large part, the friction of American settlers moving into other people's homelands. Hamilton is writing from squarely inside the settler's viewpoint, and reading him well means seeing both what he says and what that viewpoint leaves out. The military posts he is defending here were, among other things, the leading edge of a long and often brutal dispossession. That is part of what this paper is really about, even though Hamilton would never have described it that way.

Keeping both of those things in view at once — the shrewdness of Hamilton's argument about power, and the human cost baked into the frontier it defended — is just what it means to read these papers honestly, two hundred and forty years on.

So here is where Number Twenty-Four leaves us. Hamilton has stared down the scariest word in the American political vocabulary — a standing army in peacetime — and he has refused to either ban it or apologize for it. Instead he has argued that the honest thing, and the safe thing, is to admit the country will need some full-time soldiers, and then to bind those soldiers tightly to the will of the elected people through the power of the purse. Do not pretend you can live with no army. Make sure the army can never live without your yes.

And he is not finished with the subject. In Federalist Number Twenty-Five, still Alexander Hamilton, the argument turns to a different version of the same fear. Some critics were saying: fine, maybe somebody needs to keep troops — but let it be the individual states, not the national government. Keep the army close to home, where it is easier to watch. Hamilton is going to argue that this apparent compromise is actually more dangerous than the thing it claims to fix, and that leaving the common defense to thirteen separate states would invite exactly the rivalry and ruin this whole series has been warning about from the start.
