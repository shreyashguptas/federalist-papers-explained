At the end of the last episode, we saw Jay make his hardest case about the outside world. Nations go to war for unjust reasons. Kings go to war for personal vanity. Commercial rivals do not wish you well. And the world will treat you exactly as weak as you actually are. That was Federalist No. 4, and it completed one half of Jay's argument about foreign danger.

But Jay is not finished. Because there is a danger he has not yet addressed, and in some ways it is the most unsettling one. It is the danger that comes not from across the ocean, but from next door. What happens between the pieces, if America breaks apart?

That is the question at the center of Federalist No. 5. Still John Jay, still writing as Publius, still building the case for union. But now the threat he is warning about is not Britain or France or Spain. It is the Americans themselves, divided into separate confederacies, turning on each other.

Jay opens this paper in an unusual way. He does not begin with his own words. He begins with a quote from Queen Anne of England, from a letter she sent to the Scottish Parliament on July 1, 1706.

Now, that date matters. In 1706, England and Scotland were two separate kingdoms that shared a monarch but had their own parliaments, their own laws, and their own policies. They had been linked by a shared crown since 1603, when King James the Sixth of Scotland inherited the English throne and became James the First of England. But for over a century after that, the two kingdoms remained legally and politically separate. They had different trade policies. They had different foreign relationships. And they had a long, painful history of conflict, suspicion, and mutual interference.

By 1706, a formal union was being negotiated. The idea was to merge the two parliaments into one, creating a single Kingdom of Great Britain. Queen Anne supported this union, and her letter to the Scottish Parliament was an argument for it.

Here is what Jay quotes her saying. "An entire and perfect union will be the solid foundation of lasting peace. It will secure your religion, liberty, and property; remove the animosities amongst yourselves, and the jealousies and differences betwixt our two kingdoms. It must increase your strength, riches, and trade; and by this union the whole island, being joined in affection and free from all apprehensions of different interest, will be enabled to resist all its enemies."

And then a second passage. "We most earnestly recommend to you calmness and unanimity in this great and weighty affair, that the union may be brought to a happy conclusion, being the only effectual way to secure our present and future happiness, and disappoint the designs of our and your enemies, who will doubtless, on this occasion, use their utmost endeavors to prevent or delay this union."

Jay does not explain at length why he is starting with this quote. He lets it do the work on its own. But think about what he is doing.

He is showing his readers that the exact same argument, the exact same choice, has played out before. A century earlier, on the same island that most Americans traced their ancestry to, two peoples who shared a language and a monarch but maintained separate governments faced a decision: unite fully, or continue as divided neighbors. And the argument for union sounded almost identical to the one Jay has been making across these papers. Union means peace, strength, and security. Division means jealousy, weakness, and vulnerability to enemies who will exploit your separateness.

Queen Anne even warns that enemies of union will try to prevent or delay it. That is almost word for word what Hamilton warned about in Federalist No. 1, when he said that certain men would resist the Constitution because fragmentation served their personal ambitions.

And here is the thing Jay's readers would have known that makes this quote especially powerful. The union of England and Scotland actually happened. The Acts of Union passed in 1707, creating Great Britain. And Great Britain had gone on to become the most powerful nation in the world. The island that had spent centuries divided into quarreling kingdoms became, once united, the dominant commercial, naval, and military power of the eighteenth century.

Jay's readers did not need that spelled out. They were living under the consequences of British power. They had just fought a war to free themselves from it. The lesson was unmistakable. Union had made Britain formidable. Division had kept it weak. If Americans wanted to avoid being weak, they should learn from that example.

Now, having set up the British parallel, Jay draws the lesson explicitly. He reminds his readers of what he established in the previous paper: that weakness and division at home invite dangers from abroad, and that nothing would tend more to secure America from those dangers than union, strength, and good government within itself.

Then he says something worth pausing on. He says this subject is copious and cannot easily be exhausted. In other words, there is more to say than one paper can hold. He has been making this case for three essays now, and he still has ground to cover. That is a signal to his readers that the argument about foreign danger is not a quick point to be made and set aside. It is deep, and the dangers are layered.

Jay turns to the history of Great Britain itself as a cautionary tale. And this is where the essay really finds its footing.

He says the history of Britain is the one Americans know best, and it gives many useful lessons. Americans can profit from British experience without paying the price the British paid.

Then he makes an observation that sounds obvious but carries real weight. He says that even though it seems like common sense that the people of an island should be one nation, the British were for ages divided into three, England, Scotland, and Wales. And those three were almost constantly embroiled in quarrels and wars with one another.

Now here is the part that connects directly to the American situation. Jay says that even though England, Scotland, and Wales had the same true interest when it came to dealing with continental European powers, their mutual jealousies were perpetually kept inflamed by the arts and policies of those outside nations. For a long series of years, the three kingdoms were far more inconvenient and troublesome to each other than they were useful and assisting to each other.

Think about what Jay is describing. Three neighboring peoples on the same island, with the same strategic interests, who should have been natural allies, spent centuries weakening each other instead of strengthening each other. And foreign powers, France in particular, actively encouraged that division. It was in France's interest to keep England and Scotland at odds. A divided Britain was a weaker Britain, and a weaker Britain meant a stronger France.

Jay is holding up this history as a mirror. If Americans divide into three or four confederacies, would not the same thing happen? Would not similar jealousies arise, and be in like manner cherished?

And then he answers his own question. Instead of being joined in affection and free from all apprehension of different interests, he says, borrowing Queen Anne's own language, envy and jealousy would soon extinguish confidence and affection. The partial interests of each confederacy, instead of the general interests of all America, would be the only objects of their policy and pursuits. And like most other bordering nations, they would always be either involved in disputes and war, or live in the constant apprehension of them.

That is a bleak picture. But Jay is not guessing. He is reading history. The pattern he is describing, neighboring states that share a common origin but develop separate interests, growing suspicious and hostile over time, is one of the most common patterns in the history of the world. It happened between England and Scotland. It happened between the Greek city-states. It happened between the Italian republics of the Renaissance. It happened everywhere that peoples who might have been one chose instead to be many.

You might wonder whether Jay is being too pessimistic. After all, couldn't separate American confederacies simply choose to get along? Couldn't they maintain friendly relations through good intentions and shared memory?

Jay's answer, built from everything he has seen in diplomacy and everything he has read in history, is no. Not reliably. Not over time. Good intentions fade. Shared memories lose their hold. Separate governments develop separate interests. And separate interests, left to grow unchecked, produce rivalry.

Now Jay moves to a specific argument that cuts even deeper. He says that even the most optimistic advocates for separate confederacies cannot reasonably suppose that those confederacies would remain equal in strength for very long.

This is a crucial point, and it is worth slowing down for.

When people in 1787 imagined dividing America into, say, three confederacies, a northern, a middle, and a southern, they might have imagined those three units starting out roughly comparable. Maybe each would have a similar population, similar resources, similar economic potential.

Jay says that is unrealistic even at the start. But even if you could somehow arrange it, even if you could draw the lines perfectly so that each confederacy began on an exactly equal footing, that equality would not last.

Why not? Because different regions have different natural advantages. Some have better harbors. Some have richer soil. Some are positioned along major trade routes. Some attract more immigration. Beyond geography, Jay says, the governments of the different confederacies would not all be equally well run. One might have better leadership, wiser policies, more prudent management of its economy and its military. Over time, those differences in governance would compound. The confederacy with the best government would grow stronger. The ones with weaker leadership would fall behind.

And that growing inequality is where the real danger begins.

Jay describes it with a clarity that reads almost like a law of political nature. Whenever it happens, and happen it would, that any one of these confederacies rises significantly above its neighbors in political importance, those neighbors would immediately begin to look at it with envy and fear. Both of those passions, envy and fear, would lead the weaker confederacies to support whatever might diminish the stronger one's importance. They would also hold back from any action that might help it grow further or even help it secure its current position.

And the stronger confederacy would notice. It would not take long. It would begin to lose confidence in its neighbors. It would start to feel the same unfriendly disposition toward them that they feel toward it. And then Jay delivers a line that captures something deep about how distrust works between nations. He writes, "Distrust naturally creates distrust, and by nothing is good-will and kind conduct more speedily changed than by invidious jealousies and uncandid imputations, whether expressed or implied."

That sentence describes a spiral. Distrust breeds distrust. Suspicion breeds suspicion. Once two neighboring powers begin to doubt each other's intentions, every ambiguous action gets read in the worst possible light. Friendly gestures are dismissed as manipulation. Neutral acts are interpreted as hidden aggression. The relationship degrades not because of any single dramatic event, but because the atmosphere of suspicion poisons everything gradually.

Anyone who has watched two allied countries or two neighboring states drift apart over decades will recognize this pattern. It does not require villains. It requires only separate interests, unequal growth, and the slow erosion of trust that follows.

Now Jay turns to a specific prediction about which confederacy would likely become the strongest. And this is where we get one of the most vivid images in the entire essay.

He writes, "The North is generally the region of strength, and many local circumstances render it probable that the most Northern of the proposed confederacies would, at a period not very distant, be unquestionably more formidable than any of the others."

And then comes the image. "No sooner would this become evident than the Northern Hive would excite the same ideas and sensations in the more southern parts of America which it formerly did in the southern parts of Europe."

The Northern Hive. Jay is comparing the northern confederacy to a beehive, a swarming, productive, expansive force. And he is alluding to a phrase that was used in European politics to describe the vigorous, aggressive, rapidly growing nations of northern Europe, particularly those in Scandinavia and the Germanic regions, whose populations had historically expanded southward through migration, invasion, and colonization. The barbarian invasions that helped bring down the Roman Empire, the Viking raids, the movement of Germanic peoples across Europe, these were all associated in the European mind with the restless energy of the north pouring into the warmer, wealthier, more settled south.

Jay is saying that a powerful northern American confederacy would provoke exactly this kind of anxiety in the southern confederacies. The north, with its harbors, its shipping industry, its growing population, and its commercial energy, would become a hive of activity. And the southern confederacies, watching that growth, would feel the same unease that southern Europe once felt watching the vigorous peoples of the north.

And then Jay adds a line that is half prediction, half warning. He says it does not appear to be a rash conjecture that the young swarms of the Northern Hive might often be tempted to gather honey in the more blooming fields and milder air of their luxurious and more delicate neighbors.

That is poetic language for a hard idea. Jay is suggesting that a powerful northern confederacy would eventually be tempted to expand at the expense of weaker southern confederacies. Not necessarily through outright invasion, but through economic pressure, territorial ambition, and the kind of assertive expansion that strong neighbors always exert over weak ones. The young swarms gathering honey in the more blooming fields. It is a beautiful image for an ugly reality.

For modern listeners, it is worth noting that Jay's prediction about northern strength turned out to be remarkably accurate, though in a way he could not have fully foreseen. The northern states did grow faster in population, industry, and economic power over the decades that followed. And that growing disparity between north and south did become one of the defining tensions of American politics, eventually contributing to the Civil War. Jay could not have predicted the specific form that conflict would take. But his basic insight, that unequal growth between neighboring political units breeds resentment and conflict, was exactly right.

You might ask whether Jay is essentially arguing that the north would have been the aggressor in any division. Not exactly. His point is structural, not moral. He is saying that inequality itself is the problem. Whichever confederacy grew strongest would become the object of fear and jealousy from the others. And whichever confederacies fell behind would become tempting targets for the stronger one. The specific direction of the threat matters less than the dynamic. Unequal neighbors do not stay peaceful neighbors.

Now Jay pulls the threads together into one of the most important conclusions in the paper. He writes that those who consider the history of similar divisions and confederacies will find abundant reason to expect that these proposed American confederacies would be neighbors only in the sense that they would be borderers. They would neither love nor trust one another. On the contrary, they would be a prey to discord, jealousy, and mutual injuries. And here comes the devastating phrase. Jay says they would place Americans exactly in the situation that some foreign nations doubtless wish to see them in, namely, formidable only to each other.

Formidable only to each other. That phrase is the heart of the essay. Jay is saying that if America divides, the separate confederacies would not be formidable to the outside world. They would not be strong enough to deter Britain or France or Spain. They would spend their strength, their attention, and their resources watching each other, competing with each other, and preparing against each other. All of that energy that could have been directed outward, toward protecting the whole continent from foreign interference, would instead be directed inward, toward the rivalries between neighbors.

And that is exactly what European powers would want. A divided America, consumed by internal rivalries, would be no threat to European interests in the Western Hemisphere. It would be easy to manage, easy to manipulate, and easy to dominate. Jay is saying that division is not just a domestic problem. It is a gift to every foreign power that would rather see America weak.

Having established that separate confederacies would become rivals rather than partners, Jay now takes on the most common counterargument. At this point you might be thinking: even if these confederacies became rivals, couldn't they still form alliances when it really mattered? Couldn't they agree to defend each other against foreign threats, even if they didn't get along perfectly day to day?

That is exactly what some people in 1787 were arguing. Even if America divided into separate confederacies, those confederacies could form offensive and defensive alliances. They could agree to mutual defense pacts. They could create the kind of cooperation through treaty that they currently have through union.

Jay says this is a fantasy.

He asks a pointed historical question. When did the independent states into which Britain and Spain were formerly divided ever combine in such an alliance or unite their forces against a foreign enemy? The answer, which Jay's readers would have known, is that they did not. England and Scotland, before their union, spent far more time fighting each other than cooperating against outsiders. The independent kingdoms of medieval Spain spent centuries warring among themselves before the gradual process of unification that culminated in the marriage of Ferdinand and Isabella in 1469.

The pattern is consistent. Independent neighboring states that share a common cultural background but maintain separate governments do not form reliable alliances. They form rivalries.

But Jay goes further. He explains why alliances between separate American confederacies would be especially unreliable. And his reasoning is specific and concrete.

Each confederacy, he says, would have its own commerce with foreign nations to regulate by its own separate treaties. And because the different confederacies would produce different goods suited to different markets, those treaties would be essentially different from each other.

Think about what that means in practice. A northern confederacy, with its shipping industry, its fisheries, and its manufacturing, might find its most important trading partner in one European nation. A southern confederacy, exporting tobacco, rice, and indigo, might find its most important trading partner in a completely different European nation. Each confederacy would develop its own commercial relationships, its own economic dependencies, and its own political attachments to different foreign powers.

Jay spells out the consequence. Different commercial concerns must create different interests, and different interests must create different degrees of political attachment to different foreign nations. It might very well happen, he says, that the foreign nation with whom the southern confederacy is at war would be the very nation with whom the northern confederacy is most eager to preserve peace and friendship.

That is not an abstract possibility. It is exactly how European politics worked. In the eighteenth century, alliances shifted constantly because different nations had different commercial and strategic interests. Britain and France were rivals. Spain and Britain were rivals. The Dutch Republic navigated between larger powers based on trade interests. Jay is saying that separate American confederacies would be pulled into the same web of competing foreign attachments, and those attachments would make mutual defense agreements between the confederacies nearly impossible to maintain.

If your most important trading partner is the country your neighbor is fighting, you are not going to rush to your neighbor's defense. You are going to find reasons to stay neutral. You might even find reasons to side with the other party, if the economic incentives are strong enough.

A fair question here is whether this is really so different from what happens within a single country. After all, even within a united America, different regions have different economic interests. The north and the south traded with different partners. Could not those tensions exist within a union just as easily as between confederacies?

They could, and they did. But Jay's point is about the mechanism for resolving those tensions. Within a single nation, under one government, competing regional interests get negotiated, compromised, and balanced through a shared political process. The national government can set trade policy that accounts for the whole country. It can make treaties that serve the general interest, even when particular regions would prefer a different arrangement. That process is messy and sometimes unfair, but it holds the country together.

Between separate confederacies, there is no such mechanism. Each confederacy pursues its own interests with no obligation to consider the others. There is no shared legislature to negotiate compromises. There is no national executive to enforce a common policy. The only tool available is diplomacy between sovereign states, and Jay has already shown how unreliable that is.

Now Jay reaches the darkest part of his warning. He says it is far more probable that in America, as in Europe, neighboring nations acting under the impulse of opposite interests and unfriendly passions would frequently be found taking different sides in foreign conflicts.

Now you might wonder: even with all this rivalry, wouldn't the shared threat of European powers be enough to pull the confederacies back together when it truly mattered?

Jay answers with a point about geography that changes the entire calculation. Considering America's distance from Europe, he says, it would be more natural for these confederacies to apprehend danger from one another than from distant nations. A foreign power three thousand miles across the Atlantic is frightening in theory but far away in practice. A rival confederacy sharing a land border is right there, every day, with armies that can march and grievances that are always fresh.

And because of that proximity, each confederacy would be more inclined to seek protection against its American neighbors by forming alliances with foreign powers than to seek protection against foreign powers by forming alliances with its American neighbors.

Let that sink in. Jay is saying that the most likely outcome of American division is not a group of friendly confederacies cooperating against a dangerous outside world. It is a group of hostile confederacies, each allied with a different European power, using those foreign alliances as leverage against each other.

A northern confederacy allied with Britain. A southern confederacy allied with France. A western confederacy seeking Spanish support. Each one inviting a foreign patron onto American soil, not to defend against invasion, but to gain advantage over the confederacy next door.

You might wonder whether Jay is being dramatic. But again, this is not a hypothetical pattern. It is exactly what happened in Europe for centuries. The Italian city-states of the Renaissance invited French, Spanish, and German armies onto the Italian peninsula to gain advantage over each other. The result was that Italy became a battleground for foreign powers and remained divided and weak for three hundred years. The Greek city-states allied with Persia against each other. The small states of Germany were played against each other by France and Austria for generations.

The pattern is so common in history that it is almost a law. When small neighboring states cannot unite, they seek foreign patrons. And foreign patrons, once invited in, do not leave on your schedule.

Which brings Jay to his final and most chilling warning. He writes, "And here let us not forget how much more easy it is to receive foreign fleets into our ports, and foreign armies into our country, than it is to persuade or compel them to depart."

That sentence should stop you cold. It is one thing to invite a foreign ally to help you in a dispute with your neighbor. It is quite another to get that ally to go home when the dispute is over. Foreign powers that send armies and fleets do not do so out of generosity. They do so to advance their own interests. And once they are established on your soil, with their troops in your cities and their ships in your harbors, the balance of power has shifted. You are no longer the one making decisions. They are.

Jay drives this home with a historical reference that his readers would have recognized instantly. He asks: how many conquests did the Romans and others make in the characters of allies? And what innovations did they, under the same character, introduce into the governments of those whom they pretended to protect?

The Roman example is devastating because it was so systematic. Rome did not always conquer by direct invasion. Often it began by offering protection, by sending troops to help one Greek city-state against another, or one Hellenistic kingdom against a rival. The Roman legions would arrive as allies, as liberators, as peacekeepers. And then they would stay. And then, gradually, the "protected" state would find that its own government was being reshaped to suit Roman interests, that its foreign policy was being dictated from Rome, that its independence had evaporated without a single battle being fought against Rome itself.

Jay is warning Americans that this is exactly what would happen if separate confederacies began inviting European powers onto the continent. Britain or France or Spain would arrive as an ally of one confederacy, offering help against a rival. And then they would not leave. Their fleets would remain in American ports. Their armies would remain on American soil. Their influence would seep into the government of whatever confederacy had been foolish enough to invite them. And eventually, the independence that Americans had fought a revolution to win would be quietly surrendered to a foreign protector who had entered as a friend.

You might notice that Jay has now completed a kind of circle across these last three papers. In Federalist No. 3, the danger was that America's own misconduct would give foreign powers a just cause for conflict. In Federalist No. 4, the danger was that foreign powers would attack even without just cause, simply because divided states are tempting targets. Now, in Federalist No. 5, the danger is that Americans themselves would invite foreign powers in, not as conquerors but as allies in their own internal rivalries. The threat has moved from external aggression, to external opportunism, to self-inflicted vulnerability. Each paper has brought the danger closer to home.

And that is what makes this essay the sharpest of Jay's contributions. The foreign danger is not just something that happens to you. It is something you do to yourself, by dividing, by letting jealousy replace affection, by allowing the slow drift of separate interests to pull you apart until you are so afraid of each other that you reach for foreign help against your own people.

Jay closes with a simple challenge. Let candid men judge, he says, whether the division of America into any given number of independent sovereignties would tend to secure the country against the hostilities and improper interference of foreign nations.

The answer, by this point in Jay's argument, is obviously no. Division does not secure you. It exposes you. It turns your neighbors into rivals. It turns your rivals into proxies for foreign powers. It turns your ports into anchorages for foreign fleets. And it turns the independence you fought for into a memory.

It is worth stepping back and seeing what Jay has accomplished across these four papers, Federalist Nos. 2 through 5.

In Federalist No. 2, he established that Americans are already one people, united by geography, culture, and shared sacrifice. The union is not an imposition. It is a reflection of what already exists.

In Federalist No. 3, he argued that union reduces the just causes of war. One government handles treaties more consistently, restrains local provocations more effectively, and resolves disputes more credibly than a patchwork of separate governments ever could.

In Federalist No. 4, he turned to unjust causes of war. Even if America behaves perfectly, foreign powers may attack for their own reasons, out of commercial jealousy, territorial ambition, or personal royal vanity. The only protection against that kind of danger is not good behavior but strength, and strength comes from unity.

And now, in Federalist No. 5, he has shown that division would not just weaken each confederacy against the outside world. It would turn the confederacies against each other. Inequality would breed jealousy. Jealousy would breed distrust. Different commercial ties would pull them toward different foreign patrons. And those foreign patrons, once invited in, would be almost impossible to remove.

Together, these four papers make a complete argument about foreign danger. They move from identity, to just causes of war, to unjust causes of war, to the internal rivalries that would make divided states vulnerable to all of these dangers at once. It is one of the most tightly constructed arguments in the entire Federalist series, and Jay builds it with the steady, measured confidence of a man who has spent his career watching exactly these dynamics play out in the real world of international politics.

Jay is finished now. He wrote five Federalist Papers in all, four in this opening sequence and one later contribution on the treaty power. After this, illness will take him out of the project, and Hamilton and Madison will carry the argument forward. But Jay's opening sequence stands as a remarkable achievement on its own. He took the broadest, most fundamental question facing the country, whether to remain united or to break apart, and he answered it with a combination of historical knowledge, diplomatic experience, and clear-eyed realism about human nature that none of the other papers quite match.

Next time, the argument will shift. Hamilton takes over in Federalist No. 6, and he will push the case against division even further, not by looking at foreign danger but by looking directly at the history of conflict between neighboring republics and confederacies. If Jay's warning was about what the outside world would do to a divided America, Hamilton's warning will be about what divided Americans would do to each other. The tone will be different. The examples will be different. But the conclusion will be the same. Division is not safety. Division is danger.
