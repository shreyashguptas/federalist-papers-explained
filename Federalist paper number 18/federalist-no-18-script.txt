At the end of the last episode, Hamilton made you a promise. Though if you were one of the people he was arguing against, it might have sounded more like a threat.

For three papers running, he had been making one stubborn claim. A loose union of states does not fail because its center grows too strong. It fails because its center stays too weak, while the member governments stay too strong. In Federalist Number Seventeen, he told you the states would always hold the people's love, because the states run the daily justice that people can actually see and feel. And then he said the great political mistake of his age was that people had aimed their fear at the wrong target. They stood guard against a tyrant in the capital, while the real danger, a union too feeble to do its job, crept up from the opposite direction. He called it giving our jealousy a direction to the wrong side.

And he closed Number Seventeen with a promise to prove all of this. Not with theory this time. Not with arguments about human nature. With history. A review of the actual confederacies of the past, he said, would be the subject of some ensuing papers.

Today, that bill comes due.

This is The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Eighteen. Federalist Number Eighteen, which appeared in the New York newspapers in the first week of December, seventeen eighty-seven. And it comes with a change at the writing desk that is worth pausing on.

Up to now, the papers have mostly been solo performances. Jay wrote his run. Hamilton wrote his. Madison stepped in for the famous Number Ten and again for Number Fourteen. But Numbers Eighteen, Nineteen, and Twenty are different. They are the collaboration papers, signed by Hamilton and Madison together. The modern view of historians is that Madison did most of the actual writing here, with Hamilton helping. And there is a reason it fell mostly to Madison, a reason that tells you something lovely about the man.

In the months before the Constitutional Convention, while other delegates were packing their bags, James Madison was reading. His friend Thomas Jefferson, posted in Paris, had been shipping him crate after crate of books from Europe, and Madison had buried himself in the histories of every confederacy he could find, ancient and modern. He filled a notebook, page after page, with what went wrong in each one. He titled it Notes on Ancient and Modern Confederacies. He came to Philadelphia having essentially performed an autopsy on every union that had ever died.

And that is exactly the right word for these next papers. Autopsy. Because what Hamilton and Madison are about to do, starting today, is lay the dead confederacies of history out on the table, one by one, and show you what killed them. They want you to look at the bodies. They want you to see that they all died of the same disease.

And they begin where the Western imagination always begins. Ancient Greece.

Now, when you hear ancient Greece, you probably think of Athens. Marble columns, philosophers, the birth of democracy, Sparta and its warriors, the plays, the Olympics. That is the Greece everyone remembers. But there is another Greece, a forgotten one, and it is the Greece this paper cares about. It is the Greece that tried, twice, to become something like a United States of city-states. To take dozens of proud, independent towns and bind them into a single union. And both times, it failed. Madison and Hamilton are going to walk us through both failures, because the founders studied them the way a doctor studies a fatal case. Not for the drama. For the cause of death.

The first body on the table is a thing called the Amphictyonic Council.

It is a mouthful of a name, so let me just tell you what it was. It was a league of Greek cities, bound together by a shared council where each city sent deputies and each city got an equal vote. And on paper, this council was powerful. Genuinely powerful. Let me give you the list of what it was allowed to do, because the list matters enormously to the argument.

The Amphictyonic Council could declare war and carry it on. It could settle, as the final word, any quarrel between member cities. It could fine a city that broke the rules. It could raise the entire combined army of the league and march it against any member that disobeyed. It could admit new members. And then there is the part that has no equivalent in the modern world at all. The council was the guardian of the temple at Delphi, the single holiest site in all of Greece, home of the famous oracle. Which meant the league did not just have armies on its side. It had the gods on its side. To defy the council was to risk being branded a thief of sacred things, an enemy of heaven itself. The members even swore a binding oath to defend one another and to take vengeance on anyone who violated the temple.

Sit with that for a second, because here is the punch line that Madison wants you to feel. This ancient league, this Amphictyonic Council, had more power on paper than the government Hamilton has spent the last fifteen papers begging Americans to create. It could do things the Articles of Confederation could only dream of doing. It could make war, settle disputes for good, punish, coerce, and call God as a witness.

So Madison sets it up with a line dripping in irony.

"In theory, and upon paper, this apparatus of powers seems amply sufficient for all general purposes."

In theory, and upon paper. Read those words slowly, because they are a trap, and Madison is about to spring it. He has just spent a paragraph dazzling you with everything this league was authorized to do, and then he taps that little phrase, upon paper, to remind you that a list of powers and a working government are not the same thing. A constitution is not what it says it can do. A constitution is what it actually does when real people test it.

And what did this magnificent paper league actually do? Madison turns the page and gives you the autopsy in one cold sentence. Very different, he says, was the experiment from the theory.

So what went wrong? Why did a league with armies, courts, and the gods themselves fall apart?

And here is where you should feel a jolt of recognition, because the answer is the exact same flaw Hamilton has been hammering since Federalist Number Fifteen. The council's power, Madison explains, ran through deputies who were appointed by the cities, and who answered to the cities, and whose power was exercised on the cities. Not on people. On governments. It was a government that acted on other governments. And we have heard, across the last three episodes, what that produces. A law aimed at a city can only be enforced against a whole city, which means it can only be enforced by war. So the grand power to coerce a disobedient member looked terrifying on paper and was almost useless in practice, because using it meant the league making war on its own. The very thing the league existed to prevent.

That is the deep cut of this whole episode, so let me say it plainly. The Amphictyonic Council proves that you cannot fix a broken structure by writing stronger words on the parchment. You can give a weak union an impressive list of powers, and it will still be weak, because the weakness was never about the list. It was about the design. A government that can only act on member states, and never on individual citizens, will always be a government in name only.

For a more modern echo of the same lesson, think about the League of Nations after the First World War. On paper, it was magnificent. Collective security. A council of the world's nations. The machinery to stop aggression before it spread. It had its small successes in the calm of the nineteen twenties. But when real aggression came in the nineteen thirties, the League could not compel its members to do anything, because it acted on member nations who could simply ignore it and walk away. Impressive on paper. Helpless when it mattered most. Madison would not have been surprised for one second. He had already seen this movie in Greek.

So what does a union look like when the center cannot actually govern? It does not look like calm. It looks like a playground ruled by whoever is biggest.

Because once the league could not really discipline its members, the strongest members simply took turns running the place. Madison runs through it almost wearily. Athens, by the count he borrows from the old Greek orator Demosthenes, ran the whole show for some seventy years. Then Sparta took its turn and ran it for a generation more. Then, after a great battle at a place called Leuctra shattered Spartan power, the city of Thebes had its turn on top. The gavel just passed from one bully to the next. And whenever a quarrel came before the council, the deputies of the strong cities, Madison says, simply awed and corrupted the deputies of the weak ones, and the verdict went to whoever had the most muscle. So much for being the final court of justice. The court belonged to whoever could buy it or scare it.

And here is the line that captures what that did to the smaller cities. By the theory of their league, every city was supposed to be equal, each one circling the common center in equal pride and majesty, like planets around a sun. But in reality, Madison says, the little cities had become mere satellites of the orbs of primary magnitude. Satellites. Little moons, with no light of their own, dragged helplessly around the giant cities. The promise was a union of equals. The reality was a few big planets and a lot of captured moons.

Now, a fair question to stop on here. These were the Greeks. The people who basically invented Western political thought. How did they, of all people, botch something this basic? Were they just not clever enough?

And Madison's answer, borrowed from a French historian he quotes, is heartbreaking, because it is the opposite of stupidity. Had the Greeks been as wise as they were courageous, the line goes, they would have learned from their near-disasters and used their moments of peace to build a real, tighter union. They had the chance. After they heroically beat back the Persian invasions, the greatest external threat they ever faced, there was a window. They could have come together. Instead, Athens and Sparta, both drunk on the glory they had just won, turned on each other. They became first rivals, then enemies, and they did each other, as Madison's source puts it, infinitely more mischief than they had suffered from Xerxes — the Persian king whose vast invasion they had once united to throw back. That rivalry curdled into the long, ruinous Peloponnesian War. The same war, you may remember from Episode Six, that Hamilton blamed partly on the private grudges of one Athenian leader, Pericles. The war that ended with Athens — the city whose own ambition had done so much to bring it on — broken and defeated.

So the Greeks were not fools. They were brave, brilliant, and doomed, because bravery and brilliance cannot substitute for a sound structure. That is the drumbeat under everything in this paper. Good people, bad design.

And a weak union does not just rot from the inside. It rings a dinner bell for predators on the outside. Which brings us to the darkest and most important story in the paper. The story of how Greece actually lost its freedom. And I want to tell it properly, because it is the engine of the whole episode.

Here is the thing about how Greece fell. You might expect a story about losing your country to begin with an invasion. A foreign army at the gates, a heroic last stand. This is not that story. This is worse, because there was no dramatic invasion. There was an open door, and a guest who was invited in.

The guest was Philip of Macedon. Macedon was a kingdom to the north of Greece, long looked down on by the sophisticated Greek cities as a half-barbaric backwater. And its king, Philip, was one of the most patient and cunning men in the ancient world. He wanted Greece. And he understood something about the Amphictyonic Council that the Greeks themselves kept forgetting. He understood that a divided league is not a wall against an outsider. It is a way in.

His opening came from one of those petty, righteous quarrels that a weak union has no way to contain. A member region called Phocis had ploughed up some land that belonged to the temple, the sacred ground of Apollo. Sacrilege. The council fined them. The Phocians, egged on by Athens and Sparta, who were happy to use the dispute against their rivals, refused to pay. Other cities took up arms to enforce the fine and to avenge the insulted god. It was exactly the kind of small, bitter, internal fight that a real government settles in a courtroom and that a weak confederacy lets metastasize into a war.

And into that war, the weaker side made a fatal decision. They invited Philip in to help them.

He could not have asked for a better gift. Philip, Madison tells us, had secretly been fostering the whole quarrel from the start, waiting for exactly this invitation. And once he was inside the Greek world as a helpful ally, he never left. He didn't smash his way in from the outside, the way a foreign army batters through a border. He worked from within, by intrigue and bribery first, and by the sword when it came to that. He used bribes and intrigue to buy the popular leaders of one city after another. With their votes and their influence, he got himself a seat on the Amphictyonic Council. The sacred council of Greece now had a wolf sitting at the table, holding a vote. And from that seat, by his arts and his arms, he made himself the master of Greece. The cities that had spent centuries too jealous to unite with each other found that they had, between them, handed the keys to a king.

And then Madison delivers the verdict on the whole tragedy, in a line you should carry with you.

"Had Greece, says a judicious observer on her fate, been united by a stricter confederation, and persevered in her union, she would never have worn the chains of Macedon; and might have proved a barrier to the vast projects of Rome."

Hear what that is saying. The chains were not inevitable. Greece was not conquered because Macedon was unstoppable. Greece was conquered because Greece was divided, and a divided people can always be picked apart by a patient enemy who is happy to be invited in as a friend. And the line reaches even further. A united Greece, it says, might have been strong enough to stop Rome itself before Rome ever became Rome. The whole later history of the ancient world might have bent the other way, if only these cities had been able to do the one thing they could never do. Stay together.

You have heard this warning before in this series, and that is not an accident. Back in Episode Five, John Jay warned that it is far easier to welcome foreign fleets into your ports than to persuade them to leave. In Episode Seven, Hamilton warned about getting tangled in the pernicious labyrinths of other people's wars. Philip of Macedon is simply the ancient, fully-played-out version of everything they were afraid of. The foreign friend who comes when you are fighting your neighbor, and then quietly becomes your owner.

So at this point you might think the lesson of this episode is just bleak and total. Confederacies are doomed. Everybody gets conquered. Why bother trying to unite at all?

And this is where the paper turns, and turns in the most important direction, because Madison did not put these stories on the table just to scare you. He put a second Greek league on the table too. And this one is different. This one is the reason for hope. This is the Achaean League.

The Achaeans were originally a cluster of smaller, less glamorous cities. While mighty Athens and Sparta strutted across the stage, the Achaean cities, Madison says, made little figure. Nobodies. But these nobodies did something the famous cities never managed. They built a real union. And the difference, Madison makes clear, is night and day.

Listen to how much tighter they wove themselves together. Where the Amphictyonic Council was a loose gathering of bickering deputies, the Achaean League had a senate with genuine authority. It had the sole power over war and peace, over treaties, over ambassadors. And crucially, it had one chief magistrate, a single commanding general — Madison, reaching for a Roman word, calls him the praetor — who actually led the league's armies and ran its government. Not a committee of squabbling delegates. One head. The cities kept control of their own local affairs, and they remained equals. But they also did something remarkable. They adopted the same laws, the same customs, the same weights and measures, even the same money. Madison adds an honest caveat here — we cannot be sure how much of that the league actually compelled and how much simply grew up among cities living that closely together. But either way, the direction was unmistakable. They were not thirteen, or thirty, separate worlds loosely tied with string. They were starting to become one people.

And here, for me, is the single most important sentence in the entire episode. Because it answers the question that has haunted this whole stretch of the series. Does a tighter union crush the life out of its members? Does giving real power to the center mean grinding down the parts?

Madison reaches for a respected French observer of Greece, the Abbé Mably, who studied exactly this question. And the answer is the heart of the whole paper.

"The popular government, which was so tempestuous elsewhere, caused no disorders in the members of the Achaean republic, because it was there tempered by the general authority and laws of the confederacy."

Let me translate that, because it is the affirmative argument this whole series has been building toward. In the proud, independent, fully sovereign cities, the ones like Athens that kept all their power to themselves, political life was a storm. Mobs, riots, sedition, chaos. But inside the Achaean League, the very same kind of people, the very same passions, produced calm and good order. Why? Not because the Achaeans were better people. Because they were tempered. Held steady. Civilized by belonging to something larger than themselves. The general authority of the union did not strangle the member cities. It settled them. It gave them better government than they could ever have given themselves alone.

That is the thing Madison most wants you to take from this episode. Union is not just a shield against conquest. Union is a cure for the chaos within. The members of a strong confederacy are better governed, more just, more peaceful, than the same people would be standing alone, jealously clutching every shred of their own sovereignty.

And the league asked something real of its members in exchange. When the famous old warrior city of Sparta was finally brought into the league, it had to give up the ancient laws of Lycurgus, its whole storied way of life, and adopt the league's laws instead. Madison points out the contrast on purpose. The old, useless Amphictyonic Council let its members keep absolutely everything and got chaos in return. The Achaean League asked its members to truly join, to actually surrender some of their separateness, and in exchange it gave them order and strength. That is the trade. That is the whole argument for the Constitution, hiding in a story about ancient Greece.

So now a question almost has to jump out at you. If the Achaean League was so much wiser and worked so much better, why on earth is it sitting here on the autopsy table with the failures? Why did this one die too?

It is the right question, and Madison is honest about it. He does not pretend the Achaean League was perfect or that it lived happily ever after. It fell. But, and this is the line that matters, he says that though it met a similar catastrophe, it by no means equally deserved it. The good union and the bad union both ended in chains. But they did not earn those chains the same way. And the difference is the final lesson.

Because the Achaean League did not die of being too united. It died of not being united enough, and of the same two poisons that killed the first league. Old jealousy, and invited predators.

Here is how it went. The league grew. City after city joined, until almost the whole southern peninsula of Greece was inside it, and for one shining moment, Madison says, all of Greece seemed ready to unite into a single confederacy at last. The best hope ancient liberty ever had was right there, within reach. And what snuffed it out? Sparta and Athens again, those proud old cities, sick with envy at the rising glory of the upstart Achaeans, threw what Madison beautifully calls a fatal damp on the whole enterprise. Jealousy of a rising neighbor, the oldest poison in this series, killed the best chance Greece ever had.

And then, cornered and frightened, the Achaeans made the same fatal mistake the first league had made. They went looking for a powerful friend. First they leaned on Macedon, the very kingdom that had once enslaved Greece, to save them from an ambitious Spartan king. A Macedonian army came, and won, and Madison draws the grim moral that this whole series keeps circling back to.

"A victorious and powerful ally is but another name for a master."

That is the sentence to tattoo on the inside of your eyelids. When you are weak and afraid, the strong friend who rescues you does not become your partner. He becomes your owner. The Achaeans called in Macedon to beat Sparta, and all their most desperate, groveling obedience could buy from their rescuer afterward was permission to keep using their own laws. They had won the battle and lost themselves.

And then comes the last predator, the biggest one. Rome. When a later Macedonian king turned on them, the Achaeans, weakened by their own internal feuds, reached for the same desperate remedy as always. They turned to a bigger outsider still. As Madison tells the story, they threw in with Rome and helped draw Roman power deeper into Greece. And the Romans, he says, eagerly embraced the opening. Of course they did.

And here Rome runs Philip's playbook, but upgraded into something colder and more brilliant. Because Rome did not show up announcing conquest. Rome showed up announcing freedom. To everyone's astonishment, Madison notes, the Romans proudly proclaimed universal liberty throughout Greece. We are not here to rule you, they said. We are here to set you free. And it was freedom on Rome's terms — which is to say, it was also a weapon. Because once Rome had positioned itself as the great liberator, it began quietly pulling the league apart from the inside. It bought up local leaders, a man named Callicrates chief among them, who became, in Madison's phrase, mercenary instruments, bought men working to lure their own countrymen into Rome's hands. And then Rome whispered to each proud city the most seductive flattery of all. It told them the league was violating their precious sovereignty. It flattered their pride. It told each city it deserved to be fully independent and free, knowing that an independent, isolated city was a city Rome could swallow whole.

And it worked. The cities, flattered into jealousy of their own union, began to pull away from it. And Madison writes the elegy.

"By these arts this union, the last hope of Greece, the last hope of ancient liberty, was torn into pieces."

The last hope of ancient liberty. Torn into pieces. Not conquered in open battle. Talked apart. Flattered apart. Bribed apart. Rome let the Greeks do most of the work themselves, simply by appealing to each city's vanity about its own independence, until there was no union left to defend, and then Rome finished a ruin its words had already started. The free Greek world ended not with a heroic last stand but with cities proudly insisting on their separateness right up until the moment the chains went on. And Madison adds the detail that should chill you most. Achaia, the heart of the league, is loaded with chains, under which it is groaning at this hour. At this hour. Madison's authority is telling him that as he writes, two thousand years later, Greece is still not free. The wound that divided cities opened never fully closed.

If that pattern sounds familiar, it should. Hamilton named it back in Episode Seven with a Latin phrase. Divide and rule. Divide et impera. Break your rival into pieces, and the pieces are easy. Rome did not even have to do the breaking. The Greeks broke themselves, out of jealousy and pride, and Rome just held out the flattery and waited.

So now we can step back and read the two autopsy reports side by side, because together they make one verdict.

The first league, the Amphictyonic, was a sham of a union. All the powers on paper, no real authority underneath, every member clutching its full independence. It produced bullying, corruption, paralysis, and an open door for Philip. It died, and Madison's verdict is that it largely deserved to.

The second league, the Achaean, was a real attempt at union. A single head, shared laws, shared money, real authority, members who actually gave something up to belong. And it produced calm, justice, strength, and the closest thing ancient Greece ever came to true unity. It died too, but it died of the jealousy of outsiders and the old reflex of calling in foreign help, not from any flaw in the union itself. It did not deserve its chains. It just couldn't, in the end, overcome the same disease that runs through this entire series.

And that disease has a name, and Madison gives it to you in the last line of the paper, and it is the exact thing Hamilton promised you at the end of Episode Seventeen. After all the history, all the bloodshed, all the bullying and betrayal, here is the one sentence the whole autopsy was for.

These stories, Madison says, illustrate "the tendency of federal bodies rather to anarchy among the members, than to tyranny in the head."

Read that against the fear the whole Constitution was fighting. People in seventeen eighty-seven were terrified of tyranny in the head. A king in the capital. A central government grown too strong. And Madison points at two thousand years of Greek corpses and says, you are watching the wrong door. Confederacies do not die of a strong head. They die of anarchy among the members. They die of squabbling, jealous, over-mighty parts and a center too weak to hold them. The danger was never the eagle growing too large. The danger was always the union flying apart.

Which is exactly, word for word, the point Hamilton left you with last time. The Greeks gave their jealousy a direction to the wrong side. They guarded against a tyrant who never came, and died of a weakness they refused to fix. They feared the strong center, and were destroyed by the weak one.

And the men writing these papers were about to do something no Greek league ever managed. They were going to build a union that learned from both bodies on the table. Like the Achaean League, but more so, it would have a real head and shared laws. And unlike any of them, it would reach past the member states and act directly on individual citizens, the one fix Hamilton has been preaching since Number Fifteen, so that its power would never depend on making war against its own members. They were reading the graveyard of confederacies, and they were determined not to become the next stone in it.

But Greece is only the first exhibit. Madison has crates of books and a whole notebook left, and the autopsy is not finished. Because someone might fairly say, well, that was the ancient world, a vanished age of oracles and oaths. Surely things are different now. Surely a confederacy in the modern world, in civilized Europe, would not fall into the same trap.

Madison is ready for that. Next time, he and Hamilton leave the ancient world behind and walk us straight into the heart of modern Europe, to look at a confederacy that was, in their own day, sprawling across the center of the continent. The Holy Roman Empire, the great Germanic body that had lasted a thousand years, along with the mountain cantons of Switzerland. They are going to show you that the disease did not die with ancient Greece. It was alive and rotting a confederacy in their own century. The graveyard, it turns out, has fresh graves too.

This has been The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Eighteen. Next time: Federalist Number Nineteen, Hamilton and Madison again, leaving Greece for the confederacies of modern Europe, and the slow-motion failure of an empire that called itself holy, Roman, and an empire, and by the founders' reckoning was barely any of the three.
