At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had just talked Americans down off their oldest fear one more time. In Number Twenty-Eight, he admitted the uncomfortable thing his critics were sure he would dodge. Yes, he said, once in a great while, any government, even a free one, has to answer force with force, because rebellions happen everywhere. And then he turned that admission into his strongest argument for the union. A government built the American way, he showed, can be strong enough to put down a revolt and still never able to enslave the people who made it, because the people are not standing alone. They live under two governments at once, their state and the union, and those two are always watching and checking each other. The people, he said, are the weight that tips the balance. Throw yourself into whichever scale is being wronged, and you decide the outcome. The masters of their own fate. That was his phrase.

But if you were listening closely across that whole stretch of papers, from Number Twenty-Three all the way through Number Twenty-Eight, you may have noticed that Hamilton keeps leaning on one word without ever quite stopping to explain it. The militia. The armed people. The citizen who keeps a musket at home and can be called out to defend the community. He keeps telling you that this armed citizenry is the real safeguard, the thing that makes a standing army unnecessary and tyranny impossible. But he has never actually stopped to say what a militia is, who is supposed to control it, how it is supposed to work, or why on earth we should trust it.

Today he does exactly that. He zooms all the way in on the one piece of the picture he has been leaning on this entire time.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-Nine. Its title is Concerning the Militia. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. It ran in the New York press in the opening days of January, seventeen eighty-eight, appearing in the Daily Advertiser on the tenth, right in the thick of the ratification fight. And it exists because the critics had found a fresh thing to be afraid of. The new Constitution, they noticed, would let the federal government organize the militia, arm it, set its training, and call it into service. To the opponents, that was a second danger stacked on top of the first. Not only might the government keep a standing army, now it could reach its hand into every state and take command of the ordinary armed citizen too. Two swords hanging over the people instead of one.

Hamilton's entire move in this paper is to take that second fear and turn it completely inside out. A well-regulated militia, he is going to argue, is not a second threat sitting next to the standing army. It is the answer to the standing army. Get the militia right, and you never need the professional army in the first place. The thing the critics wanted to fence off with warnings is the very thing that makes their deepest fear go away.

He says it in one clean line that is worth hearing directly. To render an army unnecessary, he writes, will be a more certain method of preventing its existence than a thousand prohibitions upon paper.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the hinge of the whole essay. You can try to stop a standing army the way the critics wanted, by writing bans and limits into the Constitution, a thousand prohibitions upon paper. Or you can make the army unnecessary, by keeping the people themselves capable of defending the country. Hamilton is telling you the second method is the only one that actually works. A ban is just words. A prepared citizenry is a fact. And a government that can call on a real militia when trouble comes has no excuse to build the standing army everyone is so afraid of.

So why were the critics so alarmed about handing Congress a role in all this? It is a fair question, and before we can answer it, we have to slow down and picture what a militia actually was in this world, because to Hamilton's readers it needed no explanation at all, and to us it does. This was not a metaphor. The militia was a real, living institution woven into ordinary American life, and it was old. The idea behind it was an inheritance from England, where for centuries the law had held that free men owed a duty to help defend their community, to keep arms, and to turn out when they were called. The colonies carried that idea across the ocean and built their whole system of defense on it. In most places, nearly every able-bodied free man of fighting age was, on paper, already a member of the militia. He was expected to own a musket, to keep it ready, and to show up.

And a few times a year, he did. There were muster days, sometimes called training days, when the men of a town would gather on the common green with their weapons, be counted, drill for a while under local officers, and, honestly, spend a good part of the day socializing afterward. It was part military exercise, part town gathering. Nobody mistook these men for polished professional soldiers. They were farmers and coopers and blacksmiths who spent a morning marching in rough lines a handful of times a year. That was the militia. Not an army. The community itself, lightly armed and loosely organized, ready to be summoned in an emergency.

And these were not abstract memories in seventeen eighty-eight. They were fresh. A little more than a dozen years earlier, on an April morning in seventeen seventy-five, it was exactly this kind of ordinary town militia that had stood on the greens at Lexington and Concord and fired the first shots of the Revolution. And here is a detail worth holding onto, because Hamilton is about to build his central argument on it. The men who turned out fastest that morning had a special name. They were the minutemen. They were not the whole militia. They were a picked group, the younger and readier men, chosen and organized to be able to grab their guns and respond at a minute's notice while the rest of the militia took longer to assemble. In other words, America had already invented, and already used, the exact thing Hamilton is about to propose. A smaller, sharper, better-prepared body drawn out of the larger armed public. Keep that in your pocket. It comes back.

And there is a deeper reason this whole subject ran so hot, a reason that goes back long before America. For a couple of thousand years, thinkers about free government had believed that how a country arms itself tells you whether it will stay free. The old republican ideal, going all the way back to Greece and Rome, was that a free people defends itself with its own citizens, men who pick up arms to protect their own homes and then go back to their farms and shops when the danger passes. The opposite of that, the thing free peoples learned to dread, was the standing army, a permanent body of paid professional soldiers who belong to whoever pays them, who have no trade but soldiering, and who can just as easily be turned against the people as against a foreign enemy. Rome itself was the cautionary tale. It began as a republic defended by citizen-soldiers, and it ended as an empire ruled by professional legions who made and unmade rulers at swordpoint. And Hamilton's own readers had a fresher memory still, straight out of England. Within living historical memory, the English had watched a standing army under military rule, and had come away with such a deep distrust of permanent soldiers in peacetime that they wrote protections against it into their law. So when Americans bristled at anything touching armies and militias, they were not being paranoid for no reason. They were carrying centuries of hard-won suspicion. The citizen with his own musket was the good, safe, free way to defend a country. The professional standing army was the road that had swallowed republics before. That is the emotional and historical charge sitting under this entire debate, and it is why Hamilton works so hard to show that his militia plan sits firmly on the safe side of that ancient line.

So what does the Constitution actually say about this militia, and who ends up holding it? Because this is where a lot of the fear was coming from, and the answer is more balanced than the critics let on.

The power gets split. Congress gets to organize the militia, to arm it, and to set the standard for how it is trained and disciplined. But the states keep two things, and they are not small things. The states appoint the officers, and the states do the actual training, according to the standard Congress sets. Hamilton quotes the plan's own words on the point. The Constitution empowers the Union, he notes, to provide for organizing, arming, and disciplining the militia. And then comes the crucial reservation, in the plan's own language, reserving to the states respectively the appointment of the officers, and the authority of training the militia according to the discipline prescribed by Congress.

At this point you might be wondering why the national government needs any say at all over a state's own militia. Why not just leave the whole thing to each state, the way it had always been? And Hamilton's answer is one word. Uniformity.

Picture what you actually had in that moment. Thirteen separate militias, each drilled in its own way, each with its own commands, its own formations, its own idea of what a soldier was supposed to do when an officer shouted an order. Now imagine a war, and imagine throwing those thirteen different militias together onto the same field and asking them to move as one army. They cannot. One group forms up one way, another group hears the same order and does something else entirely. They get in each other's way. They cannot support each other in the middle of a fight, because they were never taught the same drill. Hamilton's point is that a militia is only useful if the men from different states can actually operate together, with what he calls mutual intelligence and concert. And the only way to get that is for somebody to set one common standard for everybody. That somebody is the national government. Not to seize the militia away from the states, but to make sure that when Massachusetts and Virginia have to fight side by side, they are speaking the same military language.

Now we come to the part of this paper that is genuinely fresh, and it is Hamilton at his most practical and clear-eyed. It is easy to say the country should defend itself with its citizens rather than a professional army. But what would it actually take to turn the whole population into real soldiers? Hamilton stops and does the arithmetic, and this is worth slowing down for, because his honesty here is the thing that makes the rest of his argument believable.

The dream, in theory, is a whole nation trained to arms. Every farmer, every tradesman, every working man ready to march and fight like a professional. It sounds wonderful. So why not just do it, why not drill everyone properly? It is a fair question, and Hamilton takes it seriously, and then he tells you it is a fantasy, and a ruinous one. Because becoming a real soldier is not something you pick up in an afternoon. A tolerable expertness in military movements, he writes, is a business that requires time and practice. It is not a day, or even a week, that will suffice for the attainment of it.

And there is the problem. To make the entire body of citizens genuinely proficient, you would have to pull them off their farms and out of their shops again and again, drilling them constantly, all year long. Think about what that costs. Every day a farmer spends marching in a field is a day he is not planting or harvesting. Every day a blacksmith spends drilling is a day his forge is cold. Hamilton estimates that if you actually tried to keep the whole nation trained to that level, the lost work would drain something close to the entire cost of running all the state governments combined. It would be a standing wound in the economy of the country. And it would never last, because the people would not put up with it for long. So the beautiful idea of a whole nation of trained soldiers collapses the moment you count what it would take.

Here is his realistic answer, and it is the practical heart of the paper. For the great mass of ordinary citizens, do not even try to make them professional soldiers. Just keep them armed and equipped, and gather them together maybe once or twice a year. That is it. Do not pretend the whole population is an army, because it cannot be, and trying will only exhaust and impoverish everyone.

But then, alongside that lightly-armed general public, build something smaller and serious. Hamilton calls it a select corps. A body of moderate size, drawn from the militia, that actually is well-trained and genuinely ready to take the field the moment the country needs it. Small enough to train properly. Good enough to matter. And if that idea sounds familiar, it should, because it is the minutemen all over again. A picked, ready body pulled out of the larger armed public. Hamilton is not inventing something strange and new. He is taking the thing America already did at Lexington and Concord and proposing to make it the deliberate policy of the nation. And this, he says, is the real substitute for a standing army. Not a paper ban on armies. A living, drilled, ready body of citizens who can do the job a standing army would otherwise be built to do.

And now watch him land the point he has been building toward across this whole run of papers. Even suppose, he says, the country does someday raise a real professional army for some genuine emergency. Even then, that army can never become a threat to the people's freedom, as long as this trained citizenry exists beside it. He puts it in a line that is the physical version of the balance he described last time. That army, he writes, can never be formidable to the liberties of the people while there is a large body of citizens, little, if at all, inferior to them in discipline and the use of arms, who stand ready to defend their own rights and those of their fellow-citizens.

Read that slowly, because it is the whole answer to the standing-army fear in one sentence. An army is only dangerous to a free people when it is the only organized force in the country. Put a large, trained, armed citizenry next to it, people just as capable, standing on their own ground, defending their own rights, and the army simply cannot become an instrument of oppression. There is always a counterweight. This is the two-scale idea from Number Twenty-Eight, but now you can see it. The counterweight is not an abstraction. It is your neighbors, holding muskets, knowing how to use them.

For modern listeners, it is worth noticing that almost every country eventually landed exactly where Hamilton is pointing. Not a nation permanently under arms, and not a country defended by professionals alone, but a mix. A relatively small full-time force, backed by a larger trained reserve of citizens who live ordinary lives most of the time and can be called up when they are needed. In the United States, that reserve has a direct bloodline back to the militia Hamilton is describing. Today's National Guard grew out of these state militias, and it still carries the fingerprint of this exact paper. It is trained to national standards, but in ordinary times it answers to the states and their governors, and its officers are still commissioned through the states. The split Hamilton is defending here, national standard, state control, is not a historical curiosity. It is still, in its bones, how it works.

So the design is sound and the money makes sense. But the critics had more fears to throw, and Hamilton spends the back half of the paper knocking them down, and getting steadily less patient as he goes.

First there was a strange, small objection that let Hamilton show just how incoherent his opponents were being. Some of them complained that the Constitution never spells out a power to call ordinary citizens to help an officer enforce the law, the old, humble authority any sheriff had to round up bystanders to help keep the peace. From that missing detail, they leapt to a dark conclusion, that the government must therefore intend to use military force as its only tool. Hamilton points out the trap they have walked into. These are the very same people who insist, in one breath, that this government will be all-powerful and unlimited, and then turn around in the next breath and claim it is somehow too weak to do the smallest, most ordinary thing a local sheriff can already do. It cannot be both. A government with the authority to make all the laws needed to carry out its duties obviously has the small authority to ask citizens for help enforcing them. The objection, Hamilton says, is as illogical as it is insincere.

Then comes the fear that clearly bothers him most, because it is the emotional one, so he meets it head on. What if that select corps, that trained body of citizen-soldiers, becomes the tool? What if some ambitious government takes those eager, disciplined young men and turns them into the instrument of its own power, a private force pointed at everyone else?

Hamilton finds this almost too absurd to answer with a straight face, and here he drops the warmest, most human line in the entire paper. Where in the name of common-sense, he asks, are our fears to end if we may not trust our sons, our brothers, our neighbors, our fellow-citizens?

That question is the soul of the essay. Stop and really hear who the militia is. It is not a foreign army garrisoned in your town by a distant king. It is not a caste of hired strangers. It is your own son. Your brother. The man who farms the next field over. The people in the militia are the same people you live beside every day, who share your habits, your interests, your grievances, your life. The idea that these men would suddenly turn into the willing tools of a tyrant, and point their weapons at their own families and neighbors, asks you to believe something about human nature that simply is not true. A fair question here is whether an ambitious government could somehow bend them to its will anyway. And Hamilton's structural answer is the one we already met. The officers are appointed by the states. The men will always answer more to home than to any faraway capital, because home is who commissions the people giving the orders.

And then, having made the tender argument, Hamilton lets himself have some fun, because he clearly thinks the doomsayers have embarrassed themselves. Reading the pamphlets written against the Constitution, he says, is like reading some badly written horror story, one that fills the mind with nothing but frightful and distorted shapes. He reaches for an old image of monsters. Gorgons, hydras, and chimeras dire.

Those are creatures out of ancient myth, snake-haired women who turn you to stone, many-headed serpents, fire-breathing beasts stitched together from other animals. Hamilton is saying the opponents have basically been writing monster stories about the militia and asking grown adults to be terrified of them. And then he lists the actual monsters they conjured, and they are gloriously silly. New Hampshire's militia is going to be marched all the way down to Georgia. Georgia's militia hauled up to New Hampshire. Citizens dragged hundreds of miles from their homes to go crush the people of some other state they have no quarrel with. The national debt, at one point, is supposedly going to be paid off in militiamen instead of gold coins. It is a parade of nonsense, and Hamilton parades it precisely to show how flimsy it is.

But underneath the mockery is a genuinely sharp piece of logic, and it is worth pulling out, because it is the final nail. Suppose a government really did want to become tyrannical. Think it through, Hamilton says. If those rulers already had a standing army to do their dirty work, they would not need the militia at all. And if they did not have an army, and instead tried to march the militia itself off on some hateful mission, ordering ordinary citizens to go rivet the chains of slavery onto their own countrymen, what would actually happen? The militia would turn around. It would march on the tyrants who gave the order. No usurper in history ever climbed to power by first making his own weapon hate him. You do not begin your career of oppression by insulting and enraging the very men whose muskets you depend on. So the militia cannot be the road to tyranny. If anything, it is the roadblock. The armed people are the last thing a would-be tyrant would ever want to summon.

Which brings Hamilton to his final, quieter point, the one he saves for the very end. There is one more reason to put the militia under a single national roof, and it is almost gentle. When disaster strikes one state, an invasion, an uprising, a sudden emergency, a neighboring state's militia can march over and help. Put that power of mutual aid in the hands of the Union, and no state will ever sit on its hands while the state next door burns. And this was not a hypothetical worry, either. Everyone reading had just watched Massachusetts scramble to raise force against its own rebels a year or two before, the uprising we walked through back in Numbers Six and Twenty-Eight, and everyone understood how badly a neighbor in crisis might need a neighbor's help. Hamilton calls this neighborly rescue one of the deepest purposes of the whole arrangement. This mutual succor, he writes, is, indeed, a principal end of our political association. Neighbor defending neighbor, coordinated through the union, is not a side benefit. It is part of what the union is for.

Now step all the way back, because with this paper Hamilton closes out a run of essays, from Number Twenty-Three through Number Twenty-Nine, that took the single scariest word in the entire ratification debate and slowly, patiently turned it inside out. The word was army. The fear was that a strong national government would inevitably raise a permanent military force and use it to crush the very people it was supposed to protect. And across those papers, Hamilton never once dismissed the fear. He took it seriously every time. But piece by piece he rebuilt the picture, until the thing the people were most afraid of, force in the hands of the government, had been surrounded on every side by checks. The states. The two scales. The purse strings held by the people's own representatives. And here, at the end, the armed citizenry itself, the militia, standing as both the union's first line of defense against enemies abroad and one of liberty's surest guardians against tyrants at home.

That was always the answer Hamilton was building toward. The safeguard of a free people was never going to be a promise written on parchment. It was going to be the people themselves. Armed. Organized through their own states. Trained just enough to matter. Standing, in the end, as the counterweight to every force that could ever be turned against them. The citizen in arms, Hamilton is telling you, is not the thing to fear. He is the reason you do not have to be afraid.

Next time, Hamilton turns from the sword to the purse. Having spent all these papers on armies and defense and force, he arrives at the unglamorous question that decides whether any of it is even possible. Money. Where does the government get it, and what happens to a government that cannot raise it? Because an army you cannot pay for is just a wish, and a union that cannot fund itself is a union waiting to fail.
