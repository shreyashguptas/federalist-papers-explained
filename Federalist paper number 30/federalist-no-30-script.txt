At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had just talked Americans out of being afraid of their own soldiers. In Number Twenty Nine, he took the militia, the ordinary armed citizen, the thing his critics had built up into a brand new monster, and he showed that it was not some second danger stacked on top of the standing army they already dreaded. It was the cure for that danger. A body of trained citizens, he said, your sons, your brothers, your neighbors, is the best substitute anyone has ever found for a standing army, and the best guard against one. And with that, he closed out a long march of essays, running all the way back to Number Twenty Three, about swords. Armies. Navies. Militias. About who gets to hold the weapons in a free country.

Today he sets the sword down. And he picks up something that, in his hands, turns out to be just as dangerous, and just as decisive. A ledger.

This is Federalist Number Thirty. It ran in the New York Packet on Friday, the twenty eighth of December, seventeen eighty seven. It is still Alexander Hamilton. It is still signed Publius. And it opens the least glamorous stretch of the whole series, a run of papers about a single unlovely word. Taxes.

I know. Stay with me. Because this is the paper where you can feel Hamilton becoming the man he is about to become. In a little under two years, when the new government is finally up and running, this same person will be the country's first Secretary of the Treasury, the one who builds the American financial system from scratch. And almost everything he does in that job is already sitting here, in miniature, in this one essay. If you have ever wondered why Hamilton is the face on the ten dollar bill, you start here.

So let us start where he starts. Not with a number. With an idea about what money actually is.

Hamilton does not open by talking about rates, or duties, or customs houses. He opens by telling you that money is not really about money. He writes:

Money is, with propriety, considered as the vital principle of the body politic; as that which sustains its life and motion, and enables it to perform its most essential functions.

The vital principle of the body politic. Sit with that image for a second, because it is doing a lot of work. Hamilton is asking you to think of a government as a living body. And money, to that body, is not the jewelry. It is the blood. It is the thing that has to move through every part to keep the whole thing alive. A body with no blood does not get to do the interesting things, like think and grow and defend itself. It just stops.

And so, he says, a government that cannot reliably get its hands on money faces two doors, and both of them are ugly. Behind the first door, it takes what it needs by force, plundering its own people whenever the bills come due, because it has no orderly way to raise a dime. Behind the second door, it simply withers. It sinks, in his words, into a fatal atrophy, and in a short time, it dies. Plunder, or slow death. That is the real choice facing any government that cannot honestly fund itself. There is no third door, the one where it stays weak and small and everything turns out fine anyway.

Now, a fair question here is why Hamilton is even bothering to argue this. It sounds obvious. Of course a government needs money. Who on earth was saying otherwise?

Nobody, exactly. And that is the trap he is walking his readers out of. Nobody stood up and said the government should have no money. What they had done, back when they wrote America's first constitution, the Articles of Confederation, was something much more comfortable, and much more dangerous. They had given the government the need for money without the power to raise it. And to feel why that was so deadly, you have to look at the machine they actually built. Because we have all, quietly, been living with its wreckage for this whole series.

Here is how the old system worked. The Continental Congress, the government of the Union, was not allowed to tax anybody. Not you, not me, not a single citizen. What it could do was add up what it needed, divide that total among the thirteen states, and then send each state a bill. A polite, official request for money. The formal word for it was a requisition.

And on paper, the states were obligated to pay. They had no right, Hamilton notes, to argue about whether the demand was fair. Their only job was to figure out how to come up with their share. On paper.

In real life, the bills went out, and the money did not come back. A state would look at its requisition, look at its own troubles, look at the fact that the twelve other states were dragging their feet too, and quietly decide to pay a little, or pay late, or not pay at all. And there was no consequence. Congress could send another, sterner letter. That was the entire enforcement mechanism. A government that could beg, and nothing more.

Think about what that actually meant. Picture a landlord who owns thirteen apartments but is legally forbidden from collecting rent. All he is allowed to do is mail out invoices and hope. When the checks do not come, he cannot evict anyone, cannot charge a late fee, cannot do anything but write another invoice. How long does that landlord stay in business? How does he fix a leaking roof, or pay the water bill, when his entire income depends on the goodwill of tenants who have all quietly agreed that this month is a bad month?

That was the government of the United States in the seventeen eighties. We walked through the skeleton of it back in Number Twenty One, the government with no teeth. This is that same skeleton, seen from the money side. And the results were not abstract. The soldiers who had won the Revolution went unpaid. The debts from the war, owed to foreign lenders and to Americans alike, sat there gathering shame. The government's own paper money had collapsed so completely that a phrase entered the language. When Americans of that generation wanted to call a thing worthless, they said it was not worth a Continental. That was their own currency they were sneering at. Hamilton says the Union had dwindled into a state of decay, approaching nearly to annihilation. Approaching annihilation. That is not the language of a man reaching for drama. That is roughly what anyone paying attention could see with their own eyes.

And when a government cannot pay, the people it lets down first are usually the ones who served it. In the summer of seventeen eighty three, with the war essentially won, a group of Continental soldiers who had never been paid what they were owed marched on the seat of Congress in Philadelphia and surrounded the very building where the nation's government sat. And Congress, the government of the United States, had no money to satisfy them, and no force of its own to move them along. So it did the only thing it could. It ran. The Congress of the United States slipped out of Philadelphia and reassembled up the road in Princeton, New Jersey, chased out of its own home by its own unpaid soldiers. Sit with that picture, because it is the whole argument in one scene. That is what a government with an enormous need for money and no power to raise it eventually becomes. Not a tyrant. A fugitive.

He has a name for this whole method of asking and hoping. He calls it a false light in finance. The old image behind his phrase is of a traveler at night who sees a faint glow out on the marsh and follows it, certain it must be a lantern, a farmhouse, safety, and instead it leads him deeper and deeper into the swamp until he drowns. Requisitions were that glow. They looked like a way to fund a government. They were the thing pulling the country under.

So why not just make the states pay? Put some teeth on the requisition, force the money out of them, and keep the basic setup? It is the obvious fix, and Hamilton has already thought about it, and his answer is the hinge of the whole paper. You cannot fix this with force, because the force it would take is worse than the disease. Making a state pay means marching on a state. It means the national government treating Virginia, or New York, the way an army treats an enemy. He spent whole earlier papers on exactly that nightmare, a government that can only get its way by pointing weapons at its own members. The way out is not a bigger whip. The way out is to stop begging middlemen altogether, and let the national government raise its own money directly, from the people, by the ordinary, boring, orderly methods that every other functioning government on earth already uses. Money it is genuinely owed, from people it can actually reach.

And here Hamilton reaches for a strange and wonderful example, one that flips the whole fear on its head. His critics were terrified of a government with a broad power to tax. It sounded like the doorway to tyranny. So Hamilton walks them straight into the most absolute tyranny he can name, the Ottoman Empire, the Turkish sultan, a ruler with, in theory, total power over the lives and fortunes of every subject he has. And then he points out something that should stop you cold. That all powerful sultan did not have the right to impose a new tax.

You would think that would make the sultan's subjects safer. It did the exact opposite. Because the sultan still needed money, and since he could not raise it in an open, lawful way, he did something quieter and far worse. He turned his provincial governors loose on the people. He let them, in Hamilton's phrase, pillage the people without mercy. And then, once the governors had wrung the countryside dry, the sultan squeezed the money back out of the governors. It was an entire empire run on plunder, precisely because there was no honest tax at the center of it.

That is the picture Hamilton wants burned into your mind. A government that is denied a clean, open, lawful power to tax does not become gentler. It becomes a shakedown. The choice was never between taxes and freedom. The choice is between taxes you can see, written into law, debated in the open, and plunder you cannot see, arriving in the night with no rules attached to it at all. America, he says, from a like cause, had been sliding toward that same decay. Different empire, same disease. Deny a government the honest power, and you do not kill its hunger. You just teach it to feed in the dark.

Now, the smartest people on the other side had heard all of this, and they were not fools. So they offered a compromise, and it is worth taking seriously, because it sounds so reasonable. Fine, they said. Give the national government a power to tax. But only a certain kind of it. Let it collect what they called external taxes, meaning duties on goods coming in from overseas, the tariffs at the ports. But the internal taxes, the ones that reach inside the country, on land, on property, on goods bought and sold at home, leave all of those to the individual states. Split the power. The federal government gets the coastline. The states keep the interior.

And you can see why that compromise had real appeal, quite apart from just splitting the difference down the middle. Import duties are the gentlest tax there is, in political terms, because almost nobody actually feels them. They get folded quietly into the price of tea, or cloth, or sugar, and the shopper at the counter never sees a tax collector at all. Internal taxes are the opposite of invisible. A tax on your land, or your whiskey, or the deed to your house arrives with a name, and a knock, and a receipt. And these were people who had just fought an entire revolution that began, in no small part, over taxes handed down from a government far away. So the instinct to give the distant national government only the taxes you never feel, and to keep the personal, visible ones close to home, in the hands of a state you could actually see and vote out, was not some foolish reflex. It was a careful one. Hold on to that instinct, because it is the seed of the great fear that the very next paper has to answer.

And you might be thinking that is not a bad deal at all. The national government still gets a real, independent income from the ports. Why is that not enough?

Hamilton gives two reasons, and the second one is one of the most important sentences in the entire essay.

The first reason is just arithmetic. He asks, flatly, whether any honest person believes the money from import duties alone could ever cover what the country owed. Remember the debt. A mountain of it, foreign and domestic, left over from the war, sitting on top of the everyday cost of actually running a government. Tariffs collected at the docks were never going to climb that mountain. To hand the government only that one narrow stream of income was to hand it a teaspoon and point at the ocean.

But the second reason is the deep one, and it is really a rule about power itself. He lays down a maxim: every power, he says, ought to be in proportion to its object. Every power ought to be in proportion to its object. In plain English, the size of the tool has to match the size of the job. If you give a government an unlimited responsibility, keeping an entire country safe and solvent forever, but you allow it only a limited, fenced in ability to pay for that responsibility, you have built something designed to fail. You have ordered it to move a mountain and handed it a teaspoon. The duty and the resources have to be the same size, or the whole thing collapses under the weight of its own mission.

And then Hamilton makes the move that tells you he is thinking further down the road than anyone else in the room. He asks, how could you possibly cap a nation's income in advance, when you cannot possibly know, in advance, what that nation will one day need? Here is the line:

In the usual progress of things, the necessities of a nation, in every stage of its existence, will be found at least equal to its resources.

Read that one slowly. The necessities of a nation will be found at least equal to its resources. He is saying that whatever a country has, its needs will rise to meet it, and usually outrun it. A plague. A flood. A war it did not choose. A crisis no one on the drafting committee ever imagined. You cannot write next century's emergencies into this year's budget. And so any attempt to draw a neat little line and declare that the government may raise this much money and not one dollar more is a fantasy. The line will not hold, because reality does not ask for permission. The only honest thing to do is to give the government a power to raise money that is able to grow when the need grows.

And to show you exactly how a capped government dies, Hamilton runs a thought experiment. It is the sharpest thing in the paper, so let us walk through it the way he does.

Imagine, he says, that we take the compromise. The government lives on its port duties, and in peacetime, the arrangement works. The books balance. Everyone relaxes.

Then a war breaks out. Wars do that. They do not politely schedule themselves for the years when a country happens to have money to spare.

Now watch what the government is forced to do. It suddenly needs a great deal of money, quickly, and it has no power to raise any new money on its own. It cannot reach the real wealth of the country. Requisitions, we already know, are a false light on the marsh. So there is exactly one place left to turn. The money it had already promised to its creditors, the funds set aside to pay down the national debt. It has to raid them. It has to take the very money it swore it would use to repay its lenders, and pour it into the war instead. There is no other move left on the board.

And here is the trap snapping shut. The instant a government does that, the instant it breaks its promise to its lenders, it destroys the one thing it needs most in a crisis. Its credit. Its ability to borrow. Because in the modern world, Hamilton points out, even the richest nations fight their wars on borrowed money. Nobody keeps enough cash lying around for a war. You borrow. And a poor young country like America would have to borrow even more.

Hamilton did not even have to reach for a hypothetical here, and his readers knew it. The war that had just ended, the Revolution itself, had been kept alive on borrowed money, on loans from France and from Dutch bankers, without which Washington's army might simply have melted away for want of pay and powder. Every American reading this essay understood, somewhere in their bones, that the country existed at all because someone, at the decisive moment, had been willing to lend it money. So Hamilton is really asking them a very pointed question. What happens the next time we need that loan to survive, if we have spent all the years in between proving to the world that we do not pay it back?

So there is the government, in the middle of a war, turning to the lenders and asking for a loan. And the lenders look at what it just did, robbing its old creditors to make ends meet, and they ask the obvious question. Hamilton puts it as a single, devastating line:

But who would lend to a government that prefaced its overtures for borrowing by an act which demonstrated that no reliance could be placed on the steadiness of its measures for paying?

In plain words, who lends money to someone who just proved, in public, that he does not pay his debts? You might still get a loan. But you will get it, Hamilton says, on the terms where:

They would be made upon the same principles that usurers commonly lend to bankrupt and fraudulent debtors, with a sparing hand and at enormous premiums.

A sparing hand, and enormous premiums. A trickle of money at a punishing price. The loan shark's rate. That is what a nation ends up paying when it cannot be trusted, and it pays that price at the exact moment its survival is on the line.

And if you are tempted to file all of this under eighteenth century theory, it is worth stepping into our own time for just a moment, because this is not a museum piece. This is the logic the entire modern world still runs on. Countries have credit ratings, the way people do. When a nation starts to look like it might not pay its debts, lenders demand a higher interest rate to take on the risk, and the country's borrowing costs jump. In the year two thousand eleven, the government of the United States, Hamilton's own government, grown into a superpower, spent a whole summer publicly fighting over whether it would raise its debt ceiling and keep paying what it already owed. It came right up to the edge. And for the first time in the country's history, one of the major rating agencies lowered the credit rating of the United States. Not because America had run out of money. Because, for a few weeks, it looked like the country might choose not to pay. That is Hamilton's warning, playing out on cable news more than two hundred years later. The willingness to pay turns out to be worth as much as the ability to pay. A government that even flirts with breaking its word pays for it, in higher interest, long afterward.

So the power to tax, in Hamilton's hands, quietly turns into something much bigger than the power to tax. It becomes the power to borrow. It becomes the power to be believed. A government that can raise its own money can promise to pay a lender back and be taken seriously, because everyone can see there is a real income standing behind the promise. A government that has to pass the hat to thirteen other governments before it can pay anyone, he says, would need a lender of almost superhuman gullibility. Nobody sharp about money, and lenders are always sharp about money, would ever trust it.

Hamilton knows how all of this must sound. Grim. Here he is, standing at the hopeful birth of a brand new nation, insisting on talking about debt, and war, and default, and loan sharks. And he says, in effect, I know. If you truly believe America is going to be different, blessed, floating serenely above all the troubles that have wrecked every other nation in history, then none of this will worry you. He calls that lovely dream the halcyon scenes of the poetic or fabulous age. The fairy tale age. The golden age that somehow never actually arrives.

But if you believe, he says, that America is going to get the normal human allotment, its ordinary share of the wars and disasters and hard years that have come to every people who has ever lived, then you had better build a government that can survive them. And survival, in the end, runs on money. Not on parchment promises. Not on the goodwill of thirteen separate state treasuries. It runs on a real, dependable, grown up power to fund itself.

And here is the part of the story Hamilton could not fit into the essay, because it had not happened yet. He won this argument. The Constitution was ratified, the new government got its power to tax, and a couple of years later this same man walked into the Treasury and inherited the exact wreck he had spent this paper describing, a young country buried under war debt that its own money had declared worthless. And he did the thing the essay quietly promises can be done. He used the new power to tax as a foundation, and on top of it he built a plan to honor that debt, in full, rather than walk away from it. People who had long since written those debts off as dead paper were paid what they had been promised. And in a remarkably short time, the credit of the United States, the very thing that had been approaching annihilation, turned into something that lenders across the Atlantic would actually trust again. The argument in Number Thirty was never just an argument. Within a few years its author got to run the experiment for real, and it worked.

That is the whole case, and it is worth holding it in one piece. Money is the blood of the body politic. Cut off the honest supply, and the body does not stay sweet and innocent and small. It either starts to plunder, or it starts to die. The power to tax is not really the government reaching into your pocket. It is the government being alive enough to keep the lights on, pay the soldiers, honor the debt, and borrow what it needs on the day the country's life depends on it.

You might be feeling, right about now, a small knot of worry. Because Hamilton has spent this whole paper insisting that the government's power to raise money should be able to grow without any fixed ceiling on it. And a reasonable person hears that and thinks, but that is exactly the thing I am afraid of. If there is no line, what on earth stops it? What stops a government that is allowed to tax anything from deciding, one day, to tax everything? That is the honest, serious objection, and to his credit Hamilton does not pretend it away or call the people raising it fools. He simply does not answer it here. He has used Number Thirty to prove that the power has to be large. The separate question, of what keeps a large power from swelling into a limitless one, is the entire subject of the paper that comes next.

Now step back and see where we are in the larger argument. For a long run of papers, Hamilton talked about force. The sword. The army, the navy, the militia. He has now turned to the purse. And you can already feel that these are, secretly, the same subject. An army you cannot pay for is just a wish. A navy with no treasury behind it is a row of very expensive boats. The power to defend the country and the power to fund the country are one single power, wearing two different coats. In this paper he has established that the power has to exist, and has to be broad. What he has not yet done is answer the deepest fear that a broad taxing power raises, the fear that a government you allow to tax at all will simply tax without end, and swallow the states whole. That fight is coming.

This has been The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Thirty. Next time: Federalist Number Thirty One, still Alexander Hamilton, still on the power to tax, this time taking the great fear head on, the worry that a government you let reach into the country's pockets even once will never, ever take its hand back out.
