At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had just finished naming the concrete things that separate American confederacies would fight about. Not abstractions. A list you could see from your front porch. Territory, with overlapping colonial claims to the western lands and live boundary quarrels like the Wyoming Valley. Commerce, with New York taxing the goods that flowed through its harbor and its neighbors paying the bill. Public debt, because there is nothing men differ so readily about as the payment of money. State laws that tore up private contracts, the way Rhode Island's paper money had. And foreign entanglement, each confederacy reaching for a European patron and walking into the labyrinth.

That was Federalist Number Seven. It closed with a cold little phrase. A war, not of parchment, but of the sword. Hamilton's point was that a shared legal framework is the thing that keeps ordinary economic friction from turning into ordinary military friction. Take the framework away, and you are left with the sword.

Today, Hamilton picks up exactly where that sentence left him. Federalist Number Eight, published in the New York Packet on November twentieth, seventeen eighty-seven. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. And the question he asks is the natural next one. All right. Suppose the confederacies do reach for the sword. Suppose they fight. What does living that way actually do to a free people, over time?

His answer is the most unsettling thing he has said so far. And it is not really about war at all. It is about what the fear of war does to you on the inside.

Let me give you the destination first, because Hamilton buries it in the middle of the essay and I think it belongs at the front. Somewhere in the heart of this paper there is a single sentence that the whole thing is built to deliver. He writes that to be more safe, nations at length become willing to run the risk of being less free.

Sit with that for a second. To be more safe, they become willing to run the risk of being less free. That is the argument of Federalist Number Eight in eleven words. Hamilton is not warning that disunion would be bloody, though he thinks it would be. He is warning that disunion would be corrosive. That a divided America, living in permanent insecurity, would slowly, almost willingly, trade away the very liberty it had just fought a revolution to win. Not because anyone conquered it. Because being afraid all the time changes what a people is willing to accept.

And the thing it would accept, the institution at the center of this whole paper, is one that carried enormous weight for Hamilton's readers. A standing army. A permanent, professional military force kept on foot in peacetime. To us that phrase is neutral, almost boring. Every modern country has one. To an American in seventeen eighty-seven, the words standing army were close to a curse. And to understand why this essay landed the way it did, you have to understand the century of English history sitting behind those two words.

So let me slow down and tell that story, because Hamilton's readers carried it in their bones and most of us no longer do.

Go back to England in the sixteen sixties. The English monarchy has just been restored after a civil war and a brief experiment with a republic. Charles the Second is on the throne. And Charles begins keeping a small permanent force of paid soldiers around the crown, even in peacetime. To many in Parliament this was alarming. England's tradition was a militia, ordinary subjects who took up arms when needed and went home after. A king with his own permanent troops was a king who did not have to ask anyone for anything.

Then it gets sharper. Charles dies, and his brother takes the throne as James the Second. James is openly Catholic in a fiercely Protestant country, and he is determined to rule on his own terms. He expands that peacetime army to something like twenty thousand men, and he camps a good part of it just outside London, at Hounslow Heath, where the citizens of the capital could see it. Everyone understood the message. The army was not there to repel a foreign invasion. It was there to remind the city who was in charge. James used his military commissions to advance loyalists, leaned on the courts, and pushed aside Parliament. To his opponents it looked like a free country being slowly bent into an unfree one, and the standing army was the spine of the whole project.

It ended in sixteen eighty-eight, in what the English called the Glorious Revolution. James's own officers and subjects abandoned him, he fled to France, and Parliament handed the crown to William and Mary on new terms. And one of those terms, written into the English Bill of Rights the next year, was explicit. The king could not keep a standing army in peacetime without the consent of Parliament. The English had looked at a peacetime army under the direct control of the executive, and they had concluded, from hard experience, that it was a loaded gun pointed at their own liberty.

That is the memory an American in seventeen eighty-seven inherited. It is less than a hundred years old to them. It is closer to them than the Second World War is to us. They had also just fought their own war against a king who had quartered troops among them and used soldiers to enforce his will. So when Hamilton writes the words standing army, he is not introducing a new idea to his readers. He is touching a bruise. And he knows it. In fact, the people arguing against the Constitution were already saying that the new document did not clearly forbid a standing army, and that this alone was reason to reject it.

Hamilton's move in this paper is clever, and it is the spine of the whole argument, so let me lay it out plainly.

He does not tell his frightened readers that standing armies are fine. He grants them the fear. He agrees the bruise is real. And then he tells them they are pointing at the wrong cause. The thing that produces standing armies, he argues, is not a constitution. It is insecurity. It is living next to enemies. A people that feels safe will let its guard down and keep its soldiers few, because nobody wants the expense or the danger of a large peacetime army if they do not feel they need one. But a people that feels constantly threatened will build that army themselves, willingly, and ask for it by name.

So the real question, Hamilton says, is not which piece of paper you adopt. It is which situation you choose to live in. Stay united, and you stay safe, and a safe people does not reach for the engines of despotism. Break apart into two or three jealous confederacies sharing open land borders, and you have manufactured permanent insecurity, and permanent insecurity will hand you a standing army whether your constitution mentions one or not.

Watch how he says the weaker states would get there first. A small confederacy, outmatched by a larger neighbor, cannot win a contest of raw population and resources. So it does the only thing it can. It compensates with discipline. It builds fortifications, drills professional troops, and makes itself militarily efficient to survive. And here is the part Hamilton wants his readers to feel in their stomachs. He says that in doing this, the weaker confederacy would be necessitated to strengthen the executive arm of government, and that its constitution would, in his phrase, acquire a progressive direction toward monarchy.

A progressive direction toward monarchy. He means a slow drift, not a coup. And then he gives the reason in one clean, general line. It is of the nature of war, he writes, to increase the executive at the expense of the legislative authority.

That sentence is worth holding onto, because it is doing a lot of work. War, and the constant preparation for war, always feeds the executive. Why? Because legislatures are built to deliberate, to argue, to move slowly, to represent many competing interests. That is their virtue. But it is useless in an emergency. When the enemy may be across the open border tomorrow, you want one hand on the wheel, deciding fast. So power flows, naturally, almost gravitationally, toward the single figure who can act. Do that once, in one crisis, and it is temporary. Do it for decades, under constant alarm, and the temporary becomes the permanent shape of the government. The legislature is still there. It just matters less every year.

Hamilton anticipates a clever objection here, and it is worth following, because his answer tells you something about why he thinks his own century is dangerous in a new way. Someone could push back and say, wait, the ancient Greek republics fought each other constantly, for centuries, and they never developed these permanent professional armies. So why would separate American confederacies? Hamilton's reply is that the modern world has changed the machinery of war itself. The ancient Greeks were, in his phrase, a nation of soldiers. The citizen and the fighter were the same person. But a modern people, he says, is absorbed in the pursuits of gain, in farming and trade and industry. Modern citizens do not want to be soldiers. They want to be left alone to make a living. And so the modern state, when it needs force, hires it. It raises a separate professional class of disciplined troops, paid for out of the new science of public finance, kept apart from the body of ordinary citizens. That separateness is exactly the danger. An army that is the whole people cannot easily be turned against the people. An army that is a distinct, permanent, professional caste can be. Hamilton is telling his readers that the eighteenth century has invented a more efficient army and, in the same motion, a more dangerous one.

Now you might be wondering whether this is really inevitable, or just Hamilton being gloomy. Couldn't a disciplined small republic arm itself for defense and simply stay a republic? Hamilton's answer is that it is not one country's choice to make, because the process does not stop with one country. The weaker confederacy arms up and, for a while, gains an edge. Hamilton points out, accurately, that small states under vigorous governments with disciplined armies have often beaten larger, lazier neighbors. But the larger confederacy is not going to sit and accept being humiliated by a smaller rival. Its pride will not allow it, and neither will its fear. So it builds its own army to reinstate itself. And now the first one has to build further. Each step is a reasonable response to the last step. And the sum of all those reasonable steps, Hamilton says, is that we should in a little time see established in every part of this country the same engines of despotism which have been the scourge of the Old World.

That is the arms race, described in seventeen eighty-seven. Nobody in it is a villain. Everybody is just being prudent. And the collective result is that the whole continent ends up garrisoned, taxed for its armies, and governed by swelling executives. The Old World's disease, transplanted to America by Americans, because they chose to live as rivals.

At this point a fair question is whether any of this still touches us. We are one country. We did stay united. Does Hamilton's warning have anything to say to a nation that took his advice?

I think it does, and the clearest way to hear it is through a voice much closer to our own time. In January of nineteen sixty-one, a departing American president gave a farewell address to the country. The president was Dwight Eisenhower. And Eisenhower was not a peace activist or an outsider. He had been the supreme allied commander in Europe in the Second World War, a five-star general, a man who had spent his life inside the military. Which is exactly what gives his warning its weight.

Eisenhower told the country that the United States had been compelled, by the Cold War, to do something it had never done in its history. It had built a permanent armaments industry of vast proportions, and a standing military establishment of millions of people, and it had kept them on foot continuously, year after year, in what was nominally peacetime. He said the country now had no choice about this. The danger was real and the establishment was necessary. But, he said, the nation had to understand its implications. And then he gave the thing its name. He warned against the acquisition of unwarranted influence by what he called the military-industrial complex, and he said the potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist.

Listen to how closely that tracks Hamilton. Eisenhower is describing precisely the mechanism of Federalist Number Eight. Constant external danger. The constant danger compelling a permanent military establishment. The establishment, once permanent, developing its own weight, its own influence, its own gravitational pull on the government that maintains it. The soldier, in Hamilton's words, rising in importance, and the civilian world quietly reorganizing itself around the military's needs. Hamilton predicted the dynamic in the abstract. Eisenhower, two centuries later, stood inside the fully grown version of it and told the country to keep its eyes open.

And Hamilton is very precise about the human side of this, because he thinks the danger is finally a matter of habit, of what people get used to. In a country that rarely fears invasion, he says, the soldier is a minor figure. The citizen does not look up to him for protection, and does not fear him either. The citizen regards the small army as a necessary evil to be watched warily, and stands ready to resist it if it ever oversteps. But in a country living under perpetual menace, the whole relationship inverts. The continual need for the soldier raises the soldier's importance and, in the same motion, lowers the citizen's. People who live where war is a constant fact of life have their rights infringed so routinely that their very sense of having rights begins to wear thin. And then comes Hamilton's quiet, chilling progression. By degrees, he says, the people come to see the soldiery first as their protectors, and then as their superiors, and the step from there to seeing them as their masters is neither remote nor difficult. Nobody decides to be ruled by the army. They simply get used to it, one small deference at a time, until resistance no longer feels possible.

And notice what neither man is saying. Neither one is saying the army is the enemy, or that you can simply abolish it. Eisenhower built his and defended its necessity. Hamilton is about to argue that a sensibly governed union needs only a small one. The warning in both cases is subtler than abolition. It is that a permanent military establishment is a standing temptation and a standing weight, and a free people has to know that and watch it on purpose, forever, because it will not watch itself.

So why, in Hamilton's account, does a country end up exposed to that danger in the first place, or spared from it? Here he reaches for the example his readers knew best, and it is the heart of the essay, so let me give it room.

Picture Great Britain. By Hamilton's day Britain is a free country, by the standards of the eighteenth century. It has a Parliament with real power. It has rights its subjects can name and defend. And Hamilton asks a question that sounds almost like idle curiosity but is not. Why did Britain manage to stay that free, when the great powers across the Channel did not?

His answer is one word. Geography. Britain is an island. It is ringed by water and guarded by a powerful navy, and that fact, all by itself, means Britain almost never has to worry about an army marching across its border, because it has no land border for an army to march across. So Britain never needed a large peacetime army at home. A modest force, enough to hold off a surprise landing until the militia could gather, was all national policy required, and Hamilton adds, all that public opinion would have tolerated.

Then he runs the counterfactual, and this is the move that makes the paper. He asks his readers to imagine Britain picked up and set down on the continent of Europe. Same people. Same Parliament. Same laws and traditions and love of liberty. The only thing changed is the address. Now Britain has hostile great powers on its land borders, France and the rest, each with a huge standing army. What happens?

Hamilton's answer is blunt. Britain would have been forced to match them. It would have had to make its military establishment at home as large as its neighbors', because it would have no choice, the same insecurity would press on it that presses on every continental state. And once that large permanent army existed, the slow drift would begin, the executive would swell, the civil power would shrink beside the military one, and Britain, Hamilton says, would in all probability be, in his words, at this day a victim to the absolute power of a single man.

Think about what he has just done. He has taken the freest country his readers knew, and argued that its freedom is not really a triumph of superior English character or superior English institutions. It is substantially a gift of the sea. The water did for British liberty what no constitution could have done on its own. It kept the country from ever needing the instrument that would have destroyed it. Britain stayed free, in large part, because Britain was hard to invade.

You might be wondering why Hamilton would say something so deflating about the people his own readers most admired. And the answer is that the deflation is the whole point, because it sets up his conclusion, and the conclusion is about America.

Because here is what Hamilton wants his readers to see. America, in seventeen eighty-seven, has a chance to be like Britain. Not an island in fact, but an island in effect. Europe is three thousand miles of ocean away. The European colonies that sit near America are too weak and scattered to be a serious standing threat. If America stays one country, then it has no hostile great power on its land borders at all, because it has no internal borders for a great power to be on. The whole continent, united, would enjoy what Hamilton calls an advantage similar to that of an insulated situation. The same gift the sea gave Britain, union would give America. A reason not to need a large army, and therefore a reason to stay free.

But, he says, break the country into two or three confederacies, and you have thrown that gift away with your own hands. You have drawn land borders across the continent where none existed. You have created, out of nothing, hostile neighbors with armies. You have put America in exactly the predicament of the continental powers of Europe. And then comes the line that ties the whole essay shut. Our liberties, Hamilton writes, would be a prey to the means of defending ourselves against the ambition and jealousy of each other.

Our liberties would be a prey to the means of defending ourselves. The means of defending ourselves. He is saying the armies would not even have to be turned on us by some tyrant for us to lose our freedom. The simple fact of needing them, maintaining them, living under them, paying for them, deferring to them, year after year, would do the work. We would not be conquered. We would be worn down. Safety from external danger, he writes, is the most powerful director of national conduct, and even the ardent love of liberty will, after a time, give way to its dictates.

Now I have saved one piece of the paper for here on purpose, because Hamilton actually opens with it, and I think it lands better as the texture underneath everything else rather than as the headline. Hamilton spends his first pages painting what war between the American confederacies would physically look like, and his point is that it would be uniquely terrible.

In Europe, he says, war had become almost slow. Every country bristled with stone fortifications, chains of fortified places that an invader had to reduce one by one. Campaigns got swallowed up just capturing two or three frontier garrisons. The disciplined professional armies, for all that they menaced liberty and drained the treasury, had at least made sudden conquest nearly impossible. European war, Hamilton says, had become a history of towns taken and retaken, of battles that decide nothing, of retreats more beneficial than victories, of much effort and little acquisition.

America would be the reverse. No chains of fortresses. Open frontiers, one state's territory lying exposed to the next. A jealous distrust of military establishments would make the confederacies put off building proper defenses as long as they possibly could. So the populous states would simply overrun their smaller neighbors. Conquest would be easy, and just as easily lost again. War here, Hamilton says, would be desultory and predatory. Plunder and devastation would march with it. And the thing that would fill the history books would not be grand decisive campaigns. It would be the calamities of individuals. Ordinary people, burned out and robbed, in a kind of war that never quite resolves and never quite stops.

And here is why I think that picture belongs underneath the argument rather than on top of it. That constant, grinding, unresolved insecurity is the engine. That is the state of permanent alarm that makes a people reach for the standing army in the first place. The predatory war is not a separate horror from the loss of liberty. It is the cause of it. The fear comes first. The trade of freedom for safety comes second. Hamilton even concedes, with real candor, that his ugly picture of plunder and devastation would not stay accurate for long, and he is not walking the warning back when he says that. He is sharpening it. The picture changes precisely because the confederacies would not tolerate that chaos. They would build the armies, the forts, the disciplined troops. They would impose order. And the order would cost them the very thing the order was supposed to protect.

So you might ask, finally, what Hamilton actually wants from his reader at the end of all this. And he is unusually direct about it. He says this is not some superficial or futile idea. It is solid and weighty, and it deserves the serious and mature consideration of every honest man of whatever party. He asks his readers to make what he calls a firm and solemn pause. To stop, and really sit with this one thought, and trace it all the way to its consequences. And if they do that, he says, they will stop fussing over trivial objections to the Constitution, the small complaints, the airy phantoms, as he calls them, that flit before the imaginations of its adversaries. Because next to the real danger, the substantial danger of a divided continent slowly arming away its own freedom, those small objections simply shrink.

That is the close. And it tells you something about how Hamilton understands the whole stakes of ratification. He is not selling the Constitution as a perfect document. He is asking his readers to weigh it against the actual alternative. And the actual alternative, in his account, is not a peaceful America of friendly little republics. It is a continent that has rebuilt the Old World's worst machinery on new soil, and called it self-defense.

It is worth stepping back to see where Hamilton has brought us across his three papers. In Federalist Number Six he argued that a divided America would fight itself, because that is what neighboring states do. In Number Seven he got specific, and named exactly what they would fight over. And now in Number Eight he has followed the argument to its final and darkest room. It is not enough to say disunion means war. Disunion means war, and war means fear, and fear means standing armies and a swelling executive, and that machinery, kept running for long enough, means the slow death of the liberty the whole Revolution was fought to secure. The cost of disunion is not just blood and money. The cost is freedom itself.

Next time, Hamilton turns from the danger to the cure. Federalist Number Nine is still his, and it answers an objection that had haunted republics for two thousand years. Critics of the Constitution kept pointing at the small republics of the ancient world, at Greece and Italy, and saying, look, republics are fragile, they tear themselves apart with faction and instability, they always have. Hamilton's answer is that the science of politics has actually moved forward, and that a well-built union is not the thing that endangers a republic. It is the thing that finally lets a republic survive its own internal storms. The argument is about to turn from what we have to fear to what we can build.

This has been The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Eight. Next time: Federalist Number Nine, still Alexander Hamilton, on union as the cure for the instability that destroyed the small republics of the past.
