# Federalist No. 33 Explained: The Clause That Added Nothing

## Episode Description
Article One, Section Eight lists everything Congress is allowed to do, and it is four hundred and twenty eight words long. Out loud, that takes three minutes. The laws Congress has actually made fill fifty four volumes of the United States Code. Bridging that gap is one sentence at the bottom of the list, thirty nine words, which its enemies called the sweeping clause and which Hamilton, defending it, called harmless. This episode is about how he could possibly mean that. Along the way: a Philadelphia committee that slipped the most powerful sentence in American law past a room full of arguing delegates without a single objection, the future Antifederalist who proposed the Supremacy Clause himself, a report to Congress in 1786 showing the United States had not kept its word for a single day, and the February morning in 1791 when Washington had to decide which of two cabinet secretaries was right about the meaning of one word.

## Short Episode Description
The most feared sentence in the Constitution passed the Convention without one word of debate, and the man defending it in print called it harmless. He was telling the truth, and that turned out to be the problem.

## Alternate Short Description
Congress may do about four hundred things according to the Constitution and about four million things in practice, and this is the episode about the thirty nine words in between.
