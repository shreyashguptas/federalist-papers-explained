At the end of the last episode, the tour finally ended. For the last three papers, Publius had been walking us through a graveyard, pointing at the bones of one dead confederacy after another. Ancient Greece. The Holy Roman Empire. The Swiss cantons. And last time, the one Americans loved most, the Dutch republic, rich and free and rotting from the very same flaw as all the rest. A union so weak it collected its own dues at the point of a bayonet, and survived only on the strength of a hereditary prince, even as a foreign army marched through its streets.

And out of all that wreckage came a single verdict. A government that rules over other governments, instead of over people, can enforce itself only with the sword, never with the quiet machinery of ordinary law. The cure, the one thing every one of those dead confederacies lacked, was not a sharper sword. It was a government whose laws would reach past the member states and land directly on the individual citizen, so that being obeyed would never again require making war on a member to get it.

That was Federalist Number Twenty. And with it, the tour of strangers was over. The graveyard had been walked end to end.

Today, Hamilton turns the lamp around. He stops pointing across the ocean and back across the centuries, and he aims it squarely at us. Because there is a patient he has not yet examined. The one sitting right there in the room. The actual, living government that the thirteen states were struggling along under at that very moment, in the winter of seventeen eighty-seven. The Articles of Confederation.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-One. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. Published in the Independent Journal on December twelfth, seventeen eighty-seven. And it opens the way a doctor opens an examination.

Hamilton tells his readers, in almost these exact words, that before you can settle on the right remedy, you first have to be well acquainted with the extent and the malignity of the disease. The disease. That is the word he reaches for. The dead confederacies were the autopsies of strangers. Now he is going to diagnose the patient in the room. And over the next couple of papers, he turns from the failures of other people's governments and starts naming, one by one, the specific, concrete, embarrassing things that were broken in America's own. He has circled this target before, when he argued that the deepest flaw of the Confederation was the whole idea of governing states instead of people. But now he gets specific. He stops describing the disease in general terms and starts listing its symptoms by name.

Today he names three of them. So let me walk through them the way he does, as a doctor listing symptoms. First, a government that cannot enforce its own laws. Second, a government that cannot protect its own members from being overthrown. And third, a government that cannot even raise money fairly. Three defects. Each one, on its own, would be serious. Together, they describe a government that is barely a government at all.

Let me start with the first, because it is the one that sounds almost impossible when you say it plainly.

The first defect is that the government of the United States, as it existed in seventeen eighty-seven, had no power to make anyone obey it.

Sit with that for a second, because it is easy to skim past. We are not talking about a government that was weak, or slow, or underfunded. We are talking about a government that, by its own design, had no legal way to compel obedience to a single thing it decided. Congress could meet. Congress could debate. Congress could pass a resolution. And then, if a state simply ignored that resolution, there was nothing, constitutionally, that Congress could do about it. It could not fine the state. It could not suspend the state's privileges. It could not send anyone to make the state comply. It could only ask again, and hope.

Hamilton calls this the total want of a sanction to the laws. A sanction, in the older sense he is using, is the teeth behind a rule. The penalty that makes a law a law and not just a suggestion. And the Confederation had none. He puts it in a line that should stop you cold. He says the United States offer the extraordinary spectacle of a government, and here are his words, "destitute even of the shadow of constitutional power to enforce the execution of its own laws."

Not lacking the power. Lacking even the shadow of the power. A government that cannot enforce its own laws is, in the most basic sense, not really governing. It is making requests.

At this point you might be wondering: wasn't that the whole point? Didn't the states want it that way? And the answer is yes. That is exactly the trap Hamilton is about to spring. Because the people arguing against the new Constitution, the people who liked the Articles just fine, had a favorite clause they loved to quote. It was the part of the Articles that said each state keeps every power, every right, every scrap of authority that was not expressly handed over to the central government. The opponents of the Constitution adored that clause. They quoted it constantly. It was, to them, the great protection of state freedom.

And Hamilton, very coolly, points out what that beloved clause actually means in practice. It means there is no wiggle room. You cannot argue that the power to enforce the laws is implied, or assumed, or hidden in the nature of the agreement, because the states' own favorite clause slams that door shut. If a power was not expressly written down and handed over, the central government does not have it. And the power to make a delinquent state comply was never expressly written down. So it does not exist. The very clause the Anti-Federalists were celebrating as the guardian of liberty was, at the same time, the clause guaranteeing that the national government would stay permanently toothless.

And then Hamilton lands the point that ties this whole paper back to the five that came before it. He says that in this one respect, America stands apart from every other arrangement of its kind in the world. He calls it a new and unexampled phenomenon in the political world. Think about what that means coming from him. He has just spent five papers in the graveyard. He has shown us the broken confederacies of ancient Greece, the paralyzed Holy Roman Empire, the bayonet-collecting Dutch. And his point here is that even those failures, even those disasters, at least claimed the power to coerce their members. America had built something worse than the worst of them. A union that did not even pretend to have teeth.

For a modern listener, the closest thing might be the difference between a real government and a voluntary club. Imagine a neighborhood association where the bylaws say everyone owes dues, and they set the amount, and they send out the bills, and then, if you simply do not pay, there is no late fee, no lien, no court, nothing. Just another politely worded letter next month. That is not really a governing body. It is a group of people hoping their neighbors are decent. Or think of an international body that passes a resolution condemning something, and everyone nods, and then nothing happens, because no one actually has to obey it. That was the United States government under the Articles. A standing resolution machine with no power to make the resolutions stick.

That is the first defect. No teeth.

The second defect is harder to see at first, but in some ways it is the one that scared people the most in the winter of seventeen eighty-seven. And it is this. The union had no power to protect a state's own government from being overthrown from the inside.

Here is what Hamilton means. Under the Articles, if a foreign country invaded a state, the union was at least supposed to come to its defense. But if the danger came from within, if a faction inside a state rose up, seized power by force, and erected a tyranny on the ruins of the lawful government, the national government could do nothing. It had no authority to step in. Hamilton describes it almost helplessly. He says that in such a case the national government could legally do nothing more than behold the encroachment with indignation and regret. Just watch. Just stand at the fence and disapprove, while inside one of its own member states, a mob or a strongman tore down a republic and put something uglier in its place.

And the reason this terrified people in seventeen eighty-seven is that they had just watched something very close to it happen. Hamilton does not have to invent a hypothetical here. He just points out the window. He writes about "the tempestuous situation from which Massachusetts has scarcely emerged."

Now, we have actually met this story before, back in Episode Six. But it is worth telling properly now, because here it is doing different work. The tempestuous situation Hamilton means is Shays' Rebellion.

Daniel Shays was a farmer in western Massachusetts and a former captain in the Continental Army. He had fought in the Revolution. And like a lot of veterans and small farmers after the war, he came home to a financial nightmare. The economy was in ruins. Hard money was scarce. And the state government in Boston, run largely by the coastal merchant class, was demanding taxes be paid in that scarce hard currency, while the courts were aggressively seizing the farms of people who could not pay. Picture a man who marched off to fight for independence, came home, and found the new government he had fought for was about to take his land for debt. Now multiply him by thousands.

In late seventeen eighty-six and into early seventeen eighty-seven, those farmers stopped petitioning and picked up muskets. They marched on the courthouses to physically stop the foreclosure proceedings. And then they marched on the federal arsenal at Springfield, where the weapons were. The state had no real army to send. The rebellion was finally put down by a militia that had to be privately funded, paid for largely by frightened merchants in Boston, because the government itself could not muster the force. The uprising broke apart in the snow in the early weeks of seventeen eighty-seven.

But here is the thing. It terrified the entire country's political class. Because if one state could be thrown into open armed revolt by a few thousand desperate farmers, and could barely scrape together the means to stop it, what did that say about the strength of the whole system? And, crucially for Hamilton's argument, where was the union in all this? The answer is nowhere. The union had no power to help. Massachusetts was on its own.

And now Hamilton adds the detail that turns a scary story into a genuinely chilling one. He asks his readers to imagine how it might have gone differently. The men who led Shays' Rebellion were not, in the end, would-be kings. They were broke farmers who wanted relief, not a throne. But Hamilton asks: what if they had not been? In one of the most haunting lines in the paper, he asks who can say what the outcome of those convulsions would have been, "if the malcontents had been headed by a Caesar or by a Cromwell."

A Caesar or a Cromwell. He is reaching for the two most famous examples in all of Western history of exactly this nightmare. Julius Caesar took the discontent and the armies of a Roman republic and rode them straight into one-man rule, dealing the blow the old republic never recovered from. Oliver Cromwell rose out of the English Civil War, a popular cause against the king, and ended up as Lord Protector, a military dictator in everything but name. Both of them are the same warning. A popular uprising, by itself, is just angry people. But put an ambitious, capable, ruthless man at the head of it, and that same uprising becomes the staircase he climbs to absolute power.

So Hamilton's point is this. Shays' Rebellion happened to be led by desperate men with no grand design. We got lucky. But the next one might not be. And if a Caesar had taken western Massachusetts and turned it into his personal tyranny, the government of the United States could have done precisely nothing. It would have had to stand and watch a tyranny rise inside its own borders. And, Hamilton adds, a tyranny that takes root in one state does not stay politely inside that state's lines. He asks what a despotism established in Massachusetts would do to the liberties of New Hampshire, of Connecticut, of New York. A captured state is a loaded gun pointed at every state next to it.

So Hamilton's fix is that the union ought to guarantee each state's government, ought to promise to step in and protect a lawful state government against being overthrown by force.

And here a fair objection comes up, one Hamilton raises himself, because he knows his readers are proud of their states. You might be thinking: isn't that just a license for the national government to meddle? To stick its nose into a state's private business and override what the locals want? If the federal government can step in whenever it decides a state's politics have gone wrong, haven't you just invited the bully in?

Hamilton's answer is careful and it matters. He says no, because the guarantee would only ever operate against change brought about by violence. It would not touch the people's right to reform their own government peacefully. If the citizens of a state want to throw the bums out, rewrite their constitution, change everything, by lawful and peaceable means, that right stays completely untouched. He puts it in a lovely, simple line. He says, "The natural cure for an ill-administration, in a popular or representative constitution, is a change of men."

A change of men. Meaning: in a free country, when the government is bad, the remedy is an election. You vote the rascals out. You do not need muskets, you need ballots. The federal guarantee would never stand in the way of that. It would only stand in the way of the other thing. The mob, the faction, the strongman seizing power by force. The guarantee is aimed at the coup, never at the vote.

And here is the satisfying part, the payoff that Hamilton's readers could not have known yet. This complaint of his, this defect he is naming, got fixed. It is right there in the finished Constitution. Article Four contains what we now call the Guarantee Clause, a promise by the United States to every single state of a republican form of government, and protection against invasion and against domestic violence. The thing Hamilton is standing here in seventeen eighty-seven saying is missing, the new document put in writing. You are watching a defect get diagnosed about a year before the cure goes into the founding law of the country.

That is the second defect. No power to protect its own members from being toppled.

Which brings us to the third, and this is the one where Hamilton stops being only a critic and starts, just barely, to show you the better way. The third defect is about money. Specifically, how the old government tried to pay for itself. And the system it used was called requisition by quota.

Here is how it worked, in plain terms. The national government had bills to pay, war debts mostly, enormous ones. But remember the first defect: it could not tax anybody directly, and it could not force anyone to do anything. So instead, it would add up what it needed, and then it would split that total among the thirteen states, assigning each state a share, a quota, of the national bill. Virginia owes this much. Connecticut owes that much. And then it would mail out the requests and wait. We already know how well the waiting worked.

But Hamilton, in this paper, sets the enforcement problem aside for a moment, because he wants to attack something deeper. Even if you could somehow force the states to pay their quotas, he says, the whole idea of a quota is rotten at its root. Because the quotas were figured out using land. The value of a state's land, or sometimes the number of its people, was supposed to measure how much that state could afford to pay.

And Hamilton's argument is that this is simply not how wealth works. There is, he says, no common standard, no barometer, by which you can measure how rich one place is against another. You cannot read a nation's wealth off the size of its territory or the headcount of its population. It does not work.

And to prove it, he reaches for an example his readers had just spent a whole essay thinking about. He compares the United Netherlands, the Dutch republic, with giant countries like Russia, or Germany, or France. Now, we just spent all of last episode inside the Dutch republic, so this lands with real force. The Netherlands was tiny. A small, crowded, waterlogged patch of land with a modest population. Russia and France were enormous, with vast territories and huge populations. By the land-and-people measure, the Dutch should have been poor and the giants should have been rich. And it was exactly backwards. That tiny waterlogged republic was one of the wealthiest places on earth, while the immense empires, acre for acre and head for head, could not touch it. Land did not predict wealth. Population did not predict wealth. The little rich country and the giant poorer ones proved it.

And then Hamilton brings it home, all the way down to his own backyard, because he wants no one to dismiss this as a quirk of faraway nations. Take New York, he says. Look at King's County, the small, dense district at the mouth of the harbor that we would today call Brooklyn. And compare it to Montgomery County, a huge sprawling stretch of frontier land upstate. By acres, Montgomery dwarfs Brooklyn. But the active, working, circulating wealth packed into tiny King's County was far greater than its size or its land value would ever suggest. A small place humming with commerce can be worth more than a vast place that is merely big.

So where does wealth actually come from, if not from land and bodies? Hamilton gives a kind of list, and it is worth hearing because it is genuinely modern. Wealth, he says, depends on an almost infinite variety of causes. The location of a place. Its soil and its climate. What it produces. The nature of its government. The character and the energy and the knowledge of its people. The state of its trade, its crafts, its industry. Pile all of those together, in combinations too tangled to ever measure cleanly, and you get differences in riches between places that no simple ruler could ever capture.

And if there is no honest way to measure how rich a state is, then there is no honest way to set its quota. Which means every quota system, no matter how you draw it up, is going to land unfairly. Some states will be crushed by a share far heavier than they can carry, while others barely feel the weight at all. Hamilton says this kind of arrangement cannot fail to produce glaring inequality and extreme oppression. And he makes a striking prediction: this unfairness, all by itself, would eventually tear the union apart, because the states getting crushed would simply refuse, in the end, to stay in a club that was bleeding them while their neighbors skated.

So now Hamilton has a problem to solve. The old way of funding the government, splitting a bill by quotas measured in land, is both unenforceable and unfair. So what is the alternative? And this is where, for the first time in a long while, Publius stops describing a corpse and starts sketching something that actually works.

The answer, Hamilton says, is to let the national government raise its own money, in its own way. And the particular way he has in mind is taxes on consumption. Taxes on things people buy. Import duties, excise taxes, that whole family of taxes that get folded into the price of goods. And he describes them with one of the loveliest images in the paper. Taxes on what people consume, he says, "may be compared to a fluid, which will, in time, find its level with the means of paying them."

A fluid finding its level. Think about what that picture is doing. Water poured into a container does not pile up in one corner; it spreads out and settles to its natural level on its own. Hamilton is saying a consumption tax behaves the same way. It naturally settles to what people can actually afford to pay, because it is tied to what they actually choose to buy. The rich man who buys a great deal pays a great deal. The frugal man who buys little pays little. Nobody mails you a bill for a fixed share you cannot meet. In a real sense, how much you pay is partly up to you.

And then Hamilton gives us the single most delightful idea in the entire essay. It is a little counterintuitive, so let me set it up. With a normal bill, if you want twice the money, you charge twice as much. But Hamilton points out that a consumption tax does not obey that arithmetic. He quotes a witty old saying, that "in political arithmetic, two and two do not always make four."

Two and two do not always make four. Here is what he means, and it is genuinely clever. Suppose you put a tax on some good, and it brings in a tidy sum. So you think, I will just double the tax and double my money. But when you double the tax, the price jumps, and people respond by buying less of the thing. Some stop buying it altogether. Some start smuggling it to dodge the tax. And so when the dust settles, you have collected less than you expected, sometimes even less than before you raised it. The tax, in other words, has a built-in ceiling. Push it past a certain point and it starts working against you. It defeats its own purpose.

And Hamilton sees something beautiful in that. He says this means a consumption tax contains, in its own nature, a security against excess. It polices itself. A government cannot use this kind of tax to crush its people, because the moment it tries to squeeze too hard, the revenue dries up and the squeezing stops paying. He calls it a complete barrier against any serious oppression of the citizens by taxes of this kind. The limit is not something a kindly ruler has to choose to respect. The limit is baked into how the tax works.

If that idea sounds familiar, it should. This is, two hundred years early, the basic intuition behind a lot of modern tax debate, the recognition that raising a rate does not automatically raise the take, because people change their behavior in response. It is closely related to the logic behind so-called sin taxes, where part of the goal of taxing cigarettes heavily is precisely that people buy fewer cigarettes. The tax and the behavior are linked. Hamilton saw that link clearly in seventeen eighty-seven, and he saw that the link itself was a kind of protection for the ordinary citizen. A tax that punishes its own excess is a tax you can hand to a government without quite as much fear.

So look at what Hamilton has built across this one short paper. He started as a doctor, telling us we have to understand the disease before we reach for a cure. And then he named the disease, in three parts. First, a government with no power to enforce its own laws, a government that can only ask. Second, a government with no power to protect its own member states from being overthrown by force, a government that can only watch. And third, a government that funds itself by an impossible measurement, splitting its bills by a yardstick of wealth that does not exist, producing unfairness severe enough to break the union on its own.

Three defects. And underneath all three is the same single sickness, the very one Hamilton has been chasing all the way through that graveyard of dead confederacies. It is a government that tries to act on states instead of on people. A government that asks a state to obey, instead of commanding a citizen. A government that can only beg a province for money, instead of collecting a tax from a person. That is the disease that killed every confederacy in the graveyard. And here it is again, alive and untreated, in America's own house.

But notice the turn at the very end, with that fluid finding its level, with that tax that polices itself. For the first time in a long stretch of these papers, Hamilton is not just naming what is broken. He is starting to point at the repair. He is saying the answer to a government that can only beg is a government that can reach past the states and touch the citizen directly, with a tax the citizen helps set by his own choices. The diagnosis is turning, slowly, into a prescription.

And he is not finished with the diagnosis yet. Federalist Number Twenty-Two is his as well, and it is the second half of this autopsy of the Articles. There he keeps the list going, and the next defects are big ones. The government's helplessness over trade, its complete inability to regulate commerce between the states and with the world. The deep unfairness of giving tiny Rhode Island the very same single vote as enormous Virginia, as though they were equals. And the maddening rule that let a small handful of states, by simply refusing to show up or vote yes, paralyze the entire country. If today was about a government that cannot enforce, protect, or pay, next time is about a government that cannot trade, cannot count its people fairly, and cannot get out of its own way.

This has been The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Twenty-One. Next time: Federalist Number Twenty-Two, still Alexander Hamilton, continuing the hard, honest inventory of everything that was broken in America's first government, and building, defect by defect, the case for replacing it.
