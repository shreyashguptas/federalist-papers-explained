At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had just sold you on the boldest move in the whole Constitution. The old government, the one dying under the Articles of Confederation, made its laws for states — and a law aimed at a state can only be enforced by an army, which means it can only be enforced by civil war. So the fix, Hamilton argued, is to stop aiming the law at states and start aiming it at people. Let the national government tax the individual citizen, judge the individual citizen, and reach him the way every working government reaches him — not through a soldier at the border, but through a judge in a courtroom. The majesty of the national authority, he said, would be manifested through the medium of the courts of justice.

That was Federalist Number Sixteen. And the moment Hamilton finished making that case, he handed his enemies a brand-new weapon.

Because think about what he just promised. A national government that can reach past your state and touch you directly. That can tax you, summon you, judge you, without asking your governor's permission. To a lot of Americans in seventeen eighty-seven, that did not sound like a fix. It sounded like a monster. If this new government can reach every single person in America on its own authority, what on earth stops it from growing, and growing, and growing, until there are no states left at all? Until the whole continent is run from one capital, and Virginia and New York and Connecticut are just lines on an old map?

That is the fear Federalist Number Seventeen exists to answer. And it is worth pausing on how strange Hamilton's position has become, because in the space of three days he has flipped to the opposite side of the table.

Federalist Number Seventeen was published in the Independent Journal on Wednesday, December fifth, seventeen eighty-seven — the day after Number Sixteen. Same author, Alexander Hamilton. Same pen name, Publius. Same New York readers trying to decide whether to ratify. But notice the turn he is making. For fifteen straight papers, the warning has been that the union is too weak. Too weak to pay its debts, too weak to enforce its treaties, too weak to keep the states from each other's throats. And now, for the first time, Hamilton turns around to face a completely different crowd — the people who are terrified the new government will be too strong. The people who look at his courts-and-taxes machine from last episode and see the beginnings of a tyranny.

So Hamilton has to argue both sides of the same coin in the same week. To one group: don't worry, it's strong enough to work. To the other group: don't worry, it's not so strong it'll swallow you. And the remarkable thing about Number Seventeen is that Hamilton does not just reassure the second group. He tells them, flatly, that they are afraid of the wrong thing. That they have got the danger exactly backwards.

Here is the sentence where he says it. It is the spine of the whole paper, so let me put it plainly.

"It will always be far more easy for the State governments to encroach upon the national authorities than for the national government to encroach upon the State authorities."

Sit with how counterintuitive that is. The objection on the table is that the big central government will bully and absorb the little state governments. And Hamilton says: no, you have it upside down. The pull runs the other way. It is the states that will lean on the union, crowd it, out-muscle it — not the union that will swallow the states. The thing you should actually worry about, he is telling these readers, is a federal government too feeble to do its job, getting steadily elbowed aside by thirteen powerful, beloved state governments that the people will back every time.

That is a startling thing to hear from Alexander Hamilton, of all people — the man history remembers as the great champion of central power. But he means it. And the rest of the paper is him proving it, in two moves. First, he argues that the federal government would never even want to swallow the states. And second, he argues that even if it wanted to, it could not — because the states hold something the union can never take: the affection of the people. Let me take those one at a time, because the second one is where this paper comes alive.

Start with the first. Why wouldn't an ambitious federal government want to grab the states' local powers?

Hamilton's answer is almost funny in its bluntness. He looks at the kinds of things a state government actually does — the day-to-day running of local life — and he says, in effect: why would anyone hungry for power want that job? It is boring. There is no glory in it.

He lists the things that genuinely tempt ambitious men. Commerce. Finance. Negotiation. And war. Those are the levers of real power — controlling trade, controlling money, dealing with foreign nations, raising armies. And every one of those, under the new Constitution, already belongs to the national government. That is where the action is. That is where a power-hungry man would want to be anyway.

And then look at what is left for the states. The regulation of what Hamilton calls the mere domestic police of a state. Now, a quick note there, because that word has shifted on us. When Hamilton says police, he does not mean cops in cars. In the eighteenth century, the police of a state meant the whole internal housekeeping of a community — the local ordinances, the upkeep of roads and markets, the supervision of agriculture, the ordinary regulation of ordinary daily life. The unglamorous machinery that keeps a place running. Settling a fence-line dispute between two farmers. Overseeing the planting. Running the county courthouse.

And Hamilton's point is that no one drunk on ambition lies awake at night dreaming of running the county courthouse. He says these local matters hold out slender allurements to ambition. Grabbing them, he says, would be as troublesome as it would be nugatory — nugatory being an old word for pointless, worthless. You would take on a mountain of tedious local administration and gain nothing in dignity, importance, or splendor for it. So the federal government simply has no motive to reach for it. Why would a man who already controls the army and the treasury fight you for the right to inspect your neighbor's livestock?

At this point you might be wondering whether that is a little too convenient. Surely power grabs are not always rational. Surely a government can reach for control just because it can, just out of appetite. And Hamilton, to his credit, grants you exactly that. Fine, he says — suppose pure greed for domination were enough to create the urge, with no sensible motive at all. Even then, the states would be safe. And this is where he makes his real argument, the one the whole paper is built on. Because it is not the federal government's lack of appetite that protects the states. It is the states' grip on the hearts of the people.

So why do the states have that grip? Hamilton gives two reasons, and the first is a piece of plain human psychology that you can test against your own life in about four seconds.

Here is how he puts it.

"It is a known fact in human nature, that its affections are commonly weak in proportion to the distance or diffusiveness of the object."

In plain English: the further away something is, the less you feel for it. People love what is close. Hamilton spells it out with a little ladder of attachment. A man cares more about his family than about his neighborhood. He cares more about his neighborhood than about the town. More about the town than about the whole sprawling country. Affection fades as the circle widens. It is strongest at the center, where you live, and it thins out the further you go.

Apply that to government, Hamilton says, and the conclusion is obvious. Your state government is close. It sits in a capital you could ride to. It is run by people you might actually know, dealing with matters you can see. The national government is far away and abstract — a distant body, handling distant affairs, that most people will never lay eyes on. So naturally the people will feel a stronger pull toward the government near them than toward the one over the horizon. The states start every contest with the home-field advantage, because they are home.

But Hamilton does not rest the argument on sentiment alone. He has a second reason, and it is the heart of the entire paper. He says the strong pull of the human heart toward the local would find a powerful ally in one specific thing the state governments do. One job, above all others, that binds a people to their government more tightly than anything else. And he names it.

The ordinary administration of criminal and civil justice.

That is it. That is the thing. Hamilton calls it the most powerful, most universal, and most attractive source of popular obedience and attachment. Let me slow down here, because this is the idea I most want you to carry out of this episode.

Think about what the everyday courts actually are in a person's life. This is the part of government that is, in Hamilton's phrase, the immediate and visible guardian of life and property. When someone steals from you, this is what answers. When someone breaks a contract with you, when your neighbor's fence creeps onto your land, when a man dies and his children fight over the farm, when a marriage falls apart, when a thief is dragged before a judge — this is the government you meet. Not in the abstract. In the flesh. With its benefits and its terrors, as Hamilton says, in constant activity before the public eye.

This is the government that touches the things people are most awake to — their stuff, their safety, their families, their money, the daily fabric of their lives. And Hamilton says that machinery, the ordinary courts, is the great cement of society. The glue. The thing that makes people feel a government is real, is theirs, is owed their loyalty.

And here is the punch line. In seventeen eighty-seven, every bit of that machinery belonged to the states. Picture the actual landscape of justice in early America. The sheriff is a county man. The judge who hears your land case sits in your state's court, under your state's law. The jail is local. The marriage license, the will, the deed to your property, the trial of the man who robbed you — all of it, state and local, top to bottom. If you were an ordinary American in seventeen eighty-seven, virtually every encounter you would ever have with the law in your whole life ran through your state. The federal government, by contrast, was a customs house at the port, a post road, an army somewhere far off, a handful of courts handling a narrow band of national business. For most people, on most days, the national government was something you read about, not something you felt.

So Hamilton's argument lands like this. The states own the one thing that earns a government real love — the daily, visible delivery of justice and order. That gives them, in his words, so decided an empire over their citizens that they will always be a complete counterpoise to the power of the union, and — listen to this — not unfrequently dangerous rivals to it. Dangerous rivals. He is saying the states will be so strong, so beloved, that the real risk is they overpower the union, not the other way around.

And the federal government? Its work, Hamilton says, falls less immediately under the eye of the ordinary citizen. Its benefits go to more general, more distant interests — the kind of thing, he says a little wryly, that mostly gets noticed by speculative men. By which he means the thinkers, the merchants, the policy-minded few who follow national affairs. Important work, but not the work that comes home to the feelings of an ordinary family. Nobody weeps with gratitude over a well-negotiated treaty the way they feel the judge who saves their farm.

So that is the case. The states command the people's love because the states run the part of life people can feel. And a government the people love is a government that cannot be pushed around. Now Hamilton wants to prove this is not just a clever theory. He wants to show you it has happened before, in history, at full scale. And to do that he reaches for the strangest, most vivid comparison in the paper. He compares the American states to the warlords of the Middle Ages.

This is the story I want to tell properly, because it is the engine of the whole essay.

Go back to medieval Europe, Hamilton says — the world of feudalism. Now, feudalism was not exactly a confederacy, he admits, but it ran on the same machinery, so it teaches the same lesson. Picture the structure. At the top, a king. A common head, a sovereign whose authority, on paper, stretched over the whole nation. And beneath him, a layer of great lords — the barons, the dukes, the powerful vassals — each one handed an enormous chunk of land. And on that land, the baron was, in Hamilton's exact phrase, a kind of sovereign. He had his own fighting men. His own followers, the lesser vassals and retainers who worked his land and owed their loyalty to him personally, because he was the one whose protection they lived under. The baron in his castle was the government those people actually knew.

So now ask the question this whole episode is about. Who did the common people of the Middle Ages actually obey? The distant king, whose face they had never seen? Or the lord right there, who held their land, judged their disputes, and led them into battle? You already know the answer. They followed the lord. The man who was close. The man whose justice they felt.

And Hamilton says the consequences of that arrangement were exactly what you would expect when the center is weak and the local powers are strong. Constant defiance of the king. Frequent, grinding wars between the great barons themselves. A central authority too feeble to keep the peace or even to protect the common people from being ground down by their own lords. Historians, Hamilton notes, have a name for this whole era. They call it the times of feudal anarchy. A king in name, chaos in fact, and the real power sitting out in the castles.

Now here is the turn in the story, and it is the part that makes the comparison so sharp. Did the kings always lose? No. Sometimes a king clawed his way to real power and broke the barons. And Hamilton asks the crucial question: when that happened, why did it happen? What let a king finally win?

And his answer is not what you would guess. The kings who beat their barons did not win because they were stronger or smarter. They won, Hamilton says, because of the tyranny of those barons over their own dependents. Here is the dynamic. A cruel baron, one who abused and oppressed his own people, was hated by those people. So he had enemies above him — the king he defied — and enemies below him — the followers he mistreated. And when the king and the common people share an enemy, they make common cause. Mutual danger and mutual interest, Hamilton says, drove the crown and the commoners into an alliance that proved fatal to the power of the aristocracy. The baron got crushed between the two.

But — and this is the line that matters most — Hamilton says the opposite was also true. Had the nobles, by a conduct of clemency and justice, kept the loyalty and devotion of their own people, then the contests between the barons and the king would almost always have ended in the barons' favor. In other words: a lord who treated his people well was unbeatable. The king could not touch a baron whose own people loved him. The only barons who fell were the ones who had already lost their people's hearts.

Do you see what Hamilton has just done? He has handed the states the entire blueprint. The states are the barons. The federal government is the king. And the lesson of a thousand years of European history is that the local power wins — as long as it keeps the affection of its people. The center can only triumph over a local government that has already made itself hated. So as long as the states govern with, in Hamilton's words, uprightness and prudence — as long as they keep delivering that everyday justice that binds people to them — the federal government can no more swallow them than a medieval king could topple a beloved lord.

And to nail it down with a real example, Hamilton points to Scotland. For centuries, he says, Scotland ran on the spirit of clanship — the great chiefs and their clans bound together by ties as strong as blood, as strong as family. And that bond made the Scottish nobility a constant overmatch for the power of the Scottish king. The crown simply could not master lords whose people were loyal to them down to the bone. So what finally tamed that fierce, ungovernable system? Not a stronger Scottish king. It took the union with England — the joining of the two kingdoms, which happened in seventeen oh seven, creating Great Britain — to finally fold the Scottish clans into a larger, more orderly system of law. The local loyalty was so powerful that only the creation of an entirely new and bigger nation could absorb it.

So now Hamilton brings the whole comparison home, and he adds one twist that should make the ratifiers who feared a too-strong government feel a little foolish. The states, he says, are like the feudal baronies — but with one big advantage the barons did not have. The barons often abused their people. The states, governing decently, will keep their people's confidence and good-will. And with that support behind them, the states will be able to effectively block any real encroachment by the national government. And then Hamilton goes one step further, and it is almost a warning to his own side. He writes that it will be well — meaning, we'll be lucky — if the states are not so strong that they end up obstructing the union's legitimate and necessary authority. Read that again. Hamilton's actual fear, stated out loud, is not that the federal government will be too strong. It is that the states will be too strong — strong enough to choke even the lawful, necessary things the union genuinely needs to do.

That is how completely he has turned the objection around. The reader came in afraid the eagle would eat the states. Hamilton leaves him worried the states might clip the eagle's wings.

Now. A fair question here is the one you have probably been holding the whole episode. Because you live two hundred and forty years downstream of this, and from where you are sitting, Hamilton looks like he got it spectacularly wrong. The federal government today is enormous. It reaches into daily life in a thousand ways. It taxes your paycheck directly, sends retirement checks to tens of millions of people, insures the money in your bank, runs benefits and agencies that touch nearly every household in the country. That distant, abstract government that nobody could feel? It is now, for many Americans, the most present government in their lives. So was Hamilton just flat wrong?

It is the honest question, and it deserves an honest answer, which is: partly. But the way he was wrong is more interesting than a simple miss, because Hamilton himself told you exactly how his prediction could fail. Go back to the affection principle. People love the government that is close and that they can feel. And then Hamilton added a single, quiet escape clause that turns out to be the most prophetic thing in the paper. He said the people would lean toward their state governments — and then these words —

"unless the force of that principle should be destroyed by a much better administration of the latter."

The latter being the union. Read that slowly, because Hamilton is handing you the whole future. He is saying: the states will keep the people's loyalty, unless the federal government one day starts doing such a visibly good job of delivering things people feel that it wins that loyalty for itself. He told you the states' grip was not permanent. He told you it was conditional. It lasted exactly as long as the states were the government you could feel and the union was the government you could not.

And then history pulled the lever Hamilton left sitting right there. For the first hundred and twenty-five years or so, his prediction held beautifully — the federal government really was distant, and the states really did own daily life. Then it changed, and you can almost name the moments. In nineteen thirteen, a constitutional amendment let the federal government tax incomes directly — and in time Washington would have a hand in nearly every working person's pocket. In the nineteen thirties, the federal government created Social Security, the promise of a retirement check paid straight to the individual; it insured the money in people's bank accounts; it put millions of people to work. For the first time, in a hard season, the government you could feel in your daily survival was the one in Washington, not the one in your state capital. Decades later came federal health coverage for the old and the poor. Piece by piece, the federal government stopped being a customs house over the horizon and became a check in the mailbox, a line on the paystub, a guarantee behind the bank. It did exactly the thing Hamilton said would break the states' monopoly on affection: it became a government people could feel.

So here is the fair verdict, and it is a strange one. Hamilton's prediction was overtaken — but his mechanism was dead right. He was wrong that the federal government would stay distant. He was completely right that whichever government touches your daily life is the one that wins your loyalty. The states lost their lock on the people's affection not because Hamilton's principle failed, but because his principle worked, and the federal government finally satisfied its terms.

And notice the part of his argument that did not get overtaken at all. The ordinary administration of justice — the courts, the cops, the everyday law that Hamilton said was the great cement of society — that is still, overwhelmingly, the states'. Right now, today, the vast majority of crimes are state crimes, prosecuted in state courts. Your divorce, your will, your house closing, your car accident lawsuit, the police officer in your town — almost all of it is still state and local, exactly as it was in seventeen eighty-seven. The single most powerful binding force Hamilton identified is still doing its work, still pulling Americans toward their states, still making a Texan a Texan and a Vermonter a Vermonter. He misjudged how the contest would go. He did not misjudge the rules of the contest.

Which is why the line Hamilton uses to close this paper has aged so well. He says the great mistake people keep making — the source of so much bad political thinking — is that they have, in his phrase, given our jealousy a direction to the wrong side.

"has given our jealousy a direction to the wrong side."

That is the whole paper in seven words. Jealousy here means watchfulness, suspicion, the wary eye a free people keep on power. And Hamilton's complaint is that people are pointing that wary eye in the wrong direction. They are staring at the federal government, braced for it to grow into a tyrant, while the real structural weakness of the system — the thing that was actually killing the country in seventeen eighty-seven — sat in the other direction entirely, in a union too weak to do its job against states too strong to be governed. Watch the right threat, Hamilton is saying. You are guarding the wrong door.

Now, whether his readers should have been quite so relaxed about federal power is a question history gets to keep arguing about, and we just saw that the danger turned out to be more two-sided than Hamilton let on. But within the world of seventeen eighty-seven, against a confederation that genuinely could not collect a tax or enforce a treaty, he had the diagnosis right. The patient was not in danger of an overbearing center. The patient was bleeding out from a center too weak to hold anything together.

And Hamilton ends by telling you where he is going next. He says the way to really prove all of this — to show that loose confederacies fail because the center is too weak, not too strong — is to walk through the actual history of confederate governments and see what happened to them. That review, he says, will be the subject of some ensuing papers. He is writing a promissory note. And it gets paid starting next time.

Because beginning with Federalist Number Eighteen, the series takes a sharp turn. Hamilton hands part of the work to James Madison, and together they stop arguing from human nature and start arguing from the record. They are going to put the great confederacies of history on the examining table, one by one — the leagues of ancient Greece, the sprawling tangle of the Germanic empire, the confederacies of Europe — and show that every single one of them died of the same disease. A center too weak, members too strong, and a slow slide into the chaos Hamilton has been warning about since Number Fifteen. If Number Seventeen told you the states would always be strong, the next papers are going to show you the graveyard of every union that let its members get too strong and its center too weak.

This has been The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Seventeen. Next time: Federalist Number Eighteen, where Hamilton and Madison open the history books and take us to ancient Greece, to see what really happens to a confederacy whose members never quite agree to be governed.
