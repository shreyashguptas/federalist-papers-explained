Today we start at the beginning. Federalist No. 1. Written by Alexander Hamilton. Published on October 27, 1787. And it's a better opening than most people realize.

To understand what Hamilton is doing in this essay, you need to understand the moment he's writing into.

It's the fall of 1787. The Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia has just wrapped up after a difficult summer of closed-door negotiations. The delegates had originally been sent to revise the existing framework of American government — a document called the Articles of Confederation. But what they actually did was much more radical. They scrapped the Articles almost entirely and drafted a brand new Constitution.

Now that Constitution has to be ratified by the states. Under the ratification plan the Convention agreed on, nine out of thirteen states would need to approve it before it could take effect. Each state would hold its own ratifying convention. And the debates were going to be fierce.

New York was one of the most skeptical states. The governor of New York, George Clinton, was strongly opposed to the new Constitution, and he had a large base of political support. Anti-Federalists — the people who opposed ratification — were powerful in New York. Hamilton knew that winning New York was going to be a fight.

So Hamilton, along with James Madison of Virginia and John Jay of New York, hatched a plan. They would write a series of essays for New York newspapers, arguing publicly for ratification. These essays would be signed with a single pen name: Publius — a reference to Publius Valerius Publicola, one of the founders of the Roman Republic after the kings were expelled. The name was meant to signal seriousness, republican values, and historical weight. And keeping the actual authors hidden was standard practice in political writing of the era.

Hamilton wrote the lion's share. Of the 85 papers, Hamilton wrote 51. Madison wrote 29. Jay wrote 5. Together they produced one of the most influential works of American political thought — though at the time, they were newspaper essays written on a deadline, trying to persuade ordinary readers in the middle of an urgent national debate.

But right now, in October 1787, all of that is ahead of them. Hamilton is sitting down to write the first one. And the question is: how do you open?

Hamilton's opening is sweeping. He doesn't start small. He starts with the largest claim he can honestly make.

He tells his readers — New Yorkers opening the Independent Journal — that the decision they're about to make is not just about their state, or even about America. He says it's a decision for all of humanity.

Here's how he puts it. He writes that it seems to have been "reserved to the people of this country, by their conduct and example, to decide the important question, whether societies of men are really capable or not of establishing good government from reflection and choice, or whether they are forever destined to depend for their political constitutions on accident and force."

That's a remarkable framing. Let's sit with it.

Hamilton is saying: every other country throughout history got its form of government through force, conquest, circumstance, or accident. Kings inherited thrones. Constitutions emerged from wars. Political systems were imposed on people — they didn't choose them. The idea of a free people sitting down, reasoning together, and deliberately choosing their own form of government was essentially unprecedented.

And Hamilton is telling New Yorkers: that is what you are being asked to do right now. You're not just voting on a document. You're answering a question that every civilization before you has failed to answer: can human beings actually govern themselves through reason?

If they fail — if they reject a workable Constitution and the union dissolves into competing regional confederacies — Hamilton is suggesting the world will take that as evidence that self-government doesn't work. That free peoples cannot actually deliberate their way to a stable, effective government. That accident and force always win in the end.

That's the stake he puts on the table before making a single specific argument.

You might wonder: was this an exaggeration? A bit of rhetorical inflation?

Maybe slightly. But not by much. The American experiment really was being watched by the world. European intellectuals and statesmen were fascinated. Enlightenment thinkers had theorized about republican self-government for decades, but no one had done it at this scale. Britain, France, Prussia — they all had kings or strong central governments rooted in tradition and force. The idea that a scattered collection of former colonies could design their own government through reasoned debate was genuinely novel.

Hamilton was not wrong to call this a historic moment. He was just being very direct about it.

Before we go deeper into the argument, it's worth taking a moment on who Hamilton is and what he's doing here.

Alexander Hamilton was in his early thirties when he wrote this essay. He was born in the Caribbean, in the British West Indies — on the island of Nevis — and came to New York as a young man to study at what is now Columbia University. He served as George Washington's aide-de-camp during the Revolutionary War, where he proved himself indispensable. After the war he built a career in New York law and politics, and he was a delegate at the Constitutional Convention.

Hamilton was brilliant and fast. He could produce written work at extraordinary speed. He would go on to write dozens of these Federalist essays in just a few months, sometimes cranking out multiple papers in a single week.

But here's something important to carry into everything that follows: Hamilton was not a neutral observer. He had been at the Convention. He had voted for the final Constitution — though privately, he had significant reservations. Hamilton actually wanted a stronger central government than what the delegates agreed to. He had proposed a far more powerful executive and much longer terms for senators. The delegates didn't go there. So Hamilton supported a Constitution he thought was imperfect, because he believed it was dramatically better than the existing Articles of Confederation, and because he thought the union itself was in genuine danger.

So from the very first word of Federalist No. 1, you are reading advocacy. This is not dispassionate political theory. This is a lawyer and statesman making the best possible case for his side, using every rhetorical tool he has. And Hamilton knows this, and he says so — we'll get to that moment shortly.

Having laid out the enormous stakes, Hamilton turns to the opposition. He knows people will fight against the Constitution, and he wants to characterize who they are and why they're opposing it.

He identifies two main categories of opponents.

The first group is state officeholders — people who hold positions of power under the existing state governments and who have a practical, personal reason to resist a stronger federal government. A more powerful national government means less room for state-level officials to operate independently, less patronage, less influence, less income. Hamilton says this directly. These men, he writes, have "the obvious interest of a certain class of men in every State to resist all changes which may hazard a diminution of the power, emolument, and consequence of the offices they hold under the State establishments."

The second group is what Hamilton calls men of "perverted ambition." These are people who see opportunity in a fragmented America — either by exploiting the chaos that would come from a weak or collapsed union, or by becoming big fish in smaller ponds if the thirteen states divide into separate regional confederacies. If America breaks apart into three or four smaller groupings of states, some ambitious people would find themselves much more powerful in those smaller arrangements than they could ever be in a united nation.

In both cases, Hamilton is saying: follow the incentives. Ask who benefits from the current arrangement or from the failure of the Constitution, and you'll understand a lot of the opposition.

Here's where Hamilton does something genuinely interesting. Having just described both groups of opponents as driven by self-interest and ambition, he immediately steps back from that characterization.

He says, in effect: I know I just described the opposition in pretty unflattering terms. But that's not the complete picture. Honest people can also oppose the Constitution for honest reasons.

He writes that "candor will oblige us to admit that even such men may be actuated by upright intentions; and it cannot be doubted that much of the opposition which has made its appearance, or may hereafter make its appearance, will spring from sources, blameless at least, if not respectable — the honest errors of minds led astray by preconceived jealousies and fears."

This is a careful rhetorical move — but it's also a real point. Many Anti-Federalists had sincere concerns. They worried about a distant federal government with too much power. They worried about what would happen to liberty if the proposed Constitution was adopted. These weren't cynical positions. They came from real values and real experiences — including the experience of living under British rule, which had given Americans a deep, well-founded suspicion of concentrated central authority.

Hamilton is acknowledging that. He's saying: reasonable people can look at this situation and disagree. Being wrong doesn't make someone a bad actor.

But this concession also sets up Hamilton's own position. By showing generosity toward his opponents, he presents himself as the measured, fair-minded voice in the debate. And fair-minded people are more persuasive than people who just attack everyone who disagrees with them.

He goes even further. He says we should all be humble about our certainty — even when we're right. Smart, good people end up on the wrong side of important questions all the time. Our judgments get distorted by fear, by prejudice, by the assumptions we brought in before we even started thinking about a problem. And here's a sharp observation that cuts in every direction: the people on the right side of an argument aren't always purer in their motives than the people on the wrong side. Good causes can be championed by bad actors. Bad positions can be held by people with genuinely good intentions.

In our own political moment, this is worth sitting with. We tend to assume that agreement with us signals virtue, and disagreement signals bad faith. Hamilton is pushing back on that assumption, directly and deliberately.

After the fair concession, Hamilton makes a prediction about what the actual public debate is going to look like — and he's not optimistic.

He says that despite everything he just said about being reasonable and giving the other side credit, in practice, this debate over the Constitution is going to produce a "torrent of angry and malignant passions." Both sides will caricature the other.

Supporters of the Constitution who talk about the need for an effective government will be accused of secretly wanting to establish a despotism. Their language about efficiency and energy in government will be painted as cover for authoritarian ambition.

Meanwhile, opponents of the Constitution who talk about the danger to liberty will be accused of just being demagogues — whipping up popular fear to serve their own political interests, using the language of rights and freedom as a cover for their real agenda.

And here's what Hamilton says gets forgotten in all of this. People forget that genuine love of liberty often comes packaged with a kind of excessive suspicion — a narrow, anxious distrust that can sometimes do more harm than good. And people also forget that a government that actually works, that has real power and real efficiency, is not the enemy of liberty — it's one of the things that protects it. Without effective government, you don't get freedom. You get chaos, and then someone fills the vacuum.

This is a remarkably clear-eyed description of a dynamic that has defined American political argument ever since. The side that wants stronger federal authority gets accused of tyranny. The side that wants to limit federal power gets accused of protecting entrenched interests or blocking progress. Both sides throw the words liberty, tyranny, rights, and efficiency around in ways that mean very different things to different listeners.

Hamilton's request to his readers is essentially: when you see this happening, recognize it for what it is. These are performances, not arguments. Don't let the heat of the debate convince you to stop thinking.

And now Hamilton makes what I think is the most important argument in this entire paper. It arrives in one dense paragraph near the end of his introduction, and it's easy to slide past it if you're reading quickly. But don't.

He says there's something counterintuitive you need to understand about how liberty actually gets destroyed. The obvious fear — the fear that drives a lot of the opposition to the Constitution — is that a strong government will oppress you. That the people who want a powerful central authority are the ones who will eventually take your freedom away. Hamilton does not dismiss this fear. It's a real fear with real historical basis.

But he says the actual historical record points in a different direction than most people expect. Here is his warning in his own words: "a dangerous ambition more often lurks behind the specious mask of zeal for the rights of the people than under the forbidden appearance of zeal for the firmness and efficiency of government. History will teach us that the former has been found a much more certain road to the introduction of despotism than the latter, and that of those men who have overturned the liberties of republics, the greatest number have begun their career by paying an obsequious court to the people; commencing demagogues, and ending tyrants."

Let me translate that carefully, because it's dense.

Hamilton is saying: the people who actually destroy republics usually don't come to power by openly declaring themselves tyrants. They come to power by claiming to be the great defender of the people's rights. They whip up popular anger. They present themselves as the common man's champion against corrupt elites. They use the language of liberty and freedom to accumulate personal power — and then they use that power to crush the very liberty they claimed to be defending.

The arc Hamilton is describing is: they start by flattering the people and positioning themselves as their protector, and they end as tyrants.

Commencing demagogues, and ending tyrants.

This was not an abstract observation for Hamilton and his contemporaries. The founders thought constantly about the Roman Republic, which had been the most successful republican experiment the ancient world produced — and which had collapsed. And it hadn't collapsed because someone had marched in and said "democracy is over, I'm taking over." It had collapsed through a long sequence of populist leaders, each of whom claimed to be fighting for the Roman people: the Gracchi brothers in the second century B.C., then Marius, then Julius Caesar, and eventually Augustus, who dismantled the Republic while carefully preserving its forms. Each of these figures came to power by appealing to popular grievances, by positioning themselves against elites, by claiming to speak for the common people. Each of them, in the process, concentrated power in ways that weakened the Republic and made the next step toward despotism easier.

Hamilton is warning his readers: watch carefully whose claims about defending your freedom you actually believe. The person waving the banner of popular rights loudest may be exactly the person you should be most suspicious of.

Now, it's worth saying: this argument cuts in multiple directions. Hamilton himself was accused by contemporaries — most famously by Thomas Jefferson — of harboring exactly the kind of ambitions he was warning against. Jefferson thought Hamilton wanted something close to a monarchy. There were real, serious debates about where Hamilton's ambitions ended and his principles began. So the demagogue warning that Hamilton issues in this essay was the kind of charge that got turned back on Hamilton personally, throughout his career.

History is complicated. But the core insight in that paragraph stands on its own and remains relevant: be skeptical of anyone who builds their political appeal primarily on the claim to be the unique defender of your liberty. Ask what they're actually getting out of it. Ask whether the power they're accumulating in the name of protecting you might eventually be used against you.

It's one of the most enduring observations in American political writing.

After all of that — the high stakes, the two types of opponents, the fair concession, the prediction of ugly debate, the demagogue warning — Hamilton does something a little disarming.

He comes clean.

He says, essentially: I've been warning you about people who claim to be neutral but have hidden agendas. I should be honest about my own.

He writes: "Yes, my countrymen, I own to you that, after having given it an attentive consideration, I am clearly of opinion it is your interest to adopt it."

And then, even more bluntly: "I will not amuse you with an appearance of deliberation when I have decided."

That second line is almost startling. He's saying: I'm not going to pretend I'm still weighing both sides. I've decided. I support the Constitution. I think it's the right choice for you. And I am going to tell you why, openly, with my reasoning available to anyone who wants to examine it.

Hamilton then says his personal motives are his own — you'll have to take those on faith or not. But his arguments are open to everyone and can be judged by everyone. He invites his readers to hold him accountable to the quality of his reasoning.

This is a strong position. It's actually harder to dismiss an argument made by someone who has told you their position upfront and invited scrutiny than it is to dismiss an argument made by someone pretending to be neutral. Disclosed bias is easier to account for than hidden bias.

And this disclosure sets the tone for the entire Federalist project. These are not neutral academic essays. They are open-books advocacy for the Constitution, argued on the merits. You know where the authors stand. Now watch the argument.

With that disclosure made, Hamilton lays out what this whole series of essays is going to cover. He identifies six major topics — and this is essentially the table of contents for the entire project.

First: the utility of the union to the political prosperity of the states. In other words, why being united is actually in each state's genuine interest. This is the argument of the earliest papers.

Second: the insufficiency of the existing Confederation — the Articles of Confederation — to preserve that union. Why the current system is failing and can't be patched well enough to work.

Third: the necessity of a government at least as energetic as the one proposed. Why a weak fix isn't enough. You need a government that can actually do things.

Fourth: the conformity of the proposed Constitution to the true principles of republican government. Is this thing actually a republic? Does it honor the principles Americans fought a revolution for — representation, accountability, limits on power?

Fifth: the analogy of the proposed Constitution to the existing state constitutions. This is a reassurance argument: the new federal structure isn't as radical as it looks. It mirrors what the states themselves have already built.

Sixth: the additional security that the Constitution would provide for liberty, property, and republican government itself.

That's the shape of the whole argument — six major lines of case across 85 papers.

When you know this outline, you can see that Hamilton and Madison aren't just writing political op-eds. They're writing a sustained, structured brief. They want to anticipate every significant objection, answer it thoroughly, and build a cumulative case that's hard to dismantle piece by piece. It's the most ambitious piece of public constitutional advocacy in American history.

And it starts here, with Federalist No. 1.

At the very end of the essay, Hamilton gives a brief signal of where he's going next.

He notes that most Americans in 1787 still believed in the value of union in principle — the idea of the thirteen states remaining together. But he says that the idea of splitting into separate regional confederacies was already being whispered about in private. And he's heard it. People opposed to the Constitution were beginning to suggest quietly that maybe thirteen states was just too big a territory for any single government to manage effectively — so maybe the answer was to divide into two or three smaller confederacies instead.

Hamilton says this idea will probably spread. And he says the alternative is stark: it's either the new Constitution or the dissolution of the union. There's no comfortable middle option — no version of a slightly improved Articles of Confederation that solves the problem.

So the next paper — Federalist No. 2 — will begin the argument for why the union itself is worth preserving, and what would actually be lost if it broke apart. That's our next episode.

Let me pull back and ask: what is Hamilton actually accomplishing in this opening paper?

He's not making a detailed case for any specific provision of the Constitution. He doesn't talk about the Senate, or the executive, or the courts. He's doing something earlier and more foundational than that.

He's framing the debate before it begins.

He's telling his readers what the decision actually means — not just for them, but for the history of self-government. He's naming the kinds of opponents they'll encounter and explaining why some of that opposition is self-interested. He's predicting the character of the debate they're about to live through. He's issuing a specific warning about the most seductive kind of political manipulation — the demagogue who claims to be your protector. And he's being honest about his own position, while inviting his readers to judge his arguments on their merits.

All of this in one short essay.

If you read Federalist No. 1 as just an introduction, you miss what it is. It's actually a framework for thinking about every political argument that follows — and, in a lot of ways, for thinking about American political argument in general. The questions Hamilton raises here haven't gone away.

Can a free people govern themselves through reason and deliberate choice? Or do they always end up governed by accident, fear, and force? Are the people who claim most loudly to defend your liberty actually the ones you should watch most carefully? Can you hold the two ideas together — that you need an effective government and that liberty matters — without letting one destroy the other?

Those are the questions that will run through all 85 of these papers. And they're the questions that still run through American political life today.
