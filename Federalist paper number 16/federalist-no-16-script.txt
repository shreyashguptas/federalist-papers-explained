At the end of the last episode, Hamilton finished his diagnosis of why America's first government was dying. The disease was a single design flaw — what he called the great and radical vice. Under the Articles of Confederation, the national government makes laws for states, not for people, and a law aimed at a state cannot be enforced, because you cannot fine a state or jail a state. A law with no penalty behind it is not a law at all. It is advice. That is why the requisitions went unpaid, why the treaties went unenforced, and why the country had drifted into what Hamilton called the last stage of national humiliation.

But that diagnosis left one question hanging, and Hamilton ended the paper knowing he still owed his readers an answer. Because there is an obvious fix, and everyone in seventeen eighty-seven could see it. If the problem is that the union's laws have no penalty attached — fine. Attach one. Keep the Articles of Confederation, keep the system everyone already knows, and just give Congress the power to enforce its requisitions. Give the union teeth. Let it compel a state that refuses to pay.

That was not a fringe idea. It was the moderate position — the sensible-sounding middle ground between doing nothing and gambling on an entirely new Constitution. And Federalist Number Sixteen exists to take that idea away from them. Hamilton's argument, boiled down to one sentence, is this: a government that has to point a gun at its own members is not a stronger union. It is a civil war waiting for a reason to start.

Federalist Number Sixteen was published in the New York Packet on Tuesday, December fourth, seventeen eighty-seven — three days after Number Fifteen. It is Hamilton again, still writing as Publius, still inside the autopsy of the Articles of Confederation that the last episode opened. The official title of this one is simply "The Same Subject Continued." And that is exactly what it is. Fifteen named the disease. Sixteen examines the cure everyone keeps reaching for — and shows that the cure would kill the patient faster than the disease.

Before we get into Hamilton's argument, here is a detail that makes this paper much more interesting. The idea of giving Congress the power to coerce states was not just floating around in taverns and newspapers. It had been written down, formally, as a proposed amendment to the Articles. And the man who drafted it was James Madison.

Back in seventeen eighty-one, just as the Articles of Confederation were taking effect, Madison sat on a committee of Congress that confronted the enforcement problem head on. Its proposed fix was an amendment authorizing Congress to use the armed forces of the United States — by land and by sea — against any state that failed to meet its federal obligations. Pay your share, or the union's ships blockade your ports. That amendment went nowhere, but the idea did not die. Six years later, the plan the Virginia delegation carried into the Constitutional Convention — the Virginia Plan, the document that became the skeleton of the Constitution — still had a coercion clause inside it: the power to call forth the force of the union against any state failing to do its duty.

That clause barely survived first contact with debate. When the Convention took it up at the end of May, the man who rose to bury it was Madison himself — the same man whose committee had drafted the idea six years earlier. Madison had spent those six years watching the confederation fail, and he had thought his way to the conclusion Hamilton publishes in this paper. Using force against a state, Madison told the Convention, would not look like law enforcement. It would look, in his words, more like a declaration of war than an infliction of punishment. And the state on the receiving end would treat it as exactly that — a war, one that dissolved every obligation between the attacker and the attacked. The Convention set the clause aside without a single state voting to keep it, and never picked it up again.

So the people who wrote the Constitution had already walked through this argument in private. Federalist Number Sixteen is Hamilton walking the public through it. And the way he does it is to make you watch the war.

So let us watch it. This is the scenario Hamilton builds, and it is the darkest passage he has written in the series so far.

Imagine it is seventeen eighty-eight, in a slightly different America. The Articles of Confederation are still in force, but the moderates got their wish. Congress now has its enforcement power, and a federal army to back it. A requisition goes out, the way requisitions always do — each state's share of the union's expenses, calculated and assigned. And one state does not pay. Let us say it is New York. There is a reason to pick New York, and Hamilton's readers would not have missed it: New York was the state most openly at odds with Congress in those years, the state whose refusal to hand over its customs revenue had strangled every attempt to give the union an income of its own.

So Congress, armed with its new power, orders the army to New York to collect.

Here is the first thing that goes wrong. New York is not the only state behind on its payments. It never is — we saw the numbers last episode: in the worst years, Congress collected pennies on the dollar from everyone. So the moment the federal army starts marching, every other delinquent state asks itself the same question. Not "does New York deserve this." The question is "are we next?" And Hamilton says states that share that fear will do what fearful neighbors have always done. They will band together. The state being punished and the states afraid of being punished next make common cause, and suddenly the army is not facing one government. It is facing an alliance.

Here is the second thing that goes wrong. A large state under federal coercion does not tell its people, or its neighbors, that this is a dispute about money. It reaches for bigger words. Hamilton says specious arguments of danger to the common liberty could easily be contrived — meaning, the targeted state will dress its tax delinquency in the language of freedom. This is not about our unpaid requisition. This is about whether a distant Congress can send soldiers against Americans. Liberty is at stake. And that argument, Hamilton says, would work — it would inflame people far beyond the borders of the state being coerced, including in states that had faithfully paid every dollar they owed. Worse, an ambitious state government might engineer this entire confrontation on purpose, picking a fight with the union precisely to break free of it, and lining up sympathetic politicians in the neighboring states before the first soldier ever moved.

And here is the third thing, the one Hamilton's readers could see on a map. A confederacy at war with its own members is the single best opportunity a foreign power will ever get. Britain was still garrisoning forts on American soil — Detroit, Niagara, Oswego, the troops who simply never left after the war. Now imagine the federal army is marching against New York, and a British agent arrives in Albany with an offer of money, weapons, and friendship. Would the offer be made? Of course it would. Hamilton says foreign powers would seldom be disinclined to encourage exactly this kind of breach. Helping a confederacy tear itself apart is cheaper than fighting it.

So the enforcement power, the moderate fix, the sensible compromise — within weeks of its first real use, it has produced an alliance of delinquent states, wrapped in the flag of liberty, with a foreign patron behind it, facing a federal army in the field. And at that point, Hamilton says, nothing about the outcome is certain except its size. Here is the sentence at the center of the paper.

"When the sword is once drawn, the passions of men observe no bounds of moderation."

Once the fighting starts, it stops being about the thing it was about. The requisition, the unpaid money, the legal question of who owed what — all of that evaporates. Wounded pride takes over. Hamilton says the states under attack would be carried to any extremes necessary to avenge the affront or to avoid the disgrace of submission. People who would have grumbled and paid their taxes will die before they surrender to an invading army — even an army that, on paper, is legally in the right. And Hamilton closes the scenario with a flat prediction: the first war of this kind would probably be the last, because it would end in the dissolution of the union itself.

The first war. Not the fifth, not the tenth. The enforcement power does not preserve the union. It schedules the union's funeral, and lets some future tax dispute pick the date.

Now, at this point you might be thinking: this whole scenario is absurd. No Congress would ever actually do it. No delegate from Connecticut is going to vote to send an army against Massachusetts over a missed payment, knowing everything you just described. The enforcement power would sit there, on paper, never used.

And Hamilton agrees with you. That is the smartest turn in the whole paper. He says the civil war scenario — he calls it the violent death of the confederacy — is actually the less likely outcome. Precisely because everyone can see how catastrophic enforcement would be, enforcement will never happen. The states that pay will look at the states that do not, and they will refuse to go to war over it. Instead, they will do something much easier and much more human.

They will stop paying too.

Why would Connecticut keep bleeding itself to meet every requisition while New York ignores them and faces no consequence? It will not — not for long. Hamilton says the complying states will choose the milder path of simply imitating the delinquent ones, until everyone is equally guilty. And then he compresses the whole death of the union into one sentence.

"And the guilt of all would thus become the security of all."

When everyone is guilty, no one can be punished. Each state's failure becomes every other state's excuse. The system does not explode. It just quietly stops, the way a heart stops — not the violent death, but what Hamilton calls the natural one. And he is not predicting this. He is describing it. This is the death the United States was actually dying in seventeen eighty-seven, the spiral we traced last episode, every state's compliance ratcheting down to match the laziest member, all the way to zero.

If you want to see exactly what that looks like up close — what it looks like when a state defies the union and nothing happens — you do not have to imagine it. Hamilton's readers had watched it happen the year before, across the river in New Jersey.

Remember back in Episode Seven, when Hamilton was listing the things separate American states would actually fight about, he pointed at New York's harbor. New York taxed every cargo that came through its port, and a large share of that tax was quietly paid by the people of New Jersey and Connecticut — they bought the goods, so they paid the markup, but the revenue went into New York's treasury. Hamilton asked, in that paper, how long New Jersey and Connecticut would put up with being taxed for New York's exclusive benefit.

He already knew the answer, because New Jersey had already given it. Early in seventeen eighty-six, the New Jersey legislature did something no state had done before. It did not just fall behind on its federal requisition — every state did that. It passed a formal, public resolution announcing that it would not pay. Not a penny, the legislature declared, until the plan to give the union its own revenue — the federal import duty that New York kept blocking — was finally adopted. New Jersey's complaint was, frankly, legitimate. The state had no great port of its own, so everything it bought came through New York or Philadelphia, and the duties on all of it flowed into those states' treasuries — while Congress billed New Jersey for its share of the union anyway. Taxed at the docks by its neighbors, then billed again for the country. But legitimate or not, this was open defiance. A state had looked at a law of the United States and said, out loud, on the record: no.

And what could Congress do about it? Here is the full list of what Congress did, because it is also the full list of what Congress could do. It sent three of its members to Trenton to plead. One of them, a twenty-eight-year-old delegate from South Carolina named Charles Pinckney, stood in front of the New Jersey legislature and made a remarkable argument. He did not really defend the system — he could not, and he knew it. He told New Jersey, in effect: if this system oppresses you — and it may well — then this is not the way to fix it. Open refusal does not just hurt Congress, it shows the whole world that the union is a fiction. If you want the system changed, demand a convention of the states to change it. Then he went home.

New Jersey listened politely. It formally rescinded the offending resolution — it took back the words. And then it did not pay. The words were withdrawn; the money never came. And nothing happened. No penalty, no consequence, nothing. Every legislature in America watched a state defy the government of the United States to its face and walk away without a scratch.

A fair question here is how Congress could even tell the difference between a state that was defying it and a state that was simply broke. And the answer is that it could not — Hamilton says that distinction is impossible to draw, and the impossibility is itself one of the system's fatal flaws. Any state that does not want to pay can always say it cannot pay. The excuse is always available and can never be disproven. New Jersey did not even bother using it. That is how dead the system already was.

There is one more nail Hamilton puts in this coffin, and it is a practical one. Suppose you ignored everything we have said so far, and you still wanted the coercion system. You would need an army big enough to do the coercing — a permanent, professional, national army, large enough to overawe not just little Delaware but Virginia, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, the giants. Hamilton makes two observations about that army, and the first is that it cannot be built. The union could never afford it — he compares the whole idea to the monster-taming labors of the heroes of mythology, a fantasy, not a plan. And the second observation is worse: if you somehow did build it, you would no longer have a republic. A federal army permanently strong enough to invade any state in the union is a military despotism with extra paperwork. We spent all of Episode Eight on this — Hamilton's warning that standing armies, kept up in peacetime, eventually destroy the freedom of the people they were raised to protect. A union held together by a permanent internal occupation force is not a union. It is an empire, and Americans had just finished fighting their way out of one of those.

And history agrees. Hamilton points out that the ancient confederacies historians praise most — including the Lycian league, the one we met back in Episode Nine, the one Montesquieu called the model of an excellent confederate republic — were precisely the ones that escaped this design. Where coercion of states has been tried, it only ever gets used against small, weak members — nobody coerces the strong ones — and the usual result has been a bloody war in which, as Hamilton puts it, one half of the confederacy has displayed its banners against the other half.

So that is the dead end, sealed at both ends. A union that cannot enforce its laws dies quietly. A union that enforces its laws on states dies violently. If those were the only two options, the American experiment would already be over.

Which brings us to the most important paragraph in this paper — and honestly, one of the most important paragraphs in the entire series. Because there is a third option, and the whole Constitution is built on it.

Force does not work against states. But law works fine against people.

A court can do what an army cannot. A court can summon one person, judge one person, fine one person, jail one person — without a shot fired, without a frontier crossed, without any other person in the state being touched at all. Armies are wholesale; courts are retail. And so, Hamilton says, the government of the union must stop addressing itself to states and start addressing itself to the only thing a government can actually govern — individual human beings. Here is how he puts it.

"The majesty of the national authority must be manifested through the medium of the courts of justice."

That sentence is the Constitution's engine room. The government of the union, Hamilton says, must work the way every state government already works, the way every functioning government in history has worked. It must pass laws that apply to persons. It must run its own courts to try them. It must employ what he calls the ordinary magistrate — the judge, the marshal, the sheriff — instead of the soldier. It must be able to reach the hopes and fears of individuals directly, because individuals, unlike states, can actually be governed.

This is one of those ideas that is hard to see today precisely because it won so completely. Think about how federal law actually reaches you, right now. When the federal government wants taxes, it does not send a request to your governor and hope your state legislature votes to comply. It sends a form to you. If you ignore it, you do not trigger a constitutional crisis between your state and Washington. You get a letter. Then a penalty. Then, if you keep ignoring it, a court date — addressed to you, a person, in a system designed to handle persons. No army has ever been mobilized to make you file your taxes, because none is needed. The machinery Hamilton is describing in this paragraph is so quiet, so completely victorious, that most Americans live their whole lives without noticing it was ever a question.

And the first real test of that machinery proved the point almost immediately. In seventeen ninety-four, farmers in western Pennsylvania violently resisted a federal tax on whiskey — attacking collectors, burning a tax inspector's house. Washington did raise a militia army to disperse them. But notice who that army's target was not. It was not Pennsylvania — the state was never the enemy, because the state was never the addressee of the law. In fact, Pennsylvania's own militia marched as part of the federal force. The targets were individual lawbreakers, and they were handled the way the design intended: arrested, tried in court as persons, two convicted of treason, both pardoned by Washington. Under the Articles, that same dispute would have been a standoff between a state and the union, with no tool to resolve it but the one this paper rules out. Under the Constitution, it was a criminal case.

Now you might ask the question Hamilton saves for the end of the paper, because it is the strongest objection to everything he has just argued. Fine — the new government acts on individuals. But the states have not gone anywhere. They still have their legislatures, their governors, their pride, their grievances. What happens when a state decides to stand between the federal government and its own citizens? Does the whole thing not just collapse back into force in the end?

Hamilton's answer is the cleverest passage in the paper, and it turns on a distinction that sounds like a technicality and is actually everything: the difference between not complying and actively resisting. Between doing nothing and doing something.

Under the Articles of Confederation, a state could kill a federal measure by sitting still. Federal law only reached the people through the state governments — so if a state simply declined to act, the law died. Here is Hamilton describing how easy it is.

"They have only not to act, or to act evasively, and the measure is defeated."

That is sabotage by stillness. It requires no vote against the union, no declaration, no confrontation. It does not even have to look like defiance — a state can just be slow, or distracted, or claim poverty, and the result is identical. It is invisible, it is deniable, and it is perfectly safe. That is how the Articles actually died — not by rebellion, but by neglect that never had to announce itself.

Under the Constitution, that move is gone. Federal law does not pass through the state legislature on its way to the citizen — it goes straight to the citizen, through federal courts. So a state that wants to stop it can no longer just sit still. Stillness now accomplishes nothing; the law operates whether the state government participates or not. To actually block it, the state has to act. It has to pass laws against the union, order its officers to obstruct federal courts, physically place itself between its citizens and the national government. It has to do all of that in the open, in daylight, where everyone can see — and Hamilton says such an act would be an unmistakable, undeniable, visible usurpation. Not a state being sluggish. A state breaking the Constitution in front of the whole country.

So why does that matter? Because open defiance has costs that quiet neglect never had. For open resistance to succeed, Hamilton says, it is not enough for a state legislature to want it. The courts would have to go along — and judges, he predicts, would strike down such acts as void. The people of the state would have to go along — and the people, he argues, are not going to follow their legislature into a constitutional crisis that is visibly the legislature's own doing. Defiance now requires a conspiracy of the legislature, the courts, and the people, all at once, all in public, all accepting the risk of what comes next. That, Hamilton says, is why it would almost never be tried. And then he adds one honest exception, and you should notice it, because it tells you he is not building a cage. Open resistance would be irrational, he says, unless the federal government is the one acting tyrannically — in which case resistance is not a flaw in the system. It is the remedy the people are supposed to reach for.

You might be wondering whether any state ever actually ran this experiment — whether Hamilton's prediction ever got tested. It did. The test came forty-five years later, and it played out so precisely along the lines of this paper that it reads like history was following Hamilton's script.

In eighteen thirty-two, South Carolina decided that the federal tariff — the import tax that protected northern factories while raising prices on everything the South bought — was unconstitutional, and that South Carolina had the right to refuse it. A state convention passed an Ordinance of Nullification declaring the tariff null and void inside South Carolina, and forbidding the collection of those federal duties in the state's ports. This was exactly the move Hamilton had described: a state, by formal public act, placing itself between the federal government and its own citizens. Not quiet non-compliance — that was no longer possible, just as Hamilton said. Open, visible, active resistance.

Now watch what happened, and check each step against Hamilton's predictions.

First, South Carolina expected company. The tariff hurt the entire South, and the nullifiers assumed the other southern states would stand with them — exactly the alliance of the aggrieved that Hamilton's war-game scenario depends on. They got no one. Not a single state joined. Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Virginia — one by one, the other southern legislatures looked at open defiance of the union and formally condemned it. South Carolina stood completely alone, which is exactly what Hamilton predicted open resistance would do to a state: isolate it.

Second, the president did not treat it as a negotiation between governments. Andrew Jackson — a Carolinian by birth, a slaveholder, a man who had built his career on the language of limited federal power — answered nullification with a proclamation that could have been ghostwritten by Hamilton. The Constitution, Jackson wrote, forms a government, not a league. A state cannot void a federal law, and an attempt to do it by force is treason. Congress then backed him with two bills, passed within days of each other — one authorizing the president to enforce the tariff by force, and one lowering the tariff itself, giving South Carolina a way to climb down.

And third, South Carolina climbed down. The convention reassembled, repealed its nullification of the tariff, and then — in a final gesture you have to admire for its sheer face-saving audacity — nullified the enforcement bill instead, which by then no longer mattered to anyone. No shots were fired. The union's law operated. The state that defied it ended up isolated, out-maneuvered, and alone, exactly as a forty-five-year-old newspaper essay said it would be.

But you already know this story has a second act. Twenty-eight years later, South Carolina tried again — and this time ten other states followed, and the result was the bloodiest war in American history. So was Hamilton wrong after all?

Look at the last paragraph of Federalist Number Sixteen, because Hamilton answers this directly, and the honesty of it is striking. He admits that there is a category of crisis no constitution can handle. When a cause of discontent spreads through a whole nation, or a whole region — when the quarrel is not a state government posturing but an entire people genuinely committed — then, he says, the ordinary rules stop applying. Those convulsions, he writes, commonly amount to revolutions and dismemberments of empire. No form of government can always either avoid or control them. You cannot design a constitution that prevents civil war the way you cannot design a building that prevents earthquakes. And then he closes the paper with this.

"It would be idle to object to a government because it could not perform impossibilities."

Judge a government by what is possible. A good design cannot guarantee that the catastrophic, once-in-a-century rupture never comes. What it can do is make sure the ordinary failures — the missed payments, the defiant legislatures, the tax revolts, the thousand frictions of a continental country — get resolved by courts instead of armies, so that they never become the rupture. For the first seven decades of the Constitution, that is exactly what the design did. And when the catastrophe finally came anyway, over the one question the founders had never been able to bring themselves to settle, it is worth noticing what survived it. The federal government fought that war as a government — raising its own armies, collecting its own taxes, enforcing its own laws on individuals from beginning to end — and it is hard to argue that a union still organized as a league of states, the union of the Articles, would have come out of eighteen sixty-five in one piece.

Step back, and look at what Numbers Fifteen and Sixteen accomplish together, because they are really one argument split across two papers. Fifteen established that a law without a penalty is just advice, and that a government whose laws are addressed to states can never attach a penalty to them — except war. Sixteen walks down both of the roads that leaves open. Try to enforce against states, and you get the violent death: the war game, the alliance of the delinquents, the foreign patron, the sword that observes no bounds. Decline to enforce, and you get the natural death: the guilt of all becoming the security of all, the quiet spiral America was already in. The only way out is to change what the law is addressed to. Laws for states mean armies. Laws for people mean courts. That single substitution — the courtroom where the battlefield used to be — is the deepest thing the Constitution does, and it is why Hamilton can claim, with a straight face, that the new government is not a threat to peace among the states. It is the only design under which peace among the states is even possible.

And yet, notice the question this answer creates — because Hamilton's opponents certainly noticed it. If the new federal government can reach every individual in America directly — collect its own taxes, run its own courts, enforce its own laws without ever asking a state's permission — then what stops it from growing, and growing, until it swallows the states entirely? That is the objection Federalist Number Seventeen takes up. And Hamilton's answer is one of the most surprising in the series, because he argues the danger runs in exactly the opposite direction. The federal government, he says, will always be the distant one, the abstract one, the one you never see. The states will run the things people actually care about — their property disputes, their criminal courts, the ordinary justice of daily life — and so the states will always own the people's loyalty in a way the union never can. Two and a half centuries later, with the federal government the size it is now, that prediction is worth examining with some care.
