At the end of the last episode, Hamilton was making a quiet but radical argument. For most of the opening papers, the writers had been on defense, answering the charge that the country was simply too big to govern as one republic. The old wisdom, borrowed from the French thinker Montesquieu, said that a republic had to be small. Stretch it too far and it falls apart. Hamilton turned that around. He argued that enlarging the orbit, taking in more territory and more states, was not the weakness of the plan. It was one of its great strengths. A larger confederate republic, he said, could hold down the internal disorder, the faction and insurrection, that had wrecked the small republics of Greece and Italy.

That was Federalist Number Nine. Hamilton planted the idea. He did not fully grow it.

Today, somebody else picks it up and grows it into the most famous argument in the entire series.

Federalist Number Ten, first published in the Daily Advertiser on November twenty-second, seventeen eighty-seven, and reprinted a day later in the New York Packet. Still signed Publius. But behind the name Publius, for the first time, is not Hamilton and not Jay. It is James Madison. Papers one through nine were Hamilton or Jay. Number Ten is Madison's first contribution, and he walks in and delivers what many people consider the single best essay of the eighty-five.

So before we get into the argument, it is worth knowing who just sat down at the desk.

James Madison was thirty-six years old. He was small, soft-spoken, often in poor health, the kind of man who got overlooked in a crowded room and then turned out to have done more homework than anyone else in it. He was not a soldier like Hamilton, not a diplomat like Jay. He was a scholar of government. And in the run-up to the Constitutional Convention, he had done something that tells you everything about how his mind worked. He had asked a friend in Paris, Thomas Jefferson, to ship him crates of books, histories of ancient and modern confederacies, and he had read his way through all of them, looking for one thing. He wanted to know why republics fail.

Out of that study, in the spring of seventeen eighty-seven, Madison wrote himself a private memorandum. It was not meant for publication. It was a working document, and he gave it a blunt title: "Vices of the Political System of the United States." It was a list. A diagnosis. Everything he thought was going wrong with America under the Articles of Confederation, set down coldly, point by point. And the deepest item on that list, the one he kept circling back to, was the behavior of the state legislatures. They were passing unjust laws. They were trampling minorities. They were doing it by simple majority vote, completely legally, and that was what frightened him. The injustice was not coming from a tyrant. It was coming from the people's own elected representatives, doing exactly what the majority of voters wanted.

Federalist Number Ten is that private memorandum, polished and sharpened and aimed at the public. When you read it, you are watching Madison take his most pessimistic late-night worry and turn it into a public argument for the Constitution. That is the story behind the paper. Now let us walk into the paper itself.

Madison's subject is one word, and he uses it relentlessly. Faction. To his readers, "faction" was close to a curse word. It meant a self-interested group putting itself ahead of the common good. And Madison's claim is that controlling faction is one of the very best things a well-built union can do, because faction is not a minor flaw in popular government. It is the thing that kills it.

He says the instability and injustice and confusion that factions inject into public life have been the mortal diseases under which popular governments have everywhere perished. Sit with that word, mortal. Not a weakness. A cause of death. Every time a government of the people had collapsed, Madison is saying, you could trace it back to this.

So first he wants to be precise about what he is even talking about. And here is the first line genuinely worth hearing in his own words.

"By a faction," he writes, "I understand a number of citizens, whether amounting to a majority or a minority of the whole, who are united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adversed to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community."

Let me unpack that, because the definition is doing careful work. A faction, to Madison, is a group, it could be small or it could be most of the country, joined together by some shared passion or shared interest, and that passion or interest runs against either the rights of other citizens or the long-term good of everyone. Notice what he just did. He said a faction can be a majority. That is the unsettling part. We are comfortable with the idea of a dangerous minority, a small cabal grabbing power. Madison is telling you the more dangerous case is when the faction is the majority. Because then it does not have to break the rules. It just wins the vote.

At this point you might be wondering, all right, if faction is this deadly, why not simply get rid of it? Cure the disease at its root.

Madison takes that question head-on, and his answer is the spine of the whole first half of the paper. He says there are two ways you might try to remove the causes of faction, and both of them are off the table.

The first way is to destroy liberty. Faction needs freedom to exist, the freedom to organize, to speak, to push your interest. So in theory you could stamp out faction by stamping out freedom. And here Madison gives us one of the most quoted images in American political writing.

"Liberty is to faction what air is to fire," he writes, "an aliment without which it instantly expires."

Air feeds fire. Cut off the air, the fire dies. Liberty feeds faction the same way. So yes, you could smother faction by smothering liberty. But, Madison says, that cure is worse than the disease. You do not get rid of fire by getting rid of all the air, because then you cannot breathe either. Liberty is the air a free society lives on. Killing it to stop faction would be insane.

The second way to remove the causes of faction is gentler-sounding and, Madison argues, just as impossible. You could try to give every citizen the same opinions, the same passions, the same interests. If everybody wanted the same things, there would be nothing to fight about. But Madison says human beings are simply not built that way. As long as people are free to think, they will reach different conclusions. And here he goes deeper, into something more permanent than opinion. People have different talents and abilities, and from those different abilities flow different amounts and kinds of property. One person ends up a landowner, another a merchant, another a lender, another a borrower. Protecting those different abilities, Madison says, is actually the first job of government. So government cannot erase the source of faction, because the source of faction is tangled up with the very thing government exists to protect.

And then he names the big one. He says the most common and durable source of factions has been the various and unequal distribution of property. Creditors and debtors. The landed interest and the manufacturing interest and the moneyed interest. These divisions are not a glitch. They grow up of necessity, he says, in every developed society. The faction line runs right through the economy, and it always will.

Now, this is where the paper can feel abstract, distribution of property, clashing interests, and it is worth stopping to make it concrete. Because Madison's first readers did not hear this as abstract at all. They had just lived it. And the clearest example was happening one small state over.

In the middle seventeen eighties, Rhode Island's legislature had been captured by exactly the kind of faction Madison is describing, a majority faction of small farmers and debtors who had been crushed by the postwar economy. And that majority used its power, legally, through ordinary votes, to do something extraordinary. It printed a flood of state paper money, money that was worth a fraction of its face value in the real market. And then it passed laws ordering creditors to accept that nearly worthless paper as full payment of debts. If you were owed a hundred dollars in gold, the law said you had to take a hundred Rhode Island paper dollars, worth maybe twenty-five, and call it even. If you refused, the courts would punish you and treat the debt as paid anyway.

To the debtors, this was relief. To the creditors, it was robbery with a legislature's signature on it. And here is why it haunted men like Madison. Nobody had staged a coup. No king was involved. The majority had simply voted, and the rights of the minority, in this case the people owed money, had been flattened. That is a majority faction sacrificing the rights of other citizens to its own ruling interest. Rhode Island was the live demonstration.

And it was not just Rhode Island. The year before Madison wrote, in the winter of seventeen eighty-six and seventeen eighty-seven, debt-ridden farmers in western Massachusetts had picked up arms. Many of them were veterans of the Revolution. They were losing their farms to foreclosure, and they marched on the courthouses to physically shut the courts down so the foreclosures could not proceed. That was Shays' Rebellion, and it had terrified the country's political class only months before these papers were written. We talked about it back in Episode Six, when Hamilton pointed at it as proof the existing government could not keep order.

So when Madison, later in this paper, warns about "a rage for paper money, for an abolition of debts, for an equal division of property," that is not a man inventing scary hypotheticals. He is naming Rhode Island. He is naming the impulse behind Shays' Rebellion. His readers heard those words and saw the headlines from last year. The abstraction had names and dates.

So if you cannot cure the causes of faction, that is where Madison turns next, and you might be tempted by one obvious hope. The hope is good leadership. Surely wise, public-spirited statesmen can rise above all this, balance the clashing interests, and steer toward the common good.

Madison shuts that door, gently but completely, in one of the most realistic sentences in the whole series.

"It is in vain to say that enlightened statesmen will be able to adjust these clashing interests," he writes. "Enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm."

That is the whole philosophy of the Constitution compressed into a sentence. You cannot build a system that only works when good people are in charge, because good people will not always be in charge. Sometimes the wise leader is at the helm. Often the leader is mediocre, or self-interested, or captured by the same faction everyone else is. A government that depends on a steady supply of excellent rulers is a government waiting to fail. You have to build something that works anyway.

So Madison arrives at his turning point. If you cannot remove the causes of faction, you have only one option left. You control its effects. You stop asking how to make faction not exist, and you start asking how to keep faction, when it inevitably forms, from being able to do real damage.

And here he draws a distinction that matters. If the faction is only a minority, he says, the problem more or less takes care of itself. The majority can simply outvote it. A minority faction can be annoying, it can clog things up, it can convulse society, but it cannot easily seize control through the normal forms of government. The real problem, the one the whole rest of the paper is built to solve, is the majority faction. When the faction is the majority, voting does not save you. The faction wins, fair and square, and the rights of the minority are on the menu.

So why does that matter for the Constitution? Because it tells you exactly what kind of government you need. You need a popular government, one that runs on majority rule, that somehow still keeps a majority from being able to organize itself into an instrument of oppression. That sounds almost like a contradiction. Madison's claim is that it is not, if you build the republic the right way.

And his first move is to clear away a confusion. People in seventeen eighty-seven threw around the word "democracy" loosely, and Madison wants a sharp line between two things. On one side, what he calls a pure democracy, a small society where the citizens all gather in person and govern directly, everyone in one room, deciding everything by show of hands. On the other side, a republic, a government where the people do not rule directly but elect a smaller group to govern on their behalf.

The pure democracy, Madison says, has no cure for faction at all. Picture it. A small community, everyone in the assembly together. It is small, so there are only a few real interests in it. That makes it very likely that one of those interests is an actual majority. And since everyone is in the same room, that majority can see itself, find each other, and act together instantly. There is nothing standing between a majority passion and its execution. That, Madison says, is why direct democracies in history have been short-lived and violent, spectacles of turbulence and contention. He is not insulting the idea of self-government. He is saying the small, direct, in-person version has a fatal design flaw.

The republic opens up a different prospect. And Madison says it cures faction in two distinct ways. Let me take them one at a time, because this is the engine of the paper.

The first way is representation itself. In a republic, the public's views do not go straight into law. They get passed through a chosen body of representatives first. And Madison's argument is that this filtering can refine and enlarge the public views. A good representative, a little removed from the heat of the moment, with a wider view of the country, may actually land closer to the true public interest than the raw, unfiltered crowd would have. The people's voice gets passed through a medium, and the medium can improve it.

Now, Madison is honest here, and this is the kind of honesty that makes the paper great. He immediately admits the filter can run the other way. Representation can also make things worse. Men of factious tempers, of local prejudices, of sinister designs, he says, can win election by intrigue or corruption and then betray the very people who chose them. A representative can be better than the crowd, or a representative can be a con artist. The filter is not magic.

So which is it? And here Madison makes the argument that ties everything to size, the argument that links his paper straight back to Hamilton's "enlargement of the orbit." A large republic, he says, is more likely to get good representatives than a small one. In a bigger republic, each representative is chosen by more people, which makes it harder for an unworthy candidate to pull off the cheap tricks that win small elections, and it widens the pool of genuinely capable people to choose from. Bigger is not the danger. Bigger is part of the safeguard.

But the representation argument is not the famous one. The famous one is the second cure, and it is the idea that this whole paper is remembered for. So let us slow all the way down for it, because it is the payoff.

Here is the problem restated. A majority faction is dangerous because it can find itself, organize, and act. So the question becomes brutally simple. How do you make it hard for a majority faction to form in the first place?

Madison's answer is size. Geographic, human, sprawling size. And here is the line.

"Extend the sphere," he writes, "and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens; or if such a common motive exists, it will be more difficult for all who feel it to discover their own strength, and to act in unison with each other."

Read that twice, because it is counterintuitive, and the counterintuitiveness is the whole point. Extend the sphere. Make the country bigger. Take in more people, more regions, more religions, more occupations, more economic interests. And two protective things happen at once.

The first thing: with so many different interests packed into one country, it becomes much less likely that any single one of them is actually a majority. In small Rhode Island, debtors could be a majority. Across a continent, debtors are one interest among hundreds, farmers and merchants and lenders and shippers and tradesmen and a dozen religious denominations and a dozen regions, and no single one of them is big enough on its own to dominate.

The second thing is even subtler, and it is the part people forget. Even if some bad majority motive does exist, scattered across millions of people over a huge territory, the people who feel it will have a hard time finding each other and coordinating. In the small room, the majority sees itself instantly. Spread that same majority across hundreds of miles, and it cannot easily discover its own strength or act in unison. Distance and diversity make the faction slow, clumsy, and unsure of itself. The bigness of the republic is not a bug Madison is apologizing for. It is the security system.

So why does that matter to us, now? Because this is the part of Madison's design that you can watch operating in any large modern democracy. Think about how hard it is, in a country of hundreds of millions, for any single interest group to get everything it wants. It runs into another region with different needs, another industry with the opposite stake, another community with another set of values. Coalitions have to be assembled, broadened, compromised. Pieces get traded away. That grinding, frustrating process where no faction ever gets a clean win, the thing we often complain about, is not the system breaking. By Madison's logic, that is the system working exactly as designed. The friction is the feature. He looked at a continent-sized country and saw, in its very size and variety, the brake on majority tyranny.

It is worth being honest, the way Madison was honest about representation, that the design is not perfect. Factions in American history have absolutely managed to span the whole country and oppress minorities anyway, sometimes for generations. The extended sphere makes a tyrannical majority harder to assemble. It does not make it impossible. Madison gave the republic a powerful brake. He did not give it a guarantee. But as a piece of political engineering, the core insight has held up remarkably well, and nearly every large democracy on earth runs, knowingly or not, on some version of it.

And this is where Madison brings it home, and you can hear him handing the argument back to Hamilton. The advantage a republic has over a pure democracy in controlling faction, he says, is the same advantage a large republic has over a small one, and that is exactly the advantage the Union has over the individual states standing alone. A factious leader, he says, might kindle a flame within his own state, but will be unable to spread a general conflagration through the other states. A wild scheme, a rage for paper money or the abolition of debts, is far more likely to infect one state than to infect the whole Union, the same way a fever is more likely to take over one county than a whole nation. The single state is Rhode Island. The Union is the firewall.

Which brings us to the last line, and it is the one to carry out of this episode. After all of it, the diagnosis of faction as the mortal disease, the two cures that cannot be used, the turn to controlling effects, representation, and the extended sphere, Madison lands the plane in eleven words.

"In the extent and proper structure of the Union," he writes, "we behold a republican remedy for the diseases most incident to republican government."

A republican remedy for the diseases of republican government. That is the elegance of it. The objection to a big republic was that bigness was the danger. Madison flipped it. Bigness, properly structured, is the cure. You do not have to abandon government by the people to protect people from each other. You can use the structure of a large union to do it. The remedy for the disease of self-government is more, and better-built, self-government.

That is Federalist Number Ten. It started as a worried private list of everything wrong with America, "Vices of the Political System," and it ended as the most enduring argument the founding produced for why a large republic is not a contradiction but a solution.

Next time, Hamilton is back, and he widens the lens again. Federalist Number Eleven leaves the inside of the republic and looks outward, at the world of trade and ships and ports. Hamilton's case there is that union is not only a shield against faction at home, it is what gives America real weight in the world's commerce, and the foundation, eventually, for an American navy. Madison gave us the union turned inward. Hamilton turns it back out to sea.
