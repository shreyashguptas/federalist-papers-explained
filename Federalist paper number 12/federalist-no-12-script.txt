At the end of the last episode, Hamilton was standing at the edge of the ocean. Federalist Number Eleven was his great paper on commerce — the argument that a united America would be one market large enough to make Europe bid for the privilege of trading with it, that a merchant fleet would grow into a navy, and that a navy would buy America the right to be taken seriously in the world. It was the first paper in the series that stopped warning you about what disunion would cost and started selling you what union could win. Hamilton, frankly, hungry for American power.

Today he stays on the subject of money. But he turns it around. Episode Eleven was about earning it. Federalist Number Twelve is about collecting it.

Federalist Number Twelve was published in the New York Packet on November twenty-seventh, seventeen eighty-seven. Still Alexander Hamilton, still signed Publius. And the subject is, on its face, the least glamorous one in the entire series. Revenue. Taxes. How a government actually gets its hands on money.

It does not sound like much. But stay with it, because this is the paper where you finally meet a side of Hamilton the series has only hinted at. Hamilton was, more than anything else he ever was, a money mind. Two years after he wrote this essay, he would become the first Secretary of the Treasury of the United States, and he would spend the rest of his life building the financial machinery of the country out of almost nothing — the national debt, the bank, the customs service, the credit of the United States. Federalist Number Twelve is the warm-up. It is Hamilton, two years before he gets the job, thinking out loud about the one question that job would be all about. Where does a government's money actually come from?

And the paper answers a question this series left hanging all the way back in Episode Two. Think back to the list of everything broken about the Articles of Confederation. Right at the top of it was this. The national government could not tax. It could not raise a single dollar on its own authority. All it could do was send a polite request to each of the thirteen states — Congress called these requests requisitions — asking each state to please contribute its share. And the states, very often, simply did not. They had their own debts, their own taxpayers, their own priorities. So the national treasury sat empty. Hamilton says it flatly in this paper. The treasuries of the States, he writes, have remained empty. And a government that cannot collect money is not really a government. It is a suggestion.

Federalist Number Twelve is Hamilton's answer to that. And it is a sharper answer than just saying the government should be allowed to tax. Almost everyone agreed on that much by now. Hamilton's question is more specific, and more interesting. Of all the ways a government can reach into an economy and pull out money, which one actually works? Which tax can you really collect? And his answer turns out to depend, completely, on whether America stays one country.

So let us start where Hamilton starts — with an idea so simple it sounds obvious, and so important that the whole paper is bolted to it.

A government cannot tax money that is not moving.

Think about what taxation actually is. It is the government reaching into the flow of an economy and taking a small cut as the money goes by. Which means the thing that matters is not how much wealth a country has in some total, frozen sense. It is how much of that wealth is in motion — being earned, spent, traded, lent, paid out, passed from hand to hand. A country where money moves fast and constantly is a country that can fund a government. A country where the wealth just sits there, locked up in land or buried in the ground, can be genuinely rich and still have a treasury that is bone dry.

Hamilton has a perfect example of that, and it is worth slowing down on, because it turns the whole idea into something you can see.

He points his readers to the Emperor of Germany — by which Hamilton means the Habsburg monarch who ruled the sprawling hereditary lands at the heart of Europe, the territories of what was then called the Holy Roman Empire. On paper, this should have been one of the richest rulers on the continent. His domain was vast, fertile, and well populated. And underneath it, Hamilton points out, lay some of the best gold and silver mines in all of Europe. Actual gold. Actual silver. In the ground, on his own land.

And yet, Hamilton says, that monarch could boast only slender revenues. He was, in plain fact, often short of money. He had been forced, more than once, to borrow from other nations just to cover his essential interests, and he could not, on his own resources, sustain a long war. Picture that. A ruler sitting on top of gold mines, going hat in hand to his neighbors for a loan.

How is that even possible? Hamilton's answer is the key that unlocks the rest of the paper. The gold in the mountain is not wealth a government can use. It is just rock with potential. Wealth only becomes taxable when it moves — when it is dug out and sold and spent and circulated, when it turns into trade and wages and prices. And the thing that makes wealth move, the thing that takes a sleepy farming economy and sets its money flowing, is commerce. The Emperor had the wealth. What he did not have was the commerce to put it in motion. So he owned the mines, and he still could not pay his bills.

And here Hamilton pauses to settle an old argument. For a long time, people had fought over whether a nation should build itself on agriculture or on commerce — on the farm or on the merchant ship — as if you had to choose, as if the two were rivals. Hamilton says that fight is finished, and experience has decided it. Agriculture and commerce are not rivals. They are partners. Because commerce, by making money circulate, lifts the value of everything it touches — including land itself. The merchant's ships create a market for the farmer's grain. A busy port makes the farms around it worth more. Hamilton puts the conclusion in one confident sentence.

The prosperity of commerce, he writes, is now perceived and acknowledged by all enlightened statesmen to be the most useful as well as the most productive source of national wealth, and has accordingly become a primary object of their political cares.

The most productive source of national wealth. Hamilton is not saying commerce is one nice feature of a modern economy. He is saying it is the engine. It is the thing that generates the wealth and, just as importantly, keeps it moving — and moving wealth is the only kind a government can ever realistically tax.

At this point a fair question lands. All right — if America is mostly farmland, and the farms have real value, then why not simply tax that? Tax the land. Tax people directly. Send out the assessors, set a rate, knock on doors, and collect. Why does Hamilton think a government cannot just do the obvious thing?

His answer turns on a problem most of us today never have to think about. America in seventeen eighty-seven was desperately short of cash. Not short of wealth — short of actual money, hard coin, gold and silver currency. There were almost no American banks, there was no national currency, and what little coin existed drained steadily overseas to pay for imported goods. So picture the typical American: a farmer. He might own good land, a sound house, livestock, tools. On paper he is not poor at all. But he might go weeks without holding a single coin in his hand, because his corner of the economy ran on barter, on credit, on promises written in a ledger. He had wealth. He had almost no money.

Now send the tax collector to that farmer and demand a payment in cash. He cannot pay. Not because he is unwilling — because the coins genuinely are not there. And we have already seen, in this series, exactly what happens next. Back in Episode Six, and again in Episode Ten, we talked about Shays' Rebellion — the uprising of debt-crushed farmers in western Massachusetts. Massachusetts had tried to do precisely this. It demanded hard-money taxes from cash-poor farmers, and when they could not pay, the courts foreclosed on their farms. The farmers picked up muskets. Federalist Number Twelve is, among other things, Hamilton's cold economic explanation of why Shays' Rebellion happened in the first place. You cannot squeeze cash out of an economy that does not have any. Try, and you do not collect revenue. You collect foreclosures, and then you collect a rebellion.

So heavy direct taxes on land and people will not fill an American treasury. What about excises? An excise is a tax on a good produced and sold inside the country — a tax on home-distilled whiskey, say, or on domestic tobacco, collected from the maker or the seller. Hamilton looks at excises and shakes his head at those too, and this time his reason is not about money. It is about temperament. He writes one of the sharpest lines in the whole paper.

The genius of the people, he writes, will ill brook the inquisitive and peremptory spirit of excise laws.

Let me translate that. The genius of the people means the basic character, the temperament, of Americans. Will ill brook means will barely tolerate. And the inquisitive and peremptory spirit of excise laws — that is the part that really matters. To collect an excise, the government has to come inside. It has to send an officer into your distillery, your workshop, your barn. It has to inspect, measure, count, and demand. Inquisitive — it pokes around, it asks questions. Peremptory — it gives orders, and it does not take argument. Hamilton is saying that Americans, who had just fought a revolution partly against exactly this kind of prying royal tax collection, were not going to sit quietly for a homegrown version of the same thing. The tax man in the barn was a non-starter.

So cross those off. Heavy direct taxes on land — the cash is not there. Excises — the people will not stand for the intrusion. What is left?

One thing. And it is the thing this whole paper has been walking toward. Taxes on trade. Duties on imported goods — a charge collected on foreign products as they arrive, at the dock, before they ever reach a store shelf.

Hamilton's case for the import duty is that it is the one tax almost nobody actually feels. It gets collected once, at the port, from the merchant bringing the goods in. The merchant folds the cost into his price. And by the time that cloth or sugar or rum reaches an ordinary buyer, the tax has simply become part of what things cost. There is no assessor at your door. No officer in your barn. No demand for coins you do not have. Hamilton calls it the imperceptible agency of taxes on consumption. Imperceptible — you cannot feel it. It is the painless tax. The tax that can fund a whole government without that government ever having to knock on a single citizen's door.

And here — just as Hamilton seems to have found the perfect tax — the paper turns, and delivers its real argument. Because there is a catch buried inside the import duty, and that catch is the entire point of Federalist Number Twelve.

An import duty is only worth something if you can actually collect it. And whether you can collect it depends, completely, on whether America is one country or many.

Here is the problem. A tax on imported goods creates, instantly, a reason to smuggle. If cloth is taxed at the legal port of entry, then cloth that slips in untaxed is cheaper cloth, and there is real money to be made getting it past the law. Every country that taxes trade has to fight this. The only question is how hard the fight is. And the answer to that depends on the shape of your borders.

So picture America broken into separate confederacies — Hamilton's nightmare, the scenario this whole series keeps circling back to. Say goods come in cheaply through one confederacy, lightly taxed, or barely taxed at all. What stops those goods from simply moving overland into the next confederacy, where the duties are high? Nothing much. Hamilton walks through why. The American states are stitched together by rivers and bays — natural roads, running in every direction. The people on both sides of any line speak the same language, share the same customs, and cross back and forth constantly out of plain daily habit.

Now you might ask — would Americans really smuggle goods past their own countrymen? These were neighbors, fellow former colonists, not foreign enemies. And Hamilton's answer is unsettling. Being neighbors is exactly what makes it easy. Smuggling between American confederacies would not even feel like smuggling. It would feel like an ordinary trip to see the people across the river.

And that does something brutal to a divided confederacy's finances. If the confederacy next door keeps its duties low, and your shared border is a sieve, then you cannot set your own duties high — because high duties would just send every importer to your neighbor's port and every smuggler across your shared river. Hamilton's phrase is that the separate states would be necessitated by mutual jealousy to keep their duties low. Necessitated. Forced. Trapped at low duties, which means trapped at low revenue. The one tax that could actually have funded them, neutralized — not by anyone's decision, but by the simple geometry of open land borders.

Could a divided confederacy fight back, and enforce high duties anyway? It could try. And here Hamilton hands his readers the most vivid picture in the paper. He points across the ocean, to France.

France, in the seventeen eighties, had exactly this problem — a country with long land borders against its neighbors, and duties it badly wanted to enforce. And so France maintained what Hamilton calls an army of patrols. That is not a figure of speech. It was a real, standing force of men whose entire job was to walk the internal roads and lines and catch smugglers. Hamilton reaches for a number, and he cites a serious source for it — Jacques Necker, the celebrated French finance minister, a man who had made his name laying the inner workings of the French treasury open for the public to read. By Necker's count, Hamilton says, those patrols numbered upwards of twenty thousand men. Twenty thousand. An army — that is Hamilton's own word — twenty thousand strong, existing for no other reason than to make a tax collectible.

And think about what an army like that means for the people who have to live under it. To catch smugglers moving over land, those patrols needed sweeping powers — to stop, to search, to seize, to enter. Hamilton calls those powers arbitrary and vexatious, and then he says the thing that matters most about them. Powers like that, he writes, would be intolerable in a free country. This is not merely an expense. It is a permanent police force, with the run of every road, pointed inward at a country's own citizens. That is the true price of collecting duties across open land borders. A divided America would face exactly that bill — pay for an internal army of tax patrols, or give up the revenue. Lose your money, or lose a piece of your freedom.

And now, finally, Hamilton plays his card. Because there is a third option on the table, and only one arrangement in the world unlocks it.

Stay one country.

If America is a single government, Hamilton says, then look at what you actually have to guard. Not a tangle of internal borders. Not a web of rivers and roads running between jealous neighbors. With one government covering all the states, there is, in Hamilton's words — and he capitalizes them on the page so the reader cannot miss it — but one side to guard, the Atlantic coast.

One side to guard. The Atlantic coast. That is the entire geography of the argument. A united America does not have a smuggling problem braided through its interior, because it has no taxed interior borders at all. It has a single ocean-facing edge. And an ocean is a wall. You cannot quietly slip a cargo ship loaded with taxable goods across three thousand miles of open Atlantic the way a smuggler could run a wagon across the line between France and its neighbors in a single dark night. The sheer distance does the enforcing for you.

So how much enforcement does that one coast really need? Hamilton's answer is almost gentle, and it is the loveliest line in the paper. A few armed vessels, he writes, judiciously stationed at the entrances of our ports, might at a small expense be made useful sentinels of the laws.

A few armed vessels. Judiciously stationed. At a small expense. Useful sentinels of the laws. Now set that beside France's twenty thousand patrols, and you can feel the whole argument click shut. Disunion means an internal army of tax men carrying powers that would be intolerable in a free country. Union means a handful of ships sitting quietly in the mouths of the harbors. Same tax. Same revenue. One version of America needs something close to a police state just to collect it. The other needs a small coast guard. The difference is not the tax. The difference is whether the country is one thing, or many.

If that sounds abstract, it is actually one of the most settled ideas in modern economics, and you can watch it operating right now. The European Union runs what is called a customs union. Goods get checked and charged once, when they cross the outer edge of the whole bloc — and then they move freely across every internal border after that, untaxed and uninspected, from one member country to the next. One outer edge to guard, and a free interior. That is Hamilton's argument, written into a modern treaty, two centuries later. The nations of Europe worked out, slowly and expensively, that a single shared external border is worth far more than every country forever policing its neighbors. Hamilton is telling Americans they can simply begin there.

A fair question at this point is just how much money this is really about. Is Hamilton promising a trickle, or a flood?

His numbers are striking. He says that up to now, American import duties have averaged less than three percent — barely a tax at all. In France, he reckons, the rate runs around fifteen percent. In Britain, higher still. And a united America, with its easy single coastline to guard, could safely triple its own duties, and the trade itself would barely feel it. He even works through one concrete example — a duty on imported spirits, on hard liquor. A tax of a shilling a gallon, on the millions of gallons coming into the country, could by itself raise something on the order of two hundred thousand pounds. And, Hamilton adds, with a slightly moralizing tone, if that tax happened to make Americans drink a little less, so much the better for the morals and the health of the country. Hamilton the financier and Hamilton the schoolmaster, in a single sentence.

So — step back. Why does any of this matter? Why did Hamilton give a whole paper, and why are we giving a whole episode, to the plumbing of tax collection?

Because Hamilton ends Federalist Number Twelve by raising the stakes about as high as they can go. He asks, point blank, what happens to a country that cannot solve this — that never manages to find a tax it is actually able to collect. And his answer is one of the bleakest, cleanest lines in the entire series. A nation, he writes, cannot long exist without revenues. Destitute of this essential support, it must resign its independence, and sink into the degraded condition of a province.

Sink into the degraded condition of a province. Hear what Hamilton is actually saying. A country that cannot fund itself does not get to stay a struggling-but-independent country. It stops being a country at all. It cannot pay an army, so it cannot defend itself. It cannot pay its debts, so no one will lend to it. It grows weak enough that some stronger power simply absorbs it, or quietly owns it — and it slides down from being a nation into being a province. A possession. A thing that other countries manage. Revenue, in Hamilton's hands, is not accounting. Revenue is sovereignty. The ability to collect money is the ability to remain a country at all.

And he closes by spelling out what the alternative does to ordinary people. If a divided America cannot tax its trade, the need for money does not politely vanish. The government still has bills to pay. So the whole weight comes back down, Hamilton says, with oppressive weight upon land. Back onto the farmers. Back to demanding hard cash from people who do not have it. Back, in other words, onto the road that led to Shays' Rebellion — except now it is permanent, and there is not even a full treasury at the end of it to show for the suffering. Public and private distress, Hamilton says, would keep pace with each other in gloomy concert.

That is the choice Federalist Number Twelve lays on the table. Union: a painless tax on trade, a small coast guard to protect it, and a funded, sovereign government. Disunion: a tax you cannot collect, or a police state to collect it, or the entire burden crushing down on the farms — and at the end of that road, the degraded condition of a province.

Now, it is worth being honest about Hamilton here, because this is a paper where his prophecy and his own future crash straight into each other.

On the big argument, Hamilton was simply right, and history proved it fast. Once the Constitution was ratified, the new federal government did exactly what Federalist Number Twelve described. It funded itself, overwhelmingly, on customs duties — taxes on imported goods, collected at the ports, by sentinels of the laws. And it kept doing it. For most of the next century, deep into the eighteen hundreds, the duties on trade were the main thing paying for the entire government of the United States. The income tax — the thing we now assume pays for everything — did not become a permanent fixture until more than a century after this paper was written. Hamilton did not just predict the federal revenue system. He designed it, and the design held for a hundred years.

But here is the irony, and it is a sharp one. Remember Hamilton's line about excises — that the genius of the people will ill brook the inquisitive and peremptory spirit of excise laws. That Americans would never tolerate the tax man inside the barn. Hamilton wrote that warning. And then, a few years later, as the first Secretary of the Treasury, Hamilton ignored it. In seventeen ninety-one he pushed through a federal excise — an internal tax — on whiskey. And the western farmers, who distilled their grain into whiskey precisely because whiskey was far easier to carry and sell than sacks of grain, reacted exactly the way Federalist Number Twelve had said they would. They resisted. They menaced the tax collectors. And by seventeen ninety-four it had boiled over into an armed uprising — the Whiskey Rebellion — and President Washington had to raise a militia army of some thirteen thousand men and ride out at its head to put it down. Hamilton himself rode along with the troops.

Think about that. Hamilton, in this very paper, told the country in plain words that Americans would not stand for excise taxes. Then he won the most powerful financial office in the country, imposed one anyway, and got precisely the rebellion he had forecast. He turned out to be right twice over. He was right that quiet duties on imported trade would fund the nation for a century. And he was right that excises would provoke a fight. He simply did not take his own second piece of advice.

So step back and see what Hamilton has built across these last two papers. Federalist Number Eleven made commerce the source of American power. Federalist Number Twelve makes commerce the source of American revenue — the money that lets a government exist at all. Earn it, then collect it. Two papers, and the economic case for the union is most of the way constructed.

Next time, Hamilton keeps his eye on money, but he turns from raising it to spending it. Federalist Number Thirteen is his again, and it is one of the shortest papers in the whole series. Its question is blunt and practical. Which is actually cheaper to run — one national government, or three or four separate confederacies, each with its own full set of officials, its own treasury, its own army, its own everything? Hamilton's answer is not going to surprise you. But the way he adds up the cost is worth hearing. We have spent two episodes now on how the union brings money in. Next time, on how it keeps from pouring money away.
