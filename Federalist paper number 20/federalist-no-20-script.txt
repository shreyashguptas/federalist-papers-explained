Last time, we kept walking through Madison's graveyard.

He had already laid out the ancient dead for us, two Greek confederacies on the table, and last episode he opened the modern half of the file and showed us three more bodies. The Holy Roman Empire: a thousand years old, a glittering list of powers written on parchment, and underneath all of it a nerveless body, too slow to defend itself, too weak to discipline its own strong members, so feeble that foreign powers reached into its very constitution and wrote themselves in. Poland: the same design followed all the way to the end, a nation of jealous nobles so paralyzed that its neighbors could carve it up alive, and soon erase it from the map entirely. And Switzerland, the apparent exception, held together not by good design but by mountains and poverty and fear, and cracking the moment a real question, religion, finally put it to the test.

Different centuries. Different costumes. The same disease every single time.

And right at the end of that episode, Madison told us there was one body left on the table. One last confederacy to examine. And he had saved it for last on purpose, because it was the hardest one to look at. It was the one the Americans loved.

This is The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Twenty. Federalist Number Twenty, which appeared in the New York Packet on the eleventh of December, seventeen eighty-seven, signed once more by Hamilton and Madison together, with Madison still holding the pen and still working out of that extraordinary notebook of dead confederacies he had filled before he ever got to Philadelphia. This is the last stop on the tour. And it is the most personal one.

Because the confederacy Madison turns to today is the Dutch republic. The United Provinces of the Netherlands.

To understand why this one stung, you have to understand what the Dutch republic was to an American in seventeen eighty-seven. So let me slow down and tell you, because this is the part the paper itself takes for granted, and we shouldn't.

Picture a little stretch of low, wet land on the edge of the North Sea, half of it practically clawed back from the water. In the fifteen hundreds, the people there were subjects of the Spanish crown, which at that moment ran the most powerful empire on the face of the earth. And these people, a small, stubborn, mostly Protestant nation of merchants and sailors, did something almost insane. They rebelled. A handful of waterlogged provinces stood up against the global superpower of the age. And after a brutal war that dragged on, with pauses, for the better part of eighty years, they won. They made themselves free.

And then they did something even more astonishing than winning their independence. This tiny, soggy country became one of the richest nations on earth, with what may have been the highest standard of living in Europe. Amsterdam turned into the banker of Europe. The Dutch built a merchant fleet so enormous that, by some counts, it carried close to half of all the shipping in Europe. They created what is often called the world's first publicly traded company, the Dutch East India Company, and one of the first modern public banks. And they were a refuge: more tolerant, by a wide margin, than almost anywhere else in Europe, a place that gave shelter to Protestants and Catholics and Jews and dissenters of every stripe at a time when much of the continent was still burning people for less. Here is a detail that should land for an American audience: the Pilgrims, the ones who would later sail on the Mayflower, spent years living in the Dutch town of Leiden before they ever set eyes on Plymouth Rock. The Dutch had made a country where they could pray as they pleased.

So for Americans, the Dutch republic was the proof that the whole thing was possible. Here was a republic, not a kingdom, that had thrown off a great empire, governed itself, grown fabulously rich, and stood for generations. If you wanted to argue that a free people could rule themselves and prosper without a king, you pointed across the ocean at the Dutch. They were the older sister. The model. And it was not just admiration: when the Americans went looking for friends and loans to fund their own revolution, it was Dutch bankers who opened their purses.

So when Madison lays this republic down on the table, right next to dismembered Poland and the dying Holy Roman Empire, he is not picking on some far-off curiosity. He is laying his hand on the one example his readers most wanted to believe in. He is saying: even this one. Even the sister republic. Even the best of them was carrying the disease.

And you might be wondering why a New Yorker, trying to decide how to vote on a brand-new constitution, should care about the inner plumbing of a small country across the ocean. Here is why. The Dutch were the closest thing the modern world had to America itself. A union of separate, proud, self-governing little republics, loosely tied together, all trying to act as one nation. If the disease had reached them, of all people, then it could reach anyone. It could reach us.

So Madison does what he always does. He starts with the parchment.

On paper, the Dutch union looked like a real government. It was a confederacy of seven sovereign provinces. But here is the twist that made it even more brittle than the others on the tour: each of those seven provinces was itself a bundle of independent cities, and on anything that really mattered, not only did the seven provinces have to agree, the cities inside them had to agree too. There was a central assembly, the States General, where the provinces sent their deputies. There was an executive officer at the head of the union called the stadtholder. And on paper, that central assembly could do all the things a country needs to do. It could make war and peace, sign treaties and alliances, raise armies and fit out fleets, and set each province's share of the common bills. A real government, written down.

Madison even gives the thing a grand old name. He calls it the celebrated Belgic confederacy. And don't let that word "Belgic" confuse you, it has nothing to do with the modern country of Belgium. It is the old Roman name for the whole region of the Low Countries. He means the Dutch.

And then he asks the same cold question he asked of the Holy Roman Empire. Such is the confederacy as drawn on parchment, he says. What are the characters that practice has actually stamped on it? And his answer is a single grim line, four hammer blows in a row.

"Imbecility in the government; discord among the provinces; foreign influence and indignities; a precarious existence in peace, and peculiar calamities from war."

That is the whole autopsy in one sentence. A government too weak to function. Provinces constantly at each other's throats. Foreigners reaching in and shoving it around. And a republic so shaky it was unsteady even in peacetime, and uniquely battered whenever a war came. Everything the world admired about the Dutch from the outside, the wealth, the ships, the tolerance, and on the inside, the same rot we have watched in every other confederacy on this tour.

So how did this thing actually work, day to day? That is a fair question, especially since this so-called imbecile government somehow presided over the wealthiest country in Europe.

The deepest flaw was that unanimity rule. Everything important needed every province, and inside each province every city, to say yes. Which meant any one of them, even a small and poor one, could freeze the entire machine by simply refusing. If that sounds familiar, it should. It is the Holy Roman Empire's paralysis all over again, the enemy already in the field before the council can agree on how to fight. But the Dutch found a particularly revealing way to feel that paralysis. Through money.

The constitution said each province owed a fixed share of the common expenses, the army, the navy, the national debt. Sensible enough. Except the inland provinces, the poor ones with little trade, often simply could not pay their share. And there was no power on earth that could force them to. So what actually happened? One single rich province, Holland, the province with Amsterdam sitting inside it, which all by itself paid well over half of the entire union's bills, would quietly cover not just its own share but the missing shares of the others, just to keep the army standing and the creditors paid. And then Holland would spend its time, year after grinding year, trying to claw that money back out of the provinces that had stiffed it, by sending delegations to plead, by pressure, by whatever it could manage.

And sometimes pleading was not enough. And here Madison hands us one of the most chilling little facts in the whole paper. When a province flatly refused to pay what it owed, the missing money, the deficiency, in his word, had to be collected another way.

It "had to be ultimately collected at the point of the bayonet."

Stop on that. Soldiers. Sent by the union, marched into a member province, to extract its unpaid dues at gunpoint. This is not a war against some foreign enemy. This is the central government collecting back taxes from one of its own members with an army. The union of the proud Dutch republic, the sister of American liberty, balanced its books, when it came right down to it, with bayonets pointed at its own provinces.

And then Madison adds the detail that turns this from a grim curiosity into a warning aimed straight between his American reader's eyes. He says this horrible method only worked at all, only stayed, in his phrase, practicable though dreadful, because of one specific fact about the Dutch. Holland was so overwhelmingly stronger than all the other provinces combined that the others simply did not dare to fight back. The bayonet worked because its victims were too weak to resist it.

But, Madison says, now imagine a confederacy that is not built that way. Imagine a union whose members are roughly equal to one another in size and strength, several of them each big enough to put up a real, stubborn, sustained fight. In that union, sending the army to collect a debt from a member is not a dreadful-but-workable last resort. It is the first morning of a civil war.

Now hold that thought right up next to the United States. Thirteen states, several of them, Virginia, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Madison's reader's own New York, large and proud and perfectly able to resist. The Dutch trick, the strong province collecting from the weak ones at bayonet point, could never even function in America, because America had no single giant member to play Holland's role. Which means a confederacy of American states that ever tried to enforce itself by force would not get the grim Dutch outcome of sullen order. It would get war. And that, right there, is the engine of this whole stretch of the Federalist. A government that can only enforce its laws by marching soldiers against its own members is a government that, the very moment those members are strong enough to say no, has exactly one move left on the board. And that move is bloodshed.

You can hear the modern echo of this in just about any alliance where a few wealthy members quietly carry the rest. Think of any union today where the rich partners pick up the tab for the ones who will not pay their share, and then simmer with resentment about the free-riders who do not pull their weight. That resentment is real, and it is old. The Dutch were living it three hundred years ago, with wealthy Holland footing the bill and then chasing six deadbeat provinces for the reimbursement. The only difference is that in their world, the final collection notice was a column of armed men.

There is a second flaw, and it grows right out of the first. A government that needs everyone to agree is a government that a clever outsider can freeze by buying off just one member. And the Dutch suffered from this constantly.

Madison brings in a witness here, and a good one: Sir William Temple, the English diplomat who had actually served as an ambassador to the Dutch and knew the place from the inside out. And Temple's testimony is damning. Foreign ambassadors, he reports, quickly learned that the easy way to block any Dutch decision they did not like was simply to reach right past the central government and tamper directly with the individual provinces and cities. Flatter them, bribe them, work on their little local jealousies, until one of them dug in its heels and vetoed the whole thing. In seventeen twenty-six, Madison notes, a major treaty was held up by exactly this maneuver for an entire year. One foreign power, working on one province at a time, jamming the gears of a whole nation.

And if that sounds like a quaint old problem, look at how great powers operate on unions of states in our own time. When a bloc can only act if every single member agrees, the surest way for an outside power to stop that bloc from acting against you is not to confront the whole group. It is to find the one member you can flatter or bribe or lean on, and get that one member to hold everything up. A single purchased vote is far cheaper than an army, and just as effective. The Dutch discovered that weakness the hard way, and it is alive and thriving in the diplomacy of the twenty-first century. A union that needs everyone to say yes can be stopped cold by anyone who can talk one member into saying no.

So how did the Dutch republic survive at all? If it was this paralyzed, this easily preyed upon, this prone to settling its own accounts with bayonets, what on earth held it together?

And here we reach the strangest and most revealing fact about the entire arrangement. Because the thing that kept the Dutch republic from simply flying apart was not some clever piece of constitutional engineering. It was a prince.

The Dutch had that officer at the head of the union, the stadtholder. And by Madison's day, that office had quietly hardened into a hereditary throne in everything but name, held by one family, the House of Orange, and handed down by inheritance like a crown. The stadtholder commanded the armies and the navy, he appointed officials, he sat at the dead center of everything. In a republic that existed to prove that a free people did not need a king, the one part that actually made the machine turn over was a man who was a king in all but the title.

Madison quotes a French writer, the Abbe de Mably, on precisely this point.

"Under such a government," says the Abbe de Mably, "the Union could never have subsisted, if the provinces had not a spring within themselves, capable of quickening their tardiness, and compelling them to the same way of thinking. This spring is the stadtholder."

A spring. A coiled piece of machinery buried inside the works that forces the lazy parts to move and bullies the squabbling parts into line. And that spring, Mably says, is the prince. Read that honestly and it is a devastating admission. The Dutch confederacy could only function because it had quietly grown a near-monarch inside itself to do the compelling that its own constitution simply could not do. The cure for the anarchy of too many sovereigns turned out to be a dose of the very thing a republic was built to be free of, the concentrated power of one man.

And that is one of the quietest and most important lessons in this entire stretch of the Federalist. A union too weak to govern itself does not just sit there, weak and harmless. It generates a hunger for someone, anyone, who can cut through the deadlock and make the thing finally move. The Dutch fed that hunger with a hereditary prince. Madison is warning his readers that a chronically frozen government does not stay a charming, harmless mess forever. It manufactures the demand for a strongman. You can watch that same hunger surface in just about any system today that seizes up often enough. Sooner or later, people stop asking for better rules and start asking for a strong hand. Weakness at the center is not the opposite of one-man rule. It is the runway for it.

And here is where this paper stops being history and becomes the news. Because at the very moment Madison was writing these words, in the closing weeks of seventeen eighty-seven, the Dutch republic was not a dusty cautionary tale at all. It was on fire.

The country had split into two armed camps. On one side stood a movement that called itself the Patriots, pushing to strip power back from the prince and finally reform the rotten old system. On the other side stood the supporters of the stadtholder, the House of Orange. And the quarrel had curdled into something very close to civil war. The stadtholder had been driven out of the center of the country. His wife, who happened to be a Prussian princess, the sister of the King of Prussia, was stopped and detained by Patriot militia as she tried to travel. And her brother the king took that insult as his opening. In the autumn of seventeen eighty-seven, only months before Madison sat down to write this paper, a Prussian army came pouring across the border, crushed the Patriots, and shoved the prince back onto his perch by brute force.

Sit with that picture for a second. The republic that Americans most admired, the proud, free, self-governing Dutch, was at that exact moment convulsed by faction, its government broken, and a foreign king's army marching through its streets to install the ruler of his choice. Madison did not have to reach back into ancient history for his warning this time. He could practically point out the window. This unhappy people, he writes, are suffering all at once from popular convulsions, from quarrels among their own states, and from the actual invasion of foreign arms, the crisis of their destiny, with every nation on earth watching the awful spectacle.

And then Madison does something you really do not expect from a man in the middle of building a tight legal argument. He stops arguing, for just a moment, and he grieves.

You might think this would be his moment to gloat. To say, you see, I told you, look at the wreck of your beloved Dutch model. He does the exact opposite. He looks at the suffering of this sister republic and what comes out of him sounds almost like a prayer. His first wish, he says, is that the Dutch might somehow come through this catastrophe and build a real, working union out of the ruins. And his second wish is tender, and it is aimed straight back home: that the safe and steady country now taking shape here in America might one day be an asylum, his word, a refuge that could take in those battered Dutch republicans and console them for the wreck of their own. The argument has a heart beating inside it. The same man coldly dissecting the Dutch republic also hopes that America can become the lifeboat for the people drowning inside it.

And there is one more detail Madison cannot let go of, because it cuts so close to the bone of his own work. If the flaws were this obvious, you might ask, why on earth didn't the Dutch just fix them? And the answer is that they tried. Madison is at pains to tell us that the best Dutch patriots had seen these flaws clearly for generations, and had attempted, no fewer than four separate times, calling special assemblies for the single purpose, to reform the system. And every single time, they failed. They could never get the very councils that needed reforming to agree to reform themselves. The thing that needed fixing was the same thing blocking the fix.

And that, for Madison, is the most personal note in the whole paper. Because he had just walked out of Philadelphia, out of a convention that had somehow managed to do the exact thing the Dutch never could. Get a fractious collection of jealous, sovereign states to sit together in one room and agree on a new and better frame of government. He calls the lesson of the Dutch failure a melancholy and monitory lesson, a sad and cautionary one, and he tells his fellow citizens to pause over it and be grateful. Because that small miracle of agreement that produced the Constitution was exactly the miracle the Dutch had reached for four separate times and never once caught.

And now, having laid the last body on the table, Madison gathers up everything. All of it. Every confederacy on the whole long tour. And he delivers the verdict that this entire stretch of history was built to reach.

He starts by flatly refusing to apologize for how long he has lingered over all these dead governments. Because the dead, he says, are the finest teachers we have.

"Experience is the oracle of truth; and where its responses are unequivocal, they ought to be conclusive and sacred."

An oracle, in the ancient world, was the place you traveled to in order to hear the actual voice of a god. The final word. Not to be argued with. And Madison is saying: forget theory, forget what ought to work in some philosopher's neat diagram. Look instead at what has actually happened, every time, to every government ever built this particular way. The historical record is the oracle. And when that record returns the same answer over and over without one single exception, when Greece and Germany and Poland and Switzerland and now the proud Dutch all hand back the identical verdict, then that verdict is not a guess anymore. It is a law of political nature. And you ignore it the way you would ignore a god's warning. At your peril.

And what is the answer the oracle keeps giving? Here it comes, the sentence this whole arc has been climbing toward ever since Hamilton first named the disease back in Number Fifteen. The fatal mistake, every time, in every century, is building this:

"a sovereignty over sovereigns, a government over governments, a legislation for communities, as contradistinguished from individuals."

Let me take that apart slowly, because it is the beating heart of the entire Federalist Papers. A sovereignty over sovereigns. A government whose subjects are other governments. A set of laws aimed at communities, at states and provinces and cantons and cities, instead of at flesh-and-blood individual people. That, Madison says, is the original sin of every confederacy on his list. He calls it a contradiction sitting right inside the idea, a thing that cannot work even in theory, a square circle drawn in ink. And in actual practice, he says, it is worse than merely useless. It destroys the entire point of having a government in the first place. Because a government that can only ever command other governments has, in the end, exactly one tool left when a member looks it in the eye and says no. Not a courtroom. Not a sheriff. An army. Madison lands the whole argument in his last and sharpest line. A government over governments, he says, can only enforce itself by

"substituting violence in place of law, or the destructive coercion of the sword in place of the mild and salutary coercion of the magistracy."

This is the hinge the whole series turns on, so let me lay it out as plainly as I possibly can. There are only two ways a government can ever make you obey it. One is the sword. Soldiers, force, war. The other is what Madison calls the magistracy, which is just his word for ordinary law. The judge, the sheriff, the summons, the quiet everyday machinery that reaches one citizen at a time, and that almost every one of us obeys without a single drop of blood being spilled. He calls that second kind mild and salutary, gentle, and good for you. A normal government, one that reaches individual people directly, governs with the magistrate. But a government that can only ever reach whole states, that has no grip at all on the individual citizen, has thrown the magistrate away and kept nothing but the sword. Every confederacy on this entire tour proved it in blood. When the Holy Roman Empire wanted to discipline one small city, it sent ten thousand troops to Donauworth. When the Dutch union wanted its unpaid dues, it sent the bayonet. They had no other instrument, because they had no hold whatsoever on the individual. The sword was all they had ever owned.

So that is the verdict. After two full episodes and five dead confederacies, the Amphictyonic Council and the Achaean League in the ancient world, the Holy Roman Empire and Poland and the Dutch in the modern one, with Switzerland thrown in as the exception that turned out not to be one, after every bit of it, the oracle's answer is a single sentence. A government over governments rots, every time, into anarchy among its members and the sword against them. And you cannot save it by writing it a longer list of powers. The Holy Roman Empire had a magnificent list, pages of it, and it was a corpse. The Dutch had the wealth of the entire world flowing through Amsterdam, and they still balanced their books with bayonets.

And the cure, the one thing every single one of these confederacies lacked, the one thing the men back in Philadelphia had finally managed to build, was never a sharper sword. It was the magistrate. A government whose laws would skip right past the member states and land directly on the individual citizen, so that being obeyed would never again require making war on a member to get it. A tax the government collects from a person, instead of a demand it mails to a province and prays it honors. A law a judge applies to you, instead of a polite request the union sends to your state and hopes for the best. That single shift, from governing governments to governing people, is the entire difference between the parchment corpses of history and the thing the Constitution was straining to become.

And with that, the tour is finally over. The graveyard has been walked end to end. Madison and Hamilton have laid out every confederacy worth examining, ancient and modern, pagan and Christian, north and south, and shown that all of them died of the very same thing. The argument from history is complete.

Which means it is time, at last, to come home. For five straight papers now, Publius has been pointing across the ocean and back across the centuries, saying look, look what happened to all of them. Starting next time, Hamilton turns the lamp around and aims it squarely at us. He stops touring the failures of foreigners and begins naming, one by one, the specific, concrete, embarrassing defects in America's own Articles of Confederation, the actual broken government the thirteen states were living under at that very moment. The autopsy of strangers is finished. The diagnosis of the patient sitting right there in the room is about to begin.
