At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had stared down the scariest word in the American political vocabulary and refused to blink. A standing army in peacetime. Soldiers kept under arms, paid by the government, in a season when no war is being fought. To the founding generation, that phrase was very nearly the definition of tyranny, and the new Constitution did not ban it.

Hamilton's answer, across Federalist Number Twenty-Four, came in two moves. First he argued that the objection was almost dishonest, because the new plan was actually more careful about armies than the state constitutions or the old government had ever been. And then he argued that a flat ban would be foolish anyway, because a young country boxed in by Britain and Spain genuinely needed frontier troops and a navy. The real safeguard, he said, was never to forbid the army. It was to leash it. The money that pays for it runs out every two years, so the people's own elected representatives have to vote, out in the open, to keep it alive.

But there was a fallback waiting for him. And it is the objection that opens today's paper.

Because a clever critic could accept every word Hamilton had just said and still hand him a problem. All right, the critic says. Maybe somebody does need to keep troops. Maybe this country really is too exposed to go entirely without soldiers. But why does it have to be the national government that holds them? Let the states raise and keep the troops instead, under the general direction of the Union. Keep the guns close to home, in the hands of the government right down the road, the one you can see and watch and vote out, instead of a distant national capital you will never lay eyes on.

It sounds like the reasonable middle path. It sounds like the compromise that hands you defense without handing you a national army to be afraid of. And Hamilton's entire purpose in Federalist Number Twenty-Five is to convince you that this apparently safer option is, in truth, the more dangerous one.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-Five. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. It ran in the New York Packet on Friday, the twenty-first of December, seventeen eighty-seven, only days after the last one. And its title tells you it is a continuation. The same subject. The powers necessary to the common defense, considered further. Number Twenty-Four asked whether the nation may keep an army at all. Number Twenty-Five asks a sharper question. Who should hold it, the union or the states? And then, before it is finished, it circles back to close out the argument Twenty-Four left open, about whether you can ever safely write a ban on armies into a constitution in the first place.

Let me start where the danger actually sits, because once you see the map, Hamilton's whole case falls into place.

Picture the United States in seventeen eighty-seven, and be honest about the neighborhood. To the north, behind the states and stretching off into the interior, sits British territory, with British soldiers still holding a chain of frontier forts. To the south and to the west sits Spain, holding Florida, the vast Louisiana country, and the city of New Orleans, and with it the mouth of the Mississippi. And all along the frontier live the Native nations of the interior. Now here is the thing Hamilton wants you to notice about that danger. It does not press on one state. It wraps around the whole country. In his words, these territories do not border on particular states, but "encircle the Union from Maine to Georgia."

From the northern edge of Maine down to Georgia. The entire length of the country, ringed by potential trouble. And that single geographic fact carries Hamilton's first argument all by itself. If the danger belongs to everyone, then paying to guard against it has to belong to everyone too. It ought, he says, to be the business of common councils and a common treasury. The defense of the whole cannot be quietly dumped onto whichever states happen to sit on the exposed edge.

And to show you why, Hamilton points straight at his own state, because New York is exactly one of those exposed edges.

Imagine the plan the critics want, where each state provides for its own defense. New York sits on the northern frontier, right up against the British. So New York would have to build and pay for the whole military establishment needed to protect New York. Fine, you might say, that is only fair. But look at what actually happens next. Those forts and troops on New York's northern line are not just protecting New York. They are the shield standing between the British and every state tucked in behind New York. When New York garrisons its frontier, it is also, for free, protecting Connecticut, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, the whole interior that never has to face the British directly because New York is in the way.

So New York pays the entire bill and its inland neighbors get the protection for nothing. That, Hamilton says, is neither fair to New York nor safe for anybody else. And here is the part that should worry the neighbors most of all. Under that arrangement, the safety of the whole country would hang on whether one or two exposed border states are willing and able to foot an enormous bill, year after year. In his phrase, "the security of all would thus be subjected to the parsimony, improvidence, or inability of a part."

Sit with how sharp that is. The safety of every state now depends on the stingiest, or the poorest, or the worst-run of the handful of states stuck on the frontier. If New York has a lean year and cannot pay, the hole in New York's line is a hole in everyone's line. You have taken the defense of a whole nation and made it hostage to the budget troubles of its border states. That is not a defense system. That is a single point of failure with thirteen states standing behind it.

So why does that matter beyond the accounting? Because it does not stay an accounting problem. It curdles into something worse, and this is where Hamilton connects Federalist Number Twenty-Five to everything this series has been warning about since the very beginning.

Suppose the exposed states do rise to it. Suppose New York, and one or two others like it, build up real military strength to guard those frontiers. Now the rest of the country looks up and sees something alarming. It sees, in Hamilton's words, the whole military force of the Union sitting in the hands of two or three of its members, and probably the most powerful members at that. How do you think the other states react to that sight? Exactly the way neighboring states have reacted to a strong neighbor in every episode of this series so far. With envy, and with fear. They would each want a counterweight of their own. And pretexts for building one, Hamilton says drily, could always be contrived. Nobody who wants an army has ever struggled to find a reason for it.

And now watch the thing spiral. Each state, jealous of the others, builds up its own forces. Each buildup frightens the neighbors into building up further. You end up not with one national army under common control, but with a dozen state armies, each swollen past any real need, each feeding on suspicion of the others. Hamilton says these establishments, "nourished by mutual jealousy," would swell far beyond their natural size. And because each one answers to its own state government rather than to the nation, they become something genuinely dangerous. In his words, they would be "engines for the abridgment or demolition of the national authority."

Engines for the demolition of the national authority. Read that plainly. Thirteen states, each with its own army, each with its own grievances, and no national government strong enough to hold any of them in check, because the guns all belong to the states. This is Federalist Number Six and Number Seven come to life with weapons in its hands. Back then, Hamilton warned that separate American states would drift into rivalry and war. Here he is showing you the accelerant. Hand each of those jealous, rivalrous states its own standing army, and you have not made the country safer. You have armed the very quarrel that could tear it apart.

At this point you might be waiting for Hamilton to answer the objection that started all this, the emotional one. Because the whole appeal of letting the states hold the troops was that it felt safer for your liberty. A national army sounds like a boot on your neck. Your own state's soldiers sound like your neighbors. So even if the state-by-state plan is unfair and unstable, is it not still gentler on freedom? Is your liberty not safer with the army close to home?

Hamilton's answer to that is the most brilliant thing in the paper, and it is worth slowing all the way down for, because it turns your intuition completely inside out.

He says the liberty of the people would actually be less safe with the army in the hands of the states than in the hands of the national government. Less safe. The opposite of what it feels like. And his reasoning is a small masterpiece of political psychology.

Start with a simple premise, he says. An army is a dangerous weapon of power no matter who is holding it. That part does not change. So the real question is never whether someone holds a dangerous weapon. Someone always will. The question is, in whose hands are the people watching most closely?

And here is the move. You watch the distant national government like a hawk. It is far away, it is unfamiliar, it is exactly the kind of power a free people is trained by instinct to be suspicious of. Every move it makes, you scrutinize. Your own state government, though, you trust. It is familiar. It is close. It feels like yours. And so you barely watch it at all. Which means, Hamilton says, that a weapon in the hands of your beloved local government is far more dangerous to you than the same weapon in the hands of the national government you already distrust. Because the guard you never watch is the one who can rob you blind.

He states it as a law drawn from the whole record of history. "The people are always most in danger when the means of injuring their rights are in the possession of those of whom they entertain the least suspicion."

Let that land, because it is one of the deepest sentences in the entire Federalist Papers. The threat to your freedom is not the power you fear. You are watching that one. The threat is the power you trust. It is the authority so familiar and so friendly that you stopped keeping an eye on it. Hand an army to the government you love and never question, and you have handed it to the one actor in your whole political life who could turn it against you before you ever thought to look. Hamilton is telling a people who feared the distant capital that their real blind spot was the government in their own backyard.

And notice what this does to the critics' clever compromise. Their entire pitch was that keeping the army close to home makes it safer. Hamilton has just shown that keeping it close to home, in the hands you least suspect, is exactly what makes it most able to hurt you. The comfort was the trap.

He adds one more turn of the screw. He reminds his readers that when a state government and the national government come into conflict, ordinary people tend to side with their state, the government they feel closer to. So now imagine that state, already the sentimental favorite in any showdown with the union, also holding its own army. That is not a check on federal overreach. That is a standing temptation for an ambitious state to take a run at the national government and win. Give the rivalrous state both the people's loyalty and a private army, and you have built the perfect machine for pulling the union apart from the inside.

And then Hamilton drops a quiet fact that turns the critics' proposal into something close to absurd. This idea of the states holding their own military forces, he points out, is not some daring new liberty the Constitution would take away. The government Americans are living under right now already forbids it. The Articles of Confederation, the current national government, expressly bar the individual states from keeping warships or troops without the consent of Congress. So the men who wrote the confederation, weak as it was, already understood the danger of state armies and wrote a rule against it. The critics are demanding, as a safeguard for liberty, the very thing the existing government already treats as a threat to the union.

So that is the first half of the paper. The states cannot be handed the common defense. It would be unfair to the exposed states, it would make everyone's safety hostage to a few border budgets, it would breed exactly the armed rivalry this series has warned about from the start, and, most surprising of all, it would put the army in the hands least watched and therefore most dangerous to liberty. The apparently safe option is the ruinous one.

Now Hamilton pivots. Because behind the states-should-do-it proposal was a deeper demand that he still has to answer. The critics really wanted the Constitution to simply ban peacetime armies outright. Twenty-Four argued that ban would be unwise. Twenty-Five now argues something more devastating. It argues the ban would not even work. And to prove it, Hamilton stops being a philosopher and starts being a lawyer.

He asks a question so simple it is almost rude. This ban you keep demanding, on standing armies in time of peace, what exactly would it forbid? Because there are two very different things it could mean, and the critics have never bothered to say which.

Take the softer version first. Say the ban only forbids keeping an army up in peacetime, but still lets the government raise one when trouble appears. Fine, says Hamilton. Now define your terms. Once an army has been raised, at what point does keeping it around become illegal? A week after the danger passes? A month? A year? And who decides when the danger has actually passed? Somebody has to make that call, and it can only be the national government itself. So the government raises troops against a threat, and then keeps them exactly as long as it judges the threat to persist. Which means the ban forbids nothing at all. It is a rule with a hole in the middle wide enough to march an army through. The government simply says the danger has not passed yet, and the troops stay.

So the soft ban is meaningless. Which pushes the critics toward the hard version. All right, they say, then forbid even raising an army in peacetime. No troops at all until war has actually begun. And this is where Hamilton delivers the killer line of the paper, the one that should end the argument.

A country with that rule, he says, would put on "the most extraordinary spectacle which the world has yet seen." It would be "a nation incapacitated by its Constitution to prepare for defense, before it was actually invaded."

Think about what that means in practice. In Hamilton's day, and increasingly so, nations no longer bothered with the old ceremony of formally declaring war before they attacked. War just arrived. So a country forbidden to raise troops until it was actually invaded would have to stand there and wait for the enemy to physically appear on its soil before it was legally allowed to so much as begin recruiting. You could see the storm gathering. You could watch the fleets being fitted out across the ocean. And your own constitution would forbid you to lift a finger until the blow had already fallen. In Hamilton's unforgettable phrase, "We must receive the blow, before we could even prepare to return it."

We must receive the blow before we can prepare to return it. That is the whole absurdity in nine words. Every sane instinct of self-preservation, the entire logic by which nations watch a distant danger and get ready before it arrives, would be constitutionally forbidden. And why? Hamilton names the reason with real scorn. Because we are so afraid that our own rulers, and remember who these rulers are, they are, in his words, "created by our choice, dependent on our will," might someday abuse an army, that we would rather leave ourselves naked to foreign invaders than trust the officials we ourselves elected and can vote out. We would hand ourselves to our enemies out of fear of our own representatives. That is the bargain the hard ban actually offers.

If you have ever heard the argument that a country should not keep a large military in peacetime, that it should stay lean and mobilize only when war actually comes, Hamilton is answering you across two hundred and forty years. His answer is that by the time you can see the blow coming, it is too late to start getting ready, and the nations that waited to prepare until they were actually hit have tended to pay for that wait in blood. The clearest proof came long after Hamilton was gone. In nineteen forty-one, the United States had spent years arguing that it could hang back, stay out, and prepare later if it ever truly had to. Then the blow fell at Pearl Harbor on a December morning, and the country discovered exactly what it costs to start preparing after you have already been struck. Hamilton would not have been surprised for one second. Meet the gathering storm, he says, or the storm meets you.

Now you might ask the most patriotic question of all, the one every American of that era wanted to ask. Why do we need a professional army for any of this? We have the militia. Ordinary citizens, farmers and tradesmen, who keep a musket at home and turn out to defend their country when she calls. That was the beloved American ideal, the armed free citizen, and the whole revolution had just been fought partly in its name. Why not simply trust the militia to be, as people loved to say, the natural bulwark of the nation?

Hamilton's answer here is blunt to the point of bravery, because he is about to tell a nation of proud militiamen something they did not want to hear. That comforting doctrine, he says, "had like to have lost us our independence." It nearly cost America the very war it had just won. And it did cost, in his estimate, millions that a real army would have saved.

He is careful here, and it matters. He is not sneering at the militia. He goes out of his way to say that America's militia, in the war just ended, had by their courage "erected eternal monuments to their fame." The bravery was real and he honors it. But, he says, the bravest militiamen themselves knew the truth. They knew that their courage alone could never have won the country's independence. Something more was needed, and that something was a trained, permanent, professional force. Because war, Hamilton says, in a line that has aged into plain wisdom, "is a science to be acquired and perfected by diligence, by perseverance, by time, and by practice."

War is a science. You do not get good at it by being brave for a weekend and going home. A part-time farmer with a musket, however willing, cannot stand in the open field against professional soldiers who do nothing but train for battle, year after year. To beat a regular, disciplined army, Hamilton says, you need a regular, disciplined army of your own. This is the direct ancestor of the modern American arrangement, where the country keeps a full-time professional military, and the old citizen-soldier tradition lives on in a more organized, better-trained form in the National Guard. Hamilton lost the romance of the pure militia myth on purpose, because he had watched, at Valley Forge and everywhere else, what the romance cost when it collided with British regulars.

And that brings Hamilton to the theme that closes the paper, and it is the most grown-up idea in the whole essay. He calls it the failure of violent policy, by which he means any rule so extreme, so against the grain of how the world actually works, that it cannot survive contact with reality. Such a rule, he says, defeats itself. And he does not argue this in the abstract. He points at two things happening in America at that very moment.

Look at Pennsylvania, he says. Pennsylvania's own Bill of Rights solemnly declares that standing armies are dangerous to liberty and ought not to be kept up in time of peace. It is written right there in their founding document. And yet Pennsylvania, in a season of complete peace, faced with some local disorders in a county or two, has just gone ahead and raised a body of troops anyway, and will almost certainly keep them as long as it feels any threat to public order. The state's own constitution says one thing. The state's own necessity does the opposite. The parchment loses.

Then look at Massachusetts. When Shays and his farmers rose up in rebellion, the very uprising that had terrified the whole country the year before, Massachusetts had to raise troops in a hurry to put it down, and, Hamilton notes pointedly, it did so without even waiting for the permission from Congress that the Articles of Confederation supposedly required. And having crushed the revolt, the state kept soldiers on the payroll afterward to make sure the spirit of rebellion did not come back. Massachusetts did what necessity demanded, and the paper rule about waiting for Congress simply got brushed aside.

Both examples point at the same hard truth, and Hamilton states it in a phrase worth carrying out of this episode. These cases teach us, he says, "how unequal parchment provisions are to a struggle with public necessity."

How unequal parchment is to necessity. When a real crisis meets a written rule that forbids the only sensible response, the rule loses. Every single time. You can write "no army in peacetime" into a constitution in the most solemn ink you like, and the first time the country genuinely needs troops in peacetime, it will raise them, and your solemn rule becomes a scrap of paper everyone has agreed to ignore.

To drive it home, Hamilton reaches back across two thousand years for a small, almost sly story, and it is the perfect closing image. In ancient Sparta, he tells us, there was an ironclad rule that no man could be made admiral of the fleet twice. One term, never again. But then the Spartans and their allies took a bad beating at sea from the Athenians, and the allies wanted one particular man back in command, Lysander, who had won for them before. The Spartans were stuck. Their sacred rule forbade giving Lysander the admiralty a second time. So what did they do? They did not repeal the rule. They did something slyer. They gave another man the title of admiral, and they made Lysander the vice-admiral, and then they quietly handed Lysander all the actual power. A "flimsy subterfuge," Hamilton calls it. They kept the letter of their law and gutted its meaning, because necessity demanded the good commander and the rule stood in the way.

And that, Hamilton says, is what nations always do with rules that fight against their real needs. They do not honor them. They evade them, bend them, wink at them. Which leads to his final and most serious point, the one that lifts this whole argument above a debate about armies. There is a hidden cost, he warns, to writing rules you know will have to be broken. Every time a government is forced by necessity to break one of its own fundamental laws, even for the best of reasons, it chips away at something priceless, the deep public reverence for the constitution itself. People watch the supreme law get set aside once because it had to be, and the law is a little less sacred the next morning. And worse, that first necessary breach becomes the precedent, the excuse, for the next breach, when the necessity is weaker, or fake, or simply convenient.

So the deepest reason not to ban a peacetime army is not that the army is handy. It is that a ban you will inevitably violate is worse than no ban at all, because breaking it teaches everyone, including your own rulers, that the constitution is a document you set aside whenever things get hard. A wise people, Hamilton is saying, does not fill its constitution with noble rules it cannot keep. It writes rules it can actually live by, and it protects the honor of the whole document by never forcing itself to break them.

Step back and look at what these two papers, Number Twenty-Four and Number Twenty-Five, have done together. Twenty-Four met the fear of a standing army head on and refused either to ban it or to apologize for it, and instead put the army on a short leash held by the people's own elected representatives through the power of the purse. Twenty-Five then closed off the two escape routes people kept reaching for. No, you cannot hand the job to the states, because that path leads to unfairness, to armed rivalry between jealous neighbors, and to guns resting in exactly the hands you watch least and should fear most. And no, you cannot simply ban the peacetime army in writing, because a soft ban forbids nothing, a hard ban leaves you helpless until you have already been struck, and any ban at all will be trampled the first time real necessity walks in, taking a little of the constitution's dignity with it each time.

Underneath both papers is one steady conviction, the same one that has been building since Hamilton first said the word energetic back in Number Twenty-Three. Give the government the real strength it genuinely needs to keep the nation alive, then control that strength honestly, out in the open, through elected representatives and the power of the purse. Do not try to keep yourself safe by writing down powers you refuse to grant and dangers you pretend cannot happen. That, Hamilton keeps insisting, is not caution. It is a lie you tell yourself, and the lie is more dangerous than the army.

Next time, the argument widens out again. In Federalist Number Twenty-Six, still Alexander Hamilton, the question becomes what a real safeguard against military power actually looks like, once you admit that pretending the danger away will not work. Hamilton turns to the legislature, and to that two-year money clause, and to the idea that the true protection of a free people is not a paper ban on armies, but a vigilant elected body that has to look its own soldiers in the eye and vote to pay them, over and over, forever.
