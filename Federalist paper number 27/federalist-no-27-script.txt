For three papers now, Hamilton has been standing between the American people and their oldest fear. The fear of a standing army. In Number Twenty-Four he showed that the states could not be trusted to hold the nation's soldiers alone. In Number Twenty-Five he tore down the idea of banning peacetime armies on paper, and showed that a written prohibition was either empty or dangerous. And in Number Twenty-Six he built something in its place. Not a wall, but a leash. A recurring, public, on-the-record vote that forces the legislature to justify every army it keeps, out loud, in the face of its own voters, over and over, forever.

But under all of that argument, running quietly beneath three papers about soldiers, there was an assumption Hamilton had not yet turned around to face. The critics of the Constitution had been saying something more basic than fear of the army. They had been saying that this new government would need an army just to function at all. That it would be so distant, so resented, so alien to ordinary Americans, that it could never simply be obeyed the way a normal government is obeyed. It would have to be enforced. At the point of a bayonet. On its own people.

That is the assumption Hamilton finally wheels around to answer today. And his answer flips the whole picture. The critics assumed that a government reaching into your life would breed resistance, and resistance would breed soldiers. Hamilton is going to argue almost the exact opposite. That a government which is good at its job, and which touches the lives of ordinary people directly and fairly, earns the kind of obedience that no army could ever manufacture. The sword, in his telling, is not the tool of a strong government. It is the crutch of a failed one.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-Seven. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. It went out in the New York Packet in the last days of December, seventeen eighty-seven, carrying the same argument forward without a break. And where the last three papers asked how to control the army, this one asks a deeper and quieter question. Why would a well-run union need to lean on an army to be obeyed in the first place?

Let me start where Hamilton starts, with the objection itself, stated plainly. The opponents of the Constitution had claimed, in his words, that a government of this kind cannot operate without the aid of a military force to execute its laws. Read that slowly. The charge is not that the government might someday misuse an army. The charge is that it could not run a single day without one. That force would be baked into its daily operation. That collecting an ordinary tax, or enforcing an ordinary law, would require troops, because the people would simply refuse to comply.

And Hamilton's first move is to ask a question the critics had skipped right past. Why would you assume that? Where is the evidence that Americans would be any less willing to obey this government than they are to obey their own states? The whole fear rests on a hidden guess, the guess that people will resent federal authority for no reason other than that it is federal. And Hamilton does not think people work that way. He thinks people respond to how well they are governed, not to the label on the government.

Here is the principle he lays down, and it is the foundation the entire paper is built on. He writes that their confidence in and obedience to a government will commonly be proportioned to the goodness or badness of its administration. Obedience tracks competence. People obey a government roughly in proportion to how well it runs. A government that governs well, fairly, predictably, in a way people can live with, gets obeyed as a matter of course. A government that governs badly breeds resistance no matter how it is structured. The question that actually determines whether force will be needed is not federal versus state. It is well-run versus badly-run.

And this is not some abstract maxim. You can test it against your own experience of authority. People do not obey the rules of a well-run institution because guards are standing over them. They obey because the thing works, because going along is easier than fighting, because the institution has earned a basic presumption of legitimacy simply by doing its job day after day. And when an institution governs badly, arbitrarily, unfairly, no amount of enforcement ever fully cures the resistance, because the resentment regenerates faster than any force can suppress it. Hamilton is insisting that governments obey the same rule everyone already knows from ordinary life. Respect is earned by performance. It cannot be extracted by threat.

So the real debate, Hamilton says, is not about armies at all. It is about a much simpler question. Is there any reason to expect the national government will be worse administered than the state governments? Because unless it will be, there is no reason to expect it will be resisted any more than the states are. And the states, for all their flaws, do not need standing armies to collect their taxes and run their courts.

A fair question here is whether Hamilton has any actual grounds for expecting the national government to be run well, or whether he is just asserting it. He does have grounds, and he gives them, though briskly, because he has made this case in earlier papers and will make it again in later ones. He offers three reasons, and it is worth counting them off, because together they are the extended-republic argument that Madison made famous back in Number Ten, now put to a slightly different use.

First, the national government draws its officials from a far larger field. When you elect a representative from a wide district rather than a narrow one, you are choosing from a bigger pool of people, which means better odds of landing on someone genuinely capable. A larger sphere of choice raises the quality of the choice. Second, the Senate, in the design of the Constitution, was to be chosen by the state legislatures, which are themselves selected bodies of experienced men, so that branch could be expected to be assembled with, in Hamilton's phrase, peculiar care and judgment. And third, a larger national arena is simply harder to poison with the passing local furies that so often wreck small communities. The momentary rage, the temporary prejudice, the neighborhood faction that can capture a town council in a bad season, has a much harder time seizing control of a whole continent. The bigger stage dilutes the poison.

You may recognize that last one. It is the beating heart of Madison's argument in Number Ten, that a large republic is more resistant to faction than a small one, precisely because no single passion can sweep the whole of it at once. Hamilton is borrowing that logic and pointing it at a new target. He is saying, if the extended republic really is less prone to faction and folly, then it is also less likely to govern so badly that its own people rise up against it. Good administration is not guaranteed. But the structure tilts the odds toward it, not away from it.

Now Hamilton adds a second layer, and this one is about deterrence rather than affection. Even setting aside how well the union governs, consider how much harder it is to defy. He puts it in a clean pair of opposites. The hope of impunity, he writes, is a strong incitement to sedition. The dread of punishment, a proportionably strong discouragement to it. In plain terms, people rebel when they think they can get away with it, and they stay home when they are sure they cannot. So ask which government is more frightening to a would-be rebel. A single state, which can only reach for the resources inside its own borders? Or the whole union, which can call on the combined strength of every state at once?

Hamilton draws the picture sharply. A turbulent faction inside one state, he says, might easily flatter itself that it could take on the friends of the government in that one state. The numbers might feel winnable. But that same faction can hardly be so infatuated, so lost in its own delusion, as to imagine itself a match for the combined forces of the entire Union. And so, he concludes, there is actually less danger of violent resistance to the authority of the whole Confederacy than to that of any single member of it. The bigness that the critics feared, the reach across thirteen states, turns out to be a reason the government will need force less often, not more. Nobody storms the fortress they know they cannot take.

And now we arrive at the idea at the center of this paper, the one that is genuinely surprising, the one Hamilton clearly enjoys most. It is a claim about human nature, and it runs directly against the critics' deepest instinct.

The critics had a picture in their heads. In that picture, the more a distant national government reaches into your ordinary life, your business, your contracts, your daily affairs, the more you will bristle at it, the more you will resist it, and the more soldiers it will take to hold you down. Reach breeds resentment. Presence breeds rebellion. It sounds intuitive. Keep the far-off government far off, and it stays tolerable. Let it in the door, and you will come to hate it.

Hamilton says they have it exactly backwards. And to see why, you have to start where he starts, with a plain fact about people. Man, he writes, is very much a creature of habit. We are shaped by what we encounter often and shaped hardly at all by what we rarely meet. A thing that seldom crosses our path barely registers on us. And that has a consequence for governments that is easy to miss. Listen to how Hamilton puts it. A government continually at a distance and out of sight, he writes, can hardly be expected to interest the sensations of the people.

Sit with that line, because it is the hinge of the whole argument. A government you never encounter is not a government you love or hate. It is a government you forget. It is an abstraction, a rumor, a name in a newspaper. It cannot reach your feelings at all, because it never reaches your life. Distance does not produce affection. Distance produces indifference. And indifference, Hamilton understood, is a far weaker foundation for a country than attachment.

So turn it around. What happens when the national government does enter the ordinary business of life? When people meet it in the common occurrences of their days, when it becomes familiar to their sight and to their feelings, when it touches the things they actually care about? Hamilton's answer is that the more this happens, the greater the probability that the government will, in his words, conciliate the respect and attachment of the community. Familiarity, when the thing is done well, does not breed contempt. It breeds attachment. The government that shows up, competently, in the daily fabric of your life is the government you come to regard as yours. And a government people regard as their own does not need to be enforced at gunpoint. It is obeyed out of habit, and out of a kind of ownership.

Look closely at the image Hamilton reaches for here, because it is doing real work. He talks about the government circulating through the channels and currents in which the passions of mankind naturally flow. Think of those passions as a landscape with rivers already cut into it. People care, deeply and permanently, about their property, their families, their livelihoods, their safety. Those are the riverbeds of human attention, and the water is always running through them. A government that sets itself against that current, distant and irrelevant to the things people actually feel, is a dam with nothing behind it. But a government that runs along those same channels, that shows up exactly where people are already paying attention and does right by them there, is carried by the current instead of fighting it. It does not have to force its way into people's regard. The natural flow of human concern delivers it there.

Hamilton lands the point with a phrase worth holding onto. The more the government circulates through the ordinary channels and currents in which the passions of mankind naturally flow, he says, the less it will require the aid of the violent and perilous expedients of compulsion. The violent and perilous expedients of compulsion. That is his name for the army, for force, for the bayonet. And his whole claim is that a government woven into everyday life needs those expedients less and less, not more and more. The reach the critics feared is the very thing that would make the reach unnecessary.

Now, if that argument about closeness and affection sounds familiar, it should, and this is where the series pays off, because Hamilton made almost this exact argument once before, and pointed it in the opposite direction. Back in Number Seventeen, remember, he was reassuring nervous Americans that the states would never be swallowed by the union. And the reason he gave was this same law of human nature. People love what is close and feel little for what is far away. Their affections, he said then, are weak in proportion to the distance of the object. And because the states ran the daily, visible business of life, the courts, the sheriffs, the ordinary justice people could actually see and feel, the states would always hold the people's hearts. The federal government, distant and abstract, could never compete for that affection.

That was Number Seventeen, and it was a promise to the Anti-Federalists. Do not worry, the states own the people's love, because the states own the people's daily life. Now watch what Hamilton does in Number Twenty-Seven with the very same principle. He takes that same law, closeness breeds affection, distance breeds indifference, and he says, very well, then let the national government become close too. Let it operate directly on individuals, in its own proper sphere, so that it also becomes familiar, also becomes felt, also earns a share of that same attachment. In Number Seventeen the law of affection was a fence protecting the states. In Number Twenty-Seven it is a bridge carrying the union into the people's regard. Same insight about human nature, aimed two different ways in two different papers. That is Hamilton thinking out loud across the series, and it is worth noticing how consistent his picture of human beings stays even as he turns it to different purposes.

So that is the argument from affection. A well-run government that touches your life earns your obedience without force. But Hamilton has a second, harder, more structural reason the new government will rarely need the sword, and it is the reason that actually makes the whole thing work in practice. And here you might be wondering how any of this warm talk about habit and attachment turns into something concrete. It turns concrete right here.

The deepest reason the new government can enforce its laws peacefully has nothing to do with sentiment. It has to do with what the government acts upon. And this is the point the whole series has been circling since Number Fifteen. The old government under the Articles of Confederation was a league. It governed states, not people. It could pass a resolution, address it to Virginia or to Rhode Island as whole political bodies, and then simply hope they complied. And when they did not comply, which was constantly, there was nothing left to do about it but force. You cannot summon a state to a courtroom. You cannot fine a state or jail a state. So a government built to command states, Hamilton says, is one in which, in his words, there can be no sanction for the laws but force. When a state defies it, the delinquency can only be redressed, in his words, by war and violence. That was the trap of the old system, and we spent Numbers Fifteen and Sixteen inside it. A league of states has only one enforcement tool, and that tool is an army.

The new Constitution escapes the trap by changing what the law reaches. It does not address itself to states as collective bodies. It reaches past the state, all the way down to the individual citizen. And an individual, unlike a state, is something the ordinary machinery of law already knows how to handle. A person can be summoned, tried, fined, and if need be arrested, by the same everyday courts and officers that every state already runs, without a single soldier called out and without any crisis between governments. That substitution, the courtroom where the battlefield used to be, is the thing we walked through in Number Sixteen, and I will not walk all of it again here.

But Number Twenty-Seven adds something new to that picture, and it is the piece that makes the machine actually turn. It is not just that the national government will run its own courts. It is that the government reaches down and enlists the ordinary officials of every state into the work of carrying out its laws. Here is how. The Constitution declares that federal law, on the subjects genuinely entrusted to it, is the supreme law of the land. And it binds every official in the country, legislative, executive, and judicial, in every single state, to support that law by the sanctity of an oath. Every state judge, every state officer, swears to uphold the Constitution and the laws made under it. Which means the state's own judges and magistrates are not bystanders to federal law, and they are certainly not enemies of it. By their oath, they are made part of enforcing it.

Picture what that actually looks like on the ground. A dispute lands in a county courthouse, in front of a judge who was chosen under state law and who took an oath the day he put on the robe. Part of that oath binds him to the Constitution and the laws made under it. So when a legitimate federal law bears on the case in front of him, he does not treat it as some foreign instruction arriving from a far-off capital. He applies it, because he swore that he would. To the ordinary person standing in that courtroom, there is no visible seam. No federal building, no federal officer, no sign that two different governments are involved at all. There is just the law, and a local judge he recognizes, applying it. The national government has reached that person fully and effectively, and it has done so through a face he already trusts, without a single soldier anywhere in the picture. That is what Hamilton means when he says the states are incorporated into the operations of the union. The seam disappears.

Hamilton draws the conclusion directly. The legislatures, courts, and magistrates of every state, he says, will be incorporated into the operations of the national government, as far as its just and constitutional authority extends, and will be rendered auxiliary to the enforcement of its laws. Incorporated. Made auxiliary. In plain English, the national government does not have to build a vast separate apparatus of its own, a federal enforcer in every county, backed by a federal army, to make its laws stick. It borrows the enforcement machinery every state already has, the judges and officers already sitting in every town, now bound by oath to treat legitimate federal law as the supreme law it is. The distinction between the two sources of authority, Hamilton says, will fade in the common person's mind until federal law feels no more foreign, and no more in need of an army behind it, than the law of his own state.

Now, a careful listener might feel a flicker of worry right here, and Hamilton knows it, because he pauses to flag it himself. If the national government can reach down and enlist every state's own officials into carrying out its laws, does that not threaten to hollow the states out, to turn their judges and officers into mere instruments of the center? It is a fair fear, and it is exactly the one the Anti-Federalists pressed hardest. Hamilton drops a footnote here promising that this objection, the claim that all of this will tend to destroy the state governments, will be fully answered in its proper place further on. He is not dodging it. He is filing it, because it is the very question the next several papers are going to wrestle with. For today his point stands on its own. Enlisting the states' ordinary machinery is how the union avoids needing an army, not how it builds a tyranny.

So why does that matter so much? Because it means the ordinary, everyday enforcement of the union's laws looks nothing like an occupation. It looks like a court date. It looks like a sworn local official doing his sworn duty. The whole grim picture the critics painted, a distant government forced to hold its people down by the sword, dissolves once you see that the government works through the same peaceful legal channels people already live inside every day. If the union's powers are administered, in Hamilton's words, with a common share of prudence, there is every reason to expect a regular and peaceable execution of its laws. Not perfect. Not frictionless. But regular and peaceable, the way a normal government runs, without the army the critics were so sure would be necessary.

And you can see the shape of this in American life right now, so completely that it is almost invisible. Think about how federal authority actually reaches an ordinary person today. When you file your taxes, no soldier is involved. When a federal law is broken, the case runs through a courthouse, with a judge and a jury and a process, not through a barracks. The federal government is enforced, overwhelmingly, by clerks and courts and paperwork and civil officers, not by the Army. Americans argue endlessly about whether they trust the federal government in the abstract, that distant thing on the horizon Hamilton described. But the actual pieces of it that people meet in their lives, the mail that arrives, the disaster relief after the storm, the retirement check that comes on time, the passport in the drawer, tend to be the pieces people quietly rely on and defend. The abstraction is distrusted. The parts you can feel are the parts that earn attachment. That is Hamilton's argument, playing out two hundred and forty years later, almost exactly as he drew it up.

Hamilton closes the paper by taking on the hardest version of the objection, the cynical one, and I think it is worth ending where he ends. Fine, a critic might say. Suppose you are wrong about all of it. Suppose the men who run this government turn out to be bad, indifferent to the public good, deaf to the obligations of duty. What then? And Hamilton's answer is not to insist that the rulers will be good. It is to ask a colder, shrewder question. Even if they were bad, how exactly would provoking their own people into resistance serve their ambition? A ruler who has to conquer his own country to govern it has already lost the thing rulers actually want, which is a country that runs. Force is expensive, dangerous, and self-defeating. Willing obedience is cheap and stable. Even a purely selfish government, Hamilton is saying, has every incentive to be the kind of government people obey without being forced. The sword is not just morally ugly. It is bad strategy.

So step back and look at what these four papers have done together, Numbers Twenty-Four through Twenty-Seven, because they really are one long argument about force and freedom. The first three dismantled the fear of the army from the inside. They showed that the states cannot safely hold it alone, that a paper ban will not restrain it, and that the true leash is the recurring public vote that Number Twenty-Six described, backed by time and vigilance and a watchful opposition. And now Number Twenty-Seven pulls the camera back and makes the quieter, more reassuring point underneath all of it. In the ordinary course of things, the sword will barely be drawn at all. Because a government that is competently run, that touches the lives of its citizens directly and fairly, that enforces its laws through the sworn officers and open courts every state already has, earns the sort of obedience that armies can never produce and never replace.

That is Hamilton's real answer to the deepest fear of the whole debate. The critics thought force was the foundation of the new government. Hamilton is telling them force is the mark of a government that has failed. Build it well, root it in people's daily lives, run it through courts instead of camps, and the army becomes what it should always be. A last resort, rarely reached for, and not the everyday engine of the state. Good government does not need many soldiers. It needs to be good.

Next time, Hamilton turns to the honest exception buried inside all of this. Because even the best-run government will, once in a great while, genuinely face force it must answer with force, a rebellion, an insurrection, a true emergency. In Federalist Number Twenty-Eight, still Alexander Hamilton, the argument turns to what happens when force really is necessary, who gets to use it, and why the ultimate safeguard against a government that abuses that power is not a clause on parchment at all, but the people themselves.
