Last time, we did something a little grim together. We performed an autopsy.

Madison and Hamilton laid the dead confederacies of ancient Greece out on the table, two of them, side by side, and showed us exactly what had killed each one. The first, the Amphictyonic Council, had every power you could ever want written down on paper. Armies it could raise. Quarrels it could settle for good. Cities it could punish. It even had the gods at Delphi on its side. And it died anyway, of bullying and paralysis, because for all those powers it was still only a government that acted on its member cities, and never on people. The second body was the Achaean League, a real union, a wiser one, and it actually gave its members calmer and fairer lives than they had ever managed standing alone. And then jealousy from the outside, and a desperate old habit of inviting in powerful friends, tore it to pieces too.

And the verdict written across both bodies was the same. A confederacy does not die because its center grows too strong. It dies of anarchy among its members, and of the predators that weakness invites through the door.

It is a powerful case. But if you were sitting in New York in the winter of seventeen eighty-seven, reading along by candlelight, you might have had one very reasonable objection ready. And Madison knows you have it. You might have said: well, that was the ancient world. Oracles and oaths and quarreling little city-states. A vanished age. Surely things are different now. Surely a confederacy in the modern world, in civilized, eighteenth-century Europe, would not go stumbling into the same childish trap.

Today, Madison takes that objection, and he walks you straight out of antiquity and into his own century to answer it.

This is The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Nineteen. Federalist Number Nineteen, which appeared in the New York papers in the second week of December, seventeen eighty-seven, signed once again by Hamilton and Madison together. It is the same collaboration we met last time, drawn from the same remarkable notebook, the one Madison had filled with the post-mortems of every confederacy in history before he ever set foot in Philadelphia. Last episode, he spent his ancient examples. Today he opens the modern half of the file. And he opens it with the biggest, the oldest, and on paper the most impressive confederacy in the entire world he actually lived in.

He opens it with the Holy Roman Empire.

Now, there is a famous joke about the Holy Roman Empire, and it was already old when Madison was writing. The French writer Voltaire had quipped, a generation earlier, that this thing which called itself the Holy Roman Empire was in no way holy, nor Roman, nor an empire. And that joke lands because it points at something genuinely strange. Here was a political body that sprawled across the center of Europe, over the German lands and beyond, that traced its crown back to Charlemagne around the year eight hundred, and on through the German kings from the nine hundreds onward. By Madison's day it had been lurching along, in one form or another, for the better part of a thousand years. It was the great-grandfather of European institutions. And it was a confederacy. A collection of hundreds of princes, dukes, bishops, and free cities, each running its own affairs, all loosely bound under one elected emperor.

So Madison does to it exactly what he did to the Greek leagues. He starts by dazzling you with the list of powers.

And the list is real. There was a diet, a grand assembly representing all the members of the empire. There was the emperor at the head, who could veto what the diet decided. There were even two high courts to settle disputes among the members. And the powers on the books were sweeping. The diet could make war and peace. It could sign treaties and alliances. It could raise troops and money. It could coin money, admit new members, build fortresses. And it had a dreaded ultimate weapon called the ban of the empire, by which a disobedient member could be stripped of his rights and have his lands declared forfeit. The members, in turn, were forbidden from cutting their own side deals against the empire, forbidden from taxing each other's trade without permission, forbidden from making war on one another.

Read all that, and the natural thought is: surely this one is different. Surely a confederacy with a thousand years behind it, with courts and a ban and an emperor, must be the exception. Madison quotes himself the temptation to believe it. From such a parade of constitutional powers, he says, you would naturally suppose this must be the one that breaks the pattern.

And then he closes the trap, in nine words. Nothing would be further from the reality.

Why not? For the exact same reason the Greek council failed. Because of what the whole thing rests on. The empire, Madison says, is a community of sovereigns. The diet is an assembly of sovereigns. And the laws are addressed to sovereigns. It is, once again, a government of governments. And so, he says, all those magnificent powers add up to this:

"a nerveless body, incapable of regulating its own members, insecure against external dangers, and agitated with unceasing fermentations in its own bowels."

A nerveless body. Sit with that image, because it is the whole diagnosis in three words. A body with no nerves. You can give it muscles, you can give it a thousand years of constitutional tradition, you can write it a list of powers as long as your arm. But if the nerves do not run, if the center cannot send a signal that the limbs are actually compelled to obey, then the muscles are decoration. The empire could not reliably regulate its own members. It could not defend itself from the outside. And it churned, endlessly, with quarrels boiling up from within. Powers on paper. Paralysis in fact. We have heard this before.

And here Madison gives you the most painfully vivid picture of that paralysis in the whole paper, and it is almost funny, except that it is about life and death. Picture the empire actually trying to defend itself. An enemy is massing on the border. So the diet has to meet, and the princes have to debate, and every proud sovereign body has its own jealousies and its own separate views and its own clashing demands, and all of that has to be ground through before anything can be decided. And so, Madison writes, by the time the talking is done:

"before the diet can settle the arrangements, the enemy are in the field; and before the federal troops are ready to take it, are retiring into winter quarters."

I love that line, in a bleak way. By the time the committee finishes arguing about how to fight the war, the fighting season is over. The enemy has already marched in, done its damage, and gone home for the winter to rest up, and the empire's army is only just lacing up its boots. That is what a government of sovereigns looks like the moment it faces a real threat. Endless discussion, fatally late action.

If you want the modern echo of that, you do not have to look far. Think of any alliance or union today that can only act when every single member agrees, where any one member holds a veto. When the crisis is small, it muddles along fine. But when something fast and dangerous arrives, and a decision has to be made this week, not next year, that unanimity rule turns into paralysis. Everyone debates while the thing they feared is already happening. Madison watched exactly that dynamic play out in the Holy Roman Empire two hundred and fifty years before our own institutions would rediscover it.

So that is the diagnosis. Now Madison shows you what that paralysis actually cost, written in blood, over centuries. And he does it in one long, rolling, terrible sentence that I want to read you a good piece of, because the rhythm of it is the point.

The history of Germany, he says, is:

"a history of wars between the emperor and the princes and states; of wars among the princes and states themselves; of the licentiousness of the strong, and the oppression of the weak; of foreign intrusions, and foreign intrigues; of requisitions of men and money disregarded, or partially complied with."

On and on it rolls, war after war, grievance after grievance, until he lands it on the flattest, saddest possible note: a history, he says, "of general imbecility, confusion, and misery." That is the biography of a thousand-year empire in a single breath. Not glory. Not order. Imbecility, confusion, and misery.

And the deepest wound in that whole history has a name, and it is the set-piece I want to slow down and tell you, because it is the story of how a weak confederacy does not just suffer at home. It becomes the property of foreigners.

In sixteen eighteen, the German lands fell into a war so long that we simply call it by its length. The Thirty Years' War. For a generation, the emperor with one half of the empire fought against princes backed by the other half, and the whole thing pulled in the great outside powers, until Germany itself became the battlefield where the rest of Europe came to settle its scores. Sweden marched its armies down into the German heart. France poured in money and then men. Towns were sacked, burned, and sacked again. In some regions, by the time it was over, a third of the people were simply gone, dead of the fighting, or the famine, or the plague that the armies carried with them. An entire confederacy turned into a killing ground for thirty years, because it had no center strong enough to keep its own members from each other's throats, and no strength to keep the foreigners out.

And then comes the part that should really make you wince. When the war finally ended, in sixteen forty-eight, with the agreement we call the Peace of Westphalia, who wrote the terms? Madison gives you the humiliating answer. The peace, he says, was negotiated and dictated by foreign powers. France and Sweden did not just fight in Germany. They sat at the table and dictated the settlement. And the articles of that peace, the terms set by these outsiders, became a fundamental part of the German constitution itself.

Read that twice, because it is the darkest possible version of the warning this whole series keeps sounding. A weak confederacy does not merely get invaded. It gets edited. Foreign powers reached into the basic law of the empire and made themselves permanent parties to it, guarantors with a standing right to meddle, forever. The Greeks invited Philip to a seat at their sacred council. The Germans handed France and Sweden a pen and let them write into the constitution. The disease is identical. Only the costume has changed, from a toga to a powdered wig.

So at this point a fair person asks the obvious question. If the empire was this broken, did nobody try to fix it? Did they really just let the strong devour the weak and the foreigners run the show?

And the answer is, they did try. And the way the fix failed is the most revealing story of all.

To solve the problem of enforcement, the empire was carved up into about nine or ten regional districts, called circles. And each circle was given an organization and a job: to carry out the law, by force if necessary, against any member inside it that misbehaved. On paper, it is a sensible idea. If the whole empire is too clumsy to discipline a bad actor, break it into manageable regions and let each region police its own.

But it did not produce order. Madison says the experiment served only to prove the disease was deeper than anyone admitted. Each circle, he writes, became "the miniature picture of the deformities of this political monster." A political monster. The circles either failed to do their job at all, or they did it with all the devastation and carnage of a civil war. Sometimes a whole circle would simply refuse, and become a lawbreaker itself, so that the cure became one more symptom.

And to show you exactly how that went wrong, Madison reaches into the history books and pulls out one specific case. A single story. And it is worth telling slowly, because it is the Wyoming Valley of this paper, the one concrete scene that makes the whole abstract argument suddenly real.

Picture a free imperial city called Donauworth, in the early sixteen hundreds, in the southern German circle of Swabia. In that city there lived an abbot, the Abbe de Saint Croix, who held certain old protected privileges. And on some public occasion, when he tried to exercise those privileges, the townspeople, who had their own religious grievances against him, turned on him. There was an outrage, a riot, an insult to a protected man. A clear case of disorder. Exactly the kind of thing the system was built to handle.

So the machinery turned. The city of Donauworth was placed under the ban of the empire, that dreaded ultimate punishment. And the job of enforcing the ban, of marching in and restoring order, was handed to the Duke of Bavaria. Now notice, Madison says, the Duke of Bavaria was not even the director of that circle. He was from a neighboring one. But he eagerly took the assignment. And he showed up in front of this one small city with ten thousand troops, by Madison's count, and some later histories put the number closer to fifteen thousand.

Ten thousand soldiers, or more, to discipline a town that had insulted an abbot.

And here is the turn that tells you everything. Because the Duke of Bavaria, Madison says, had a private interest all along. His own ancestors had once had some faded, antiquated claim to that very place. So when he arrived, under the perfectly legal banner of enforcing the empire's law, he simply, in his own words and his own name, took the city. He disarmed it. He punished its people. And he annexed Donauworth to his own lands, and kept it. As Madison drily notes, this was what he had secretly intended from the very beginning.

That is the whole rotten machine in a single story. The grand mechanism of justice, the ban of the empire, the lawful enforcement by a neighboring prince, turned out to be nothing but a license. A strong member used the empire's own legal weapon as a polite excuse to grab a weak member's territory. The enforcement of order became the instrument of plunder. And the empire, the supposed authority over them all, could do nothing but watch one of its cities get swallowed by one of its dukes, in broad daylight, with the paperwork all in order.

Do you hear the echo? In ancient Greece, Madison told us, the deputies of the strong cities simply awed and corrupted the deputies of the weak ones, and the verdict always went to whoever had the most muscle. Here it is again, two thousand years later, in Christian Germany, in powdered wigs. The strong eat the weak, and they use the constitution as the napkin.

So, the obvious next question. If it was this bad, this paralyzed, this preyed-upon, how did the Holy Roman Empire keep going at all? Why didn't this disjointed machine simply fall to pieces?

Madison's answer is almost the saddest part. It survived, he says, not because of any strength, but because of weakness. The members were each too weak and too frightened to risk standing alone against the great powers surrounding them, so they huddled together out of fear. The emperor had vast private lands and family power of his own, separate from his imperial title, and he had every interest in keeping the creaky old system limping along, because being its head made him the first prince in Europe. And, most tellingly, the foreign powers wanted it kept weak. They had long since figured out that a feeble, divided Germany in the middle of Europe was a Germany they could manage, and they actively worked to keep it that way. The empire stayed alive not because it worked, but because everyone, inside and out, had a reason to keep the patient barely breathing and never let it heal.

And if you want to see where that road ends, Madison says, you only have to glance at the freshest grave in Europe. And he points at Poland.

This is the moment in the paper where the history stops feeling old. Because Poland, when Madison was writing, was not ancient at all. It was right there, in the news, bleeding. Poland was its own kind of confederacy, a government built on top of a crowd of powerful local nobles, each clutching his own independence so jealously that the whole nation could barely move. And Madison's verdict on it is one cold, devastating sentence:

"Equally unfit for self-government and self-defense, it has long been at the mercy of its powerful neighbors; who have lately had the mercy to disburden it of one third of its people and territories."

Listen to the bitterness in that. Had the mercy to disburden it. He is talking about the partition of Poland. In seventeen seventy-two, just fifteen years before Madison wrote, three powerful neighbors, Russia, Prussia, and Austria, had simply reached in and carved off enormous pieces of a weaker country and divided the meat among themselves, and they had done it without a real war, because Poland was too internally paralyzed to stop them. A third of her territory, and by many counts an even larger share of her people, gone, signed away. And Madison's grim little joke, the mercy to disburden it, drips with contempt. As if the robbers had done Poland a favor by relieving her of the burden of a third of herself.

And here is the part Madison could not have known, the part that makes this passage almost unbearable in hindsight. He was describing the first partition. There would be two more. Within a decade of Madison writing these words, Poland would be partitioned again, and then a third time, and by seventeen ninety-five it would be gone entirely, erased from the map of Europe, with no Polish state at all for the next hundred and twenty-three years. Madison pointed at Poland as a warning. History turned his warning into a tombstone. A government over local sovereigns, too jealous to unite, was not just sick. It was a country that could be made to disappear.

So why does any of this matter to a New Yorker in seventeen eighty-seven, deciding how to vote on a constitution? Because Madison is building a wall of evidence, and every brick is a real place. The Amphictyonic Council. The Achaean League. The Holy Roman Empire. And now Poland. Ancient and modern, pagan and Christian, north and south. Everywhere he looks, the same design produces the same death. He is daring his reader to find him a counterexample. To name one confederacy of jealous sovereigns that actually worked.

And there is one place a clever reader might point. One apparent exception. Switzerland.

Because the Swiss cantons, those small mountain communities, had been bound together for centuries, and they were famously stable, famously free, famously not conquered. If loose confederacies always die, the skeptic says, then how do you explain the Swiss?

Madison takes the challenge head on, and his answer is sharp. The connection among the Swiss cantons, he says, scarcely amounts to a confederacy at all. Look closely, he says, and you find they have no common treasury. No common army, even in war. No common money. No common courts. None of the ordinary marks of a single sovereign state. So what holds them together? Not good design. Madison lists the real reasons, and not one of them is institutional. They are held together by their mountains, by the sheer luck of a landscape that walls them off. They are held together by being individually so small and weak that none can dominate the others. They are held together by their fear of the powerful neighbors all around them. And they are held together by being a simple, similar people, with few things to fight about in the first place.

In other words, Switzerland is not a counterexample at all. It is a confederacy that has never really been tested, propped up by geography and poverty and fear. And Madison knows how to prove that. Because there was one moment the Swiss confederacy did get tested, by a real and dividing question. And on that question, it cracked.

The question was religion. When the Reformation split Christian Europe into Protestant and Catholic, it split Switzerland too, right down the middle, canton against canton. And here is Madison's verdict on what that revealed:

"the moment a cause of difference sprang up, capable of trying its strength, it failed."

The moment a real difference appeared, the moment something arrived that actually tested whether this union was a union, it failed. The religious quarrel boiled over into violent and bloody contests, more than once. And in practice, Madison says, it severed the league. The Protestant cantons and the Catholic cantons started holding their own separate assemblies, handling their most important business apart, leaving the common assembly with almost nothing real to do. The confederacy did not technically dissolve. It just quietly stopped being one, on everything that mattered.

And then the final, familiar note, the one this whole series cannot stop sounding. When the two halves split, they did exactly what divided peoples always do. They went looking for outside friends, on opposite sides. The Protestant cantons, led by Berne, tied themselves to the Protestant Dutch. The Catholic cantons, led by Lucerne, tied themselves to Catholic France. The same country, reaching out two different hands, to two different foreign patrons, against itself. We have watched this happen now in ancient Greece, where the leagues called in Macedon and Rome. We have watched it in Germany, where the warring princes leaned on Sweden and France. And here it is one more time, in the clean mountain air of Switzerland. A divided confederacy always, always ends up holding a foreign hand.

Now, I want to give Switzerland the ending that Madison himself could not give it, because it is one of the most satisfying turns in this whole story, and it proves his point better than he ever knew.

For sixty more years after Madison wrote, the Swiss kept limping along in their loose, tested, half-severed league. And then, in the eighteen forties, the old religious fault line finally erupted into a short, sharp civil war between the Catholic and Protestant cantons. And when the smoke cleared, the Swiss did something remarkable. In eighteen forty-eight, they sat down and threw out the loose old confederacy, and they built themselves a real federal union instead, with a true central government that could actually act. And they did not invent it from scratch. They looked across the ocean, at the very document these Federalist Papers were written to defend, and they borrowed its design. A national legislature in two houses, one of them giving every canton an equal voice and the other based on population, modeled openly on the American Congress. Switzerland, the supposed great exception, eventually survived only by swallowing exactly the medicine Madison was prescribing in seventeen eighty-seven. The cure was real. It just took the Swiss another sixty years and a war to take it.

So let us step back and read the whole file Madison has now laid out, because with this paper the modern half of his case clicks into place beside the ancient half.

Last time, two Greek graves. The sham union that deserved to die, and the good union that died anyway of jealousy and invited predators. This time, the modern wards of the same hospital. The Holy Roman Empire, a thousand years old, a parade of powers on paper, and underneath, a nerveless body, preyed upon by its own strong members and edited by foreign hands. Poland, the same design taken all the way to its conclusion, partitioned alive and soon to vanish from the earth. And Switzerland, the apparent exception, saved only by mountains and fear, and shattering the moment a real question put it to the test.

Different centuries. Different costumes. Identical disease. And the disease is always the same one Hamilton named back at Number Fifteen and has been naming ever since. A government that acts on governments instead of on people will always rot into anarchy among its members. It will always be too slow to defend itself, too weak to discipline its strong, and too tempting for foreigners to leave alone. You cannot fix it by writing a longer list of powers on the parchment. The Holy Roman Empire had a glorious list of powers, and it was a corpse that simply had not finished falling down.

And the men writing these papers had read every one of these post-mortems, ancient and modern, and they had made up their minds not to add the United States to the list. They were going to build the one thing none of these confederacies ever had. A union whose laws reached past the member states and landed directly on the individual citizen, so that its strength would never again depend on making war against its own members to be obeyed.

But Madison is not quite finished with his tour of the graveyard. He has shown you the ancient world and he has shown you the German lands, Poland, and the mountains of Switzerland. There is one body left on the table, and it is the one closest to home, because it is the one the Americans most admired. The Dutch. The United Provinces of the Netherlands, the proud, rich, Protestant republic that many Americans looked to as a model and a sister. Next time, in Federalist Number Twenty, Hamilton and Madison are going to walk us through the Dutch republic and show that even this most respectable of confederacies suffered from the very same fatal flaw, and then Madison is going to gather up the whole long argument, all the bodies, all the graves, and deliver the final verdict that this entire stretch of history was building toward.

This has been The Federalist Papers: Explained. Episode Nineteen. Next time: Federalist Number Twenty, Hamilton and Madison once more, on the Dutch republic that Americans so admired, and the hard lesson the founders drew from watching even the best of the old confederacies fail.
