At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had taken his argument about disunion to its darkest place yet. The earlier papers had been about peace and prosperity. Federalist Number Eight was about freedom itself. Hamilton's claim there was unsettling and simple. If America split into separate confederacies, those confederacies would live in permanent fear of each other. And permanent fear does something to a country. It forces it to stay armed. It builds standing armies, raises up the soldier above the citizen, and pushes power steadily toward the executive, because war always feeds the part of government that fights it. And the cruelest turn in that paper was this. Hamilton said that even a people who love liberty will, over time, trade some of it away for safety. To be more secure, he wrote, they become willing to run the risk of being less free. Disunion, in other words, would not just make America weaker. It would slowly make America less free, from the inside.

That was Federalist Number Eight. And it leaves a question hanging in the air, one that Hamilton knows his readers are now asking. He has spent paper after paper describing the dangers of breaking apart. But what is the case for the thing he actually wants? What does union itself do for you? Not what disunion costs, but what union provides.

That is where Federalist Number Nine begins. It was written by Alexander Hamilton, published in the Independent Journal on November twenty-first, seventeen eighty-seven, signed, as always, Publius. And its title tells you the promise it is making. The Union as a Safeguard Against Domestic Faction and Insurrection. Hamilton is going to argue that a firm union is not just a shield against the outside world. It is a shield against ourselves. Against the thing that has wrecked free governments more reliably than any foreign army ever has. Faction. Internal division. The slow tearing-apart of a republic by its own people.

And to make that case, Hamilton does something interesting. He opens not with America, but with a graveyard.

He asks his readers to look at the history of the small republics of ancient Greece and of Renaissance Italy. The little city-states. Athens, Sparta, Thebes. Florence, Venice, Genoa, Milan. And his verdict on that history is brutal. He says it is impossible to read it without feeling, in his words, sensations of horror and disgust. Not disappointment. Not concern. Horror and disgust.

Here is the picture he paints. These were small free states, and they were, he says, continually agitated, kept in perpetual vibration between the extremes of tyranny and anarchy. Sit with that phrase for a second. Perpetual vibration between tyranny and anarchy. A city would collapse into chaos, into mob rule and street violence, and then out of that chaos a strongman would seize control, and the city would swing into tyranny, and then the tyrant would be overthrown and it would collapse back into chaos again. Back and forth. Generation after generation. Never landing anywhere stable.

And Hamilton, who can write with real beauty when he wants to, lingers here. He says that when these republics did have moments of calm, the calm only served as a short-lived contrast to the furious storms that were coming. When you glimpse an interval of happiness in their history, he says, you look at it with regret, because you know the pleasant scene is about to be overwhelmed by the tempestuous waves of sedition and party rage. And when a flash of glory does break through the gloom, some moment of brilliance, some great achievement, it dazzles you for an instant, and then it just makes you mourn. Because you realize that the vices of those governments wasted talent that magnificent. The soil produced extraordinary people, and the politics ruined them.

That is the set-piece Hamilton chooses to open with, and it is worth asking why. Why begin a paper meant to sell the Constitution with a tour of failed republics?

Because of who was using that history, and what they were using it for. This is the part a modern listener can miss. In Hamilton's time, the collapse of the ancient republics was not just a sad story. It was a political weapon. And it was being used against him.

Hamilton names the people wielding it. He calls them the advocates of despotism. These were the voices, in Europe and even in America, who looked at Athens and Florence and said, there is your proof. Free government does not work. Self-government always ends in this. The mob, the demagogue, the strongman, the collapse. They argued, Hamilton says, not just against particular forms of republican government, but against the very principles of civil liberty itself. They had decided that freedom and order could not coexist, and they took what he calls a malicious exultation, an ugly satisfaction, in pointing at every failed republic as evidence.

So Hamilton is fighting on contested ground from the first sentence. And he does something honest here, something that makes the whole paper stronger. He does not deny the history. He could have tried to argue that Athens and Florence were not really that bad, or not really republics, or not really relevant. He does not. He concedes it. He says, plainly, that the dark portraits the advocates of despotism have sketched of republican government were too just copies of the originals. Too accurate. The picture is real. The ancient republics really did fail, really did tear themselves apart, really did vibrate between tyranny and anarchy.

So why isn't that the end of the argument? If self-government really did collapse everywhere it was tried in the ancient world, why should anyone believe America will be different?

And this is the hinge of the entire paper. Hamilton's answer is one of the most quietly radical ideas in the whole Federalist series. He says that the science of politics, like most other sciences, has received great improvement.

The science of politics. Hamilton is claiming that governing is not a fixed art, frozen since antiquity. It is a body of knowledge. And like any science, it accumulates. It learns. The reason America does not have to repeat the fate of Athens is not that Americans are better people than the Athenians. It is that Americans, in seventeen eighty-seven, simply know things about how to build a government that the ancient world did not know. The ancients were not stupid. They were early. They were working without tools that have since been discovered.

And then Hamilton names the tools. He gives a list of what he calls discoveries, improvements in the science of politics that were either unknown to the ancients or barely understood by them. There are four of them, and they are worth taking one at a time, because together they are very close to a description of the machine the Constitution is trying to build.

The first is the regular distribution of power into distinct departments. Separating the government into branches, so that the people who make the laws are not the same people who carry them out or judge them. The ancient republics tended to pile power into a single assembly. Modern design pulls it apart on purpose.

The second is the introduction of legislative balances and checks. Not just separating the branches, but arming each one with the means to push back on the others. Giving the parts of government the ability to resist each other, so that no single part can run away with the whole thing.

The third is an independent judiciary. In Hamilton's words, the institution of courts composed of judges holding their offices during good behavior. Judges who keep their jobs as long as they do them honestly, which means they cannot be fired for ruling against the powerful. A court that does not have to flatter anyone to survive.

And the fourth is representation itself. The representation of the people in the legislature by deputies of their own election. This is the one that sounds most obvious to us, and that is exactly the point. The ancient republics largely expected the citizens to show up in person, in the square, and vote directly, which only worked in a city small enough to fit in a square. The idea that a free people could govern themselves across a large territory by electing representatives to act for them, that was not obvious. It was an invention.

Hamilton's claim is that these four things, taken together, are powerful means by which the excellences of republican government may be retained and its imperfections lessened or avoided. You get to keep what is good about self-government, the liberty, the consent, while engineering out the failure modes that destroyed Athens.

Now, a fair question here is whether this is just Hamilton being optimistic. Is the science of politics really a thing, or is that just a flattering phrase? And it is worth pausing, because history has actually tested this idea, and Hamilton turns out to have been onto something durable.

Look at what happened after the Second World War. When West Germany sat down to write its constitution in nineteen forty-nine, it did not start from scratch and it did not just copy the ancients. It studied failure. It studied, specifically, how the previous German republic had collapsed into dictatorship, and it deliberately engineered protections against the exact mechanisms that had failed. That is the science of politics treated as a science. So is post-apartheid South Africa in the nineteen nineties, building a constitutional court and a bill of rights with the express knowledge of how power had been abused before. Each generation of constitution-writers now reads the previous generation's mistakes the way an engineer reads a report on a collapsed bridge. That is precisely Hamilton's claim. Government design accumulates. We are not doomed to the ancient cycle, because we are not starting where the ancients started.

But Hamilton is not done with his list. Because after naming those four well-known improvements, he says he wants to add one more. And he flags it as his own contribution, something that, in his words, may appear novel to some. He wants to add to the catalogue a fifth idea. He calls it the enlargement of the orbit.

The enlargement of the orbit. It is an astronomical image. He is picturing a republic as something that revolves within a certain space, and he is saying, make the space bigger. Enlarge the orbit within which a free government operates. That can mean a single large state instead of a tiny one. Or, and this is the part that matters for this paper, it can mean consolidating several smaller states into one great confederacy.

And here Hamilton has walked straight into a fight. Because he openly admits something. He says this fifth principle, the enlargement of the orbit, has been made the foundation of an objection to the new Constitution. In other words, the very thing Hamilton is calling an improvement, his opponents are calling the Constitution's fatal flaw. They are saying the proposed government is too big. That a republic stretched across thirteen states and all that territory cannot possibly stay free. It is too large to be a republic at all.

And to make that argument, Hamilton's opponents had a star witness. The most respected political thinker of the age. A Frenchman named Montesquieu.

This is the heart of Federalist Number Nine, and it is genuinely one of the great rhetorical maneuvers in the whole series, so let me slow down and tell it properly.

You have to understand who Montesquieu was to the people reading these papers. Charles-Louis de Secondat, the Baron de Montesquieu, had published a book called The Spirit of the Laws decades earlier, and by seventeen eighty-seven it was something close to scripture for anyone thinking seriously about government. If you wanted to win a political argument in that era, the strongest move available to you was to show that Montesquieu was on your side. He was the authority. He was the expert witness everyone wanted to call.

And the Anti-Federalists, the people fighting ratification, had called him. Constantly. Because Montesquieu had written, very clearly, that a republic needs a small territory. That free self-government only works in a compact space. A small republic, he argued, can survive. A large one cannot. Stretch a republic too wide and it falls apart, or it slides into the rule of one man. The Anti-Federalists quoted this everywhere. With great assiduity, Hamilton says, they cited and circulated it. It was their best line. It came from the most trusted authority alive, and it seemed to say, in plain words, that the Constitution's whole design was impossible. America is too big to be one republic. Montesquieu says so.

So picture Hamilton's position. His opponents have the most respected name in political philosophy mounted on their side of the wall. What does he do?

He does not attack Montesquieu. That would be suicide. Instead, he does something far more devastating. He picks up his opponents' own witness, turns him around, and points him in the other direction.

Hamilton's first move is to ask a simple, deadly question. When Montesquieu said a republic must be small, how small did he actually mean? What was he picturing? And the answer is that Montesquieu was picturing the ancient city-states. He was reasoning from Athens. From the little republics of antiquity. And here is the thing. By that standard, Hamilton points out, almost every single American state is already too big. Virginia is too big to be Montesquieu's republic. So is Massachusetts. So is Pennsylvania, New York, North Carolina, Georgia. They already blow past the dimensions Montesquieu had in mind.

Watch what that does. It springs a trap on the Anti-Federalists. Because if you take Montesquieu's small-republic rule as the strict measure of truth, Hamilton says, you are left with only two options, and both of them are catastrophes. Option one. You admit no free government can work at this scale, and you take refuge in the arms of monarchy. You give up on the republic entirely. Option two. You start chopping. You break the big states down into smaller and smaller pieces, splitting America into, in Hamilton's wonderful phrase, an infinity of little, jealous, clashing, tumultuous commonwealths. Tiny squabbling statelets. The wretched nurseries of unceasing discord. He even notes, with some disgust, that a few writers on the other side had actually started hinting that breaking up the large states might be a good thing. Hamilton's response to that is acid. He says that kind of policy might serve the ambitions of small men who want a multiplication of petty offices to scramble for, but it could never promote the greatness or the happiness of the American people.

So that is the first half of the jiu-jitsu. Hamilton has shown that the Anti-Federalists' favorite quote, taken seriously, doesn't argue for the America that exists. It argues for either monarchy or for shattering the country into fragments. Their own authority, followed honestly, leads somewhere they do not want to go.

But then Hamilton delivers the real blow. And this is the move that makes the whole thing sing.

He says, in effect, that his opponents read part of Montesquieu's book and stopped. They quoted the passage about small republics, and they did not turn the page. Because elsewhere in the very same work, Montesquieu addresses this exact problem, and he proposes a solution, and the solution he proposes is the confederate republic.

Montesquieu, it turns out, had seen the dilemma himself. He knew small republics were vulnerable, especially to being conquered from outside. And his answer was a form of government that lets several small republics join together into a larger one, so that they keep the internal freedom of a small republic while gaining the external strength of a large state. He had a name for it. The confederate republic. He had, Hamilton says, explicitly treated it as the expedient for extending the sphere of popular government.

Let that land. The Anti-Federalists said Montesquieu proves the Constitution is impossible. And Hamilton has just shown that Montesquieu personally described, recommended, and named the exact kind of government the Constitution is trying to create. The star witness for the prosecution turns out to have been a witness for the defense the whole time.

Hamilton does quote Montesquieu directly here, and I will give you the line that matters most, because it is the keystone. Montesquieu wrote that mankind would have been forced, eventually, to live under the government of a single person, that is, under monarchy, had they not contrived a kind of constitution that has all the internal advantages of a republican, together with the external force of a monarchical government. And then the name. I mean, Montesquieu wrote, a confederate republic.

That is the whole argument in one borrowed sentence. Montesquieu is saying the confederate republic is what saves free people from having to surrender to a king. It is the thing that lets you be both free and strong. Hamilton barely has to add anything. He just says he has quoted these passages at length on purpose, because they are a luminous abridgment of the arguments for the Union, and because they wipe out the false impression that a misreading of Montesquieu had created. He let the opposition's own expert close the argument for him.

So why does this matter beyond the clever debating trick? Because it reframes what the Constitution even is. The Anti-Federalists wanted you to think the choice was between small, safe, familiar republics on one hand, and some dangerous oversized novelty on the other. Hamilton, using Montesquieu, says no. The confederate republic is not a novelty and it is not a danger. It is the well-known, expert-endorsed answer to a problem free governments have always faced. The Constitution is not a leap into the dark. It is the application of the best available political science.

Now, there is one more objection Hamilton has to clear away before he is done, and it is a more technical one, but he handles it in the same spirit.

Some of his opponents had drawn a sharp line between two words. A confederacy, and a consolidation. And they argued that a true confederacy has very specific features. They said a real confederacy can only act on the member states as whole units, never reaching the individual citizens directly. They said the central council must have no role in any matter of internal administration. And they said every member must have an exactly equal vote, the smallest state and the largest state weighing the same. By that definition, they argued, the proposed Constitution is not a real confederacy at all. It is a consolidation. It is the states being swallowed.

Hamilton's response is that this whole definition is, in his words, more subtle than accurate. It is arbitrary. It is, he says, supported neither by principle nor precedent. Nobody handed down these rules. They were invented to make the Constitution fail a test it was never required to pass. The real definition of a confederate republic, Hamilton says, is much simpler. It is just an assemblage of societies. An association of two or more states into one state. As long as the member states keep their own separate organization and their own real powers for local purposes, you still have a confederacy, in fact and in theory, even if those states are subordinate to the general government on national questions. And the proposed Constitution, he points out, does exactly that. It does not abolish the state governments. It makes them constituent parts of the national sovereignty. It even gives them a direct voice, their representation in the Senate, and leaves real and important powers in their hands.

And here Hamilton reaches for his last set-piece, his piece of hard historical proof. The Lycian confederacy.

This is the kind of example a modern listener has almost certainly never heard of, and Hamilton uses it precisely because it is concrete. Lycia was an ancient league, down in what is now southern Turkey, made up of twenty-three cities that had bound themselves together. And Lycia is interesting because of how it handled the two things the Anti-Federalists insisted a real confederacy could never do.

First, the voting. The Anti-Federalists said every member of a true confederacy must vote equally. Lycia did not do that. In the common council of Lycia, the largest cities got three votes, the middle-sized cities got two, and the smallest got one. The big members carried more weight. Exactly the kind of arrangement the Anti-Federalists said was forbidden.

And second, and this is the sharper one, the common council of Lycia actually appointed the judges and the magistrates inside the individual cities. Hamilton himself calls this the most delicate species of interference in their internal administration. Think about how far that reaches. If there is anything you would expect a city to control for itself, it is the choosing of its own officials. And the Lycian central council reached right into that. So here is an ancient confederacy that, by the Anti-Federalists' strict definition, was not a confederacy at all. And yet, Hamilton notes, Montesquieu, the same Montesquieu, looked at Lycia and wrote that if he were to give a model of an excellent confederate republic, it would be the model of Lycia.

So the example does two jobs at once. It proves, with a real historical case, that a confederate government can reach inside its members and weight its members unequally and still be a confederacy. And it once again has Montesquieu, the opposition's own authority, calling exactly that arrangement excellent. The supposedly iron rules that the Constitution was accused of breaking turn out to be, in Hamilton's phrase, the novel refinements of an erroneous theory. Somebody made them up. The actual history of confederate government does not support them.

It is worth pausing here on how alive this argument still is. We have, in our own time, a working example of a confederate republic, and it shows the same tension Hamilton was describing. The European Union is, in many ways, a modern attempt at exactly this. Sovereign states that keep their own governments, their own internal life, their own identity, but bind themselves together for shared strength, shared markets, shared security. And the European Union has spent its whole existence arguing about precisely the question Hamilton was arguing about. How much should the central authority be able to reach the individual citizen directly? How much should bigger members count for more? Where is the line between a confederacy and a consolidation? Hamilton would have recognized that debate immediately. He would have said it is the permanent debate of any confederate republic, and that the line is, as he put it, a mere matter of discretion. There is no magic formula. There is only design.

So step back and look at what Hamilton has built in this short paper.

He started in a graveyard, with the wreckage of the ancient republics, and he conceded the wreckage was real. Then he refused to let it be the end of the story, because, he said, the science of politics has learned things since then. He named the improvements. The separation of powers, the checks and balances, the independent courts, and representation. And then he added his own, the enlargement of the orbit, the idea that a republic can be made safer by being made larger. He took the opposition's most powerful witness, Montesquieu, and showed that Montesquieu had personally endorsed the confederate republic as the way to make free government both large and lasting. And he closed by showing, with the example of Lycia, that the rigid definitions used to attack the Constitution were never real rules at all.

And underneath all of it is the promise in the title. The Union as a safeguard against domestic faction. Hamilton's deepest point is that the thing that killed Athens and Florence, faction, internal division, the tearing-apart, is not cured by making your republic small and pure. It is contained by binding republics together, so that when faction flares up in one part, the soundness of the rest can hold it, reform it, and quell it. A single small republic that catches fire burns down. A confederate republic has the rest of itself to put the fire out.

That idea, faction and how a well-built republic survives it, is about to get the deepest treatment it will ever receive. Because the next paper is not Hamilton's. Federalist Number Ten is the first paper in this series written by James Madison. And it takes this exact question, what do you do about faction, and it does not just defend the large republic as adequate. It argues that the large republic is actually better at controlling faction than the small one ever could be. It is the most famous of all the Federalist Papers, and it turns Hamilton's safeguard into a full theory.
