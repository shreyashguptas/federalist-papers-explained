At the end of the last episode, Madison finished the case for union — and he finished it twice. First with a ruler, then with his heart. Federalist Number Fourteen answered the oldest objection to the Constitution — that America was simply too big to govern itself — by drawing a line between two words everyone kept confusing. A democracy needs all its citizens in one place. A republic only needs their representatives. Then Madison measured the country with a map, showed that its representatives had already been meeting for thirteen years, and predicted that roads and canals would keep shrinking the distances. And at the very end, the quietest man of the founding generation wrote the most passionate passage in all eighty-five papers — about the mingled blood that consecrated the union, and about America's right to do what has never been done before.

That was the end of the first movement. Fourteen essays, three writers, one question: why should America be one country? The case is closed. The jury has heard it all.

Which leaves a problem. Because there was one group of readers in seventeen eighty-seven who could nod along with every word of those fourteen essays and still vote against the Constitution. They could agree that union prevents war, that union keeps America out of European hands, that a large republic is the best protection against faction, that the country is not too big — agree with all of it — and then simply say: fine. Union matters. And we already have one.

And they were right about that last part. This is the thing that is easy to forget about seventeen eighty-seven. America was not lawless, and it was not divided. It had a constitution — the Articles of Confederation. It had a national legislature — the Confederation Congress — that had been meeting for years. It had won a war, signed a peace treaty, and created a system for settling the western lands. The United States, as a union, already existed. So the real question facing the country was never just whether union is good. It was whether the union America already had could survive. And if the answer was yes — if the existing system could be patched, amended, improved — then there was no reason to take the enormous risk of tearing it down and starting over.

That question is what the second movement of this series is about. Starting today, the Federalist Papers stop defending the idea of union and start attacking the government that was supposed to be preserving it. And the man who leads the attack is the man who had been waiting to make it for ten years.

Federalist Number Fifteen was published in the Independent Journal on Saturday, December first, seventeen eighty-seven — the day after Madison's Number Fourteen. It is Alexander Hamilton's, and it is the angriest essay in the series so far. Last time, I said that in this paper Hamilton stops being a lawyer making a case and becomes a doctor delivering a diagnosis. That is exactly the shape of it. The symptoms. The disease. And the reason the disease is fatal.

But before the diagnosis, you need to know something about the doctor. Because the anger in this paper did not begin in seventeen eighty-seven. It began a decade earlier, in a freezing camp in Pennsylvania, and Hamilton had been carrying it ever since.

This series has met Hamilton the lawyer, Hamilton the economist, Hamilton the man who would become the first Secretary of the Treasury. But there is an earlier Hamilton we have not talked about yet, and he is the one writing this paper. Before any of that, Hamilton was a soldier. He joined the Revolution as a young artillery officer, and while he was still in his early twenties he was pulled onto George Washington's personal staff — his aide-de-camp, the man who drafted the general's letters, read the dispatches, and saw everything that crossed the commander's desk for four years of war.

And what crossed that desk, winter after winter, was failure. Not military failure — political failure. In the winter the army camped at Valley Forge, soldiers starved and froze in a country that had food and clothing to spare. The supplies existed. The system for getting them to the army did not, because Congress had no power to make anyone provide them. It could only ask the states. The states, busy with their own problems, mostly did not answer. Hamilton watched men die of that arithmetic. He wrote the letters begging the states for help. He read the polite refusals.

By seventeen eighty, still in the middle of the war, Hamilton — in his mid-twenties — had seen enough to write the whole argument down. He sent a long letter to James Duane, a member of Congress from New York, laying out what he saw as the fundamental defect of the entire system. Congress, he wrote, had no real power, and the power it did have on paper it could not enforce. The letter reads like a rough draft of the essay he would publish seven years later. He was not reacting to a crisis. He was describing a design.

And the war kept proving him right, all the way to the end and past it. In seventeen eighty-three, with the fighting over, the army that had won the Revolution was camped at Newburgh, New York — unpaid, some of the men owed years of back wages, because Congress could not raise the money to settle its debts to its own soldiers. The anger among the officers grew dangerous enough that, for a few weeks that March, it was a real question whether the army that had just won the Revolution might turn on the government that could not pay it. It took George Washington himself, in person, to talk his officers back from the edge — not over ideology, but over the simple fact that their government could not pay its bills.

Then came the peace, and the failure just changed clothes. In seventeen eighty-six, Hamilton was one of the delegates at a convention in Annapolis, Maryland, called to fix the way the states were strangling each other's trade. Twelve delegates showed up. Twelve — from five states. Most of the country did not bother to send anyone. The convention could do nothing, and a different man would have gone home and written a bitter letter about it. Hamilton instead drafted the convention's official report and slipped into it an invitation: since the problems were too big for this meeting, let there be another one — in Philadelphia, the following May, to consider everything. That invitation became the Constitutional Convention.

So when you hear the fury in Federalist Number Fifteen, understand where it comes from. This is not a pamphleteer scoring points. This is a man who watched soldiers starve because of a flaw in a document, who wrote the diagnosis while the war was still being fought and was ignored, and who has now, finally, gotten the country to sit still and listen. The paper is angry because the patient waited ten years to call the doctor.

So. The diagnosis. Hamilton starts with the symptoms, and his method is simple. He just describes what being an American in seventeen eighty-seven actually feels like — and he does it as a series of questions, each one a wound.

Let me walk you through the country he describes, because every stop on this tour is real.

Start at the northern frontier. Four years earlier, in the peace treaty that ended the Revolution, Britain promised to withdraw its troops from a chain of forts on American soil — at Detroit, at Niagara, at Oswego, along the Great Lakes. In seventeen eighty-seven, the British flag is still flying over every one of them. British garrisons, on American territory, four years after the war ended. Why? Because Britain knows America cannot do anything about it. And here Hamilton adds the most quietly devastating detail in the whole paper. Are we in a condition to resent or to repel the aggression, he asks? And he answers: we have neither troops, nor treasury, nor government. Then he attaches a footnote to that sentence — a footnote of five words. I mean for the Union. The states have governments. The nation, functionally, does not.

There is a second reason the British will not leave those forts, and it makes everything worse. The peace treaty was a two-way deal — and America has broken its own side of it. The treaty said British creditors could collect their prewar debts, and that the states would stop punishing people who had stayed loyal to the king during the war. The states simply refused, and Congress could not make them comply. So when American diplomats demand that Britain honor the treaty, Britain points at the states and asks why America does not honor it first. Hamilton concedes the point with gritted teeth. We are not even in a position to complain with dignity, he says, until we remove the just charges against our own conduct.

Now go west. The farmers settling Kentucky and Tennessee float their crops down the Mississippi to reach the sea — it is the only road they have. Spain closed that river to Americans three years ago. We talked about this back in Episode Four, when Jay used it as a warning about foreign powers. It is still true, and the government has done nothing about it, because there is nothing it can do.

Now cross the ocean to London, where John Adams has spent more than two years as America's first ambassador to Britain. He is there to negotiate a trade agreement and to get those forts back. He gets nothing. Not because he is a bad diplomat, but because the British government sees no reason to negotiate with him at all. Any treaty he signs would need thirteen state governments to actually honor it, and everyone in Europe has watched what American states do to treaties. Britain does not even bother to send an ambassador to America in return. Hamilton wraps that entire humiliation into one sentence: our ambassadors abroad are the mere pageants of mimic sovereignty. Costumes. Actors playing representatives of a country that, as far as Europe can tell, does not really exist.

And now come home, to a kitchen table anywhere in America. The man sitting at it fought in the Revolution, and the government still owes him for it. The debt from the war — owed to foreign lenders, owed to American citizens, owed to the soldiers themselves — sits unpaid, with no plan to pay it. Public credit is gone. Private credit has dried up with it, because nobody lends in a country where debts are optional. The value of land has collapsed. Hamilton runs through all of it, question after question, and then stops and delivers the sentence this whole episode has been building toward — the one I quoted to you at the end of the last episode.

"We may indeed with propriety be said to have reached almost the last stage of national humiliation."

Not a dangerous moment. Not a difficult chapter. The last stage. Hamilton is saying that national failure is a terminal process, and America is in the final phase of it. His claim is that there was scarcely a single way for a nation to be humiliated that America was not, at that very moment, experiencing.

Those are the symptoms. And here is where a lesser writer would have stopped, because the symptoms alone make a powerful case. But symptoms are not a diagnosis. At this point you might be asking the question Hamilton wants you to ask: how does this happen? How does a country win a war against the most powerful empire on earth and then, four years later, find itself unable to pay a bill or enforce a treaty or answer an insult? It is not because Americans are lazy or stupid or poor. The country is bursting with natural advantages — Hamilton says so in this very paper. So what is the disease?

Hamilton's answer is one sentence, and it is the most important sentence in this entire stretch of the Federalist Papers. Here it is.

"The great and radical vice in the construction of the existing Confederation is in the principle of legislation for states or governments, in their corporate or collective capacities, and as contradistinguished from the individuals of which they consist."

That sentence has some eighteenth-century starch in it, so let me say it plainly. Under the Articles of Confederation, the national government makes laws for states, not for people. Every law Congress passes is addressed to thirteen governments, never to a single citizen. Congress cannot tax you. It can only tell Virginia to collect taxes and send the money in. Congress cannot draft you. It can only ask Massachusetts for soldiers. Congress never touches a person. It only ever sends instructions to governments — and then waits, and hopes.

Back in Episode Twelve, talking about taxes, we put it this way: a government that cannot collect money is not really a government, it is a suggestion. Federalist Number Fifteen is the paper where Hamilton explains why it is a suggestion. Listen to how he describes what happens to Congress's laws in the real world.

"In theory their resolutions concerning those objects are laws, constitutionally binding on the members of the Union, yet in practice they are mere recommendations which the states observe or disregard at their option."

Laws in theory. Recommendations in practice. The states treat acts of Congress the way you treat a suggestion from a neighbor — something to consider, weigh against your own convenience, and feel free to ignore.

If you want to feel how alive this problem still is, look at the world's attempts to govern groups of countries today. The United Nations passes resolutions that member states ignore whenever compliance is inconvenient — and the only enforcement options are persuasion, sanctions, or war. Or take the alliance we mentioned back in Episode Four — NATO, the most successful military alliance in modern history. Its members once pledged to spend two percent of their economies on defense. For years, most of them simply did not do it. And nothing happened to them — no fine, no court, no penalty — because nothing could happen to them. An agreement among sovereign governments has exactly as much force as the governments choose to give it, and not one ounce more. That is not a flaw in those organizations. That is what they are. Hamilton's point is that in seventeen eighty-seven, that is all the United States was. Not a country. A treaty wearing the costume of a country.

So why does that matter so much? Why is a government of states doomed, rather than just slow and frustrating? This is where Hamilton goes deepest, and the logic deserves to be walked through slowly, because once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

Government, Hamilton says, means the power to make laws. And a law, to actually be a law, needs one thing attached to it.

"It is essential to the idea of a law, that it be attended with a sanction; or, in other words, a penalty or punishment for disobedience. If there be no penalty annexed to disobedience, the resolutions or commands which pretend to be laws will, in fact, amount to nothing more than advice or recommendation."

A law without a penalty is advice. Think about how true that is at every scale of life. A speed limit with no police and no tickets is a polite opinion painted on a sign. A tax code with no consequences for cheating is a donation request. The thing that turns words into law is not the words. It is what happens to you if you ignore them.

So every real government needs a way to punish disobedience. And Hamilton says there are exactly two instruments that can do it. The courts — judges, sheriffs, fines, jail. Or the army. The magistracy or the sword. Now here is the catch, and it is the hinge of the whole argument. Courts work on people. A court can summon a person, try a person, fine a person, jail a person. But a court cannot jail Virginia. There is no courtroom big enough to hold a state. Against a state — against a government — the only penalty that exists is force. Which means that a national government whose laws are addressed to states has exactly one enforcement tool: war against its own members. Every act of disobedience — every ignored tax requisition, every violated treaty obligation — leaves the Union a choice between two options: let it go, or send an army.

Hamilton states the consequence in the coldest line of the paper. In a system like this, he writes, every breach of the laws must involve a state of war, and military execution must become the only instrument of civil obedience. A government of states does not get to have law enforcement. It gets to choose between surrender and civil war, every single time, forever. And since no sane person will start a war over a late tax payment, the real choice, every single time, is surrender. That is why the laws became recommendations. Not because anyone decided they should be. Because the design left no other possibility.

This — right here — is why the Articles of Confederation cannot be fixed by amendment. And this is the answer to the objection I mentioned at the top of the episode, the people who said: we already have a union, just improve it. You cannot improve it, Hamilton says, because the flaw is not a missing power that can be added. The flaw is the addressee. The system is built, from its foundation, to talk to governments instead of people. Hamilton's image is architectural: the defects are not in the walls or the roof, they are fundamental errors in the structure of the building, which cannot be fixed except by changing the first principles, the main pillars of the fabric. You do not renovate a building whose foundation is the problem. You build a new one.

And now you can see what the Constitution actually is — the deep design change underneath all the details. The Constitution's answer to Federalist Number Fifteen is that the national government will pass over the states entirely and act directly on individual citizens. A federal law under the Constitution is addressed to you. A federal tax is owed by you. A federal court can summon you. That single change — switching the addressee of the law from governments to persons — is what turns the Union from a treaty into a country. It means the national government enforces its laws the way every real government does: with courts, on people, one case at a time. Not with armies, on states. If you remember Episode Nine, this is exactly the question the European Union has spent its whole existence wrestling with — how directly the central authority can reach individual citizens. The countries that designed the EU were, in a real sense, working through Hamilton's problem. He got there first, by watching the failure from the inside.

Now, there is an objection forming here, and it is a fair one. All this talk of penalties and enforcement and coercion — does it have to be this grim? These thirteen states fought a war side by side. They share a cause, a history, a continent. Surely, on the important things, they would mostly do their part voluntarily — not because they fear punishment, but because it is right, and because it is in their own interest. Why build everything around force?

Hamilton has been waiting for that question his whole adult life, and his answer is the philosophical core of the paper. He asks: why does government exist at all? Why do human beings, anywhere, ever, need laws with penalties instead of just agreements and good intentions?

"Why has government been instituted at all? Because the passions of men will not conform to the dictates of reason and justice, without constraint."

That is Hamilton's entire political philosophy in one sentence, and you have heard it from him before. Back in Episode Six, it was: men are ambitious, vindictive, and rapacious. People do not follow rules because rules are reasonable. They follow rules because something happens to them if they do not. Build a system that depends on people voluntarily doing the right thing, and you have built a system that fails the first time doing the right thing becomes expensive.

And then he adds a twist that makes it worse — an observation that modern psychology has spent a century confirming. Groups, Hamilton says, behave worse than individuals. A person who would be ashamed to break a promise will happily vote, inside a committee, to break the same promise — because in a group, the blame spreads thin. Shame loses its grip, he says, when the infamy of a bad action is divided among many instead of falling on one. A state legislature can do, with a clear conscience, what no individual legislator would do under his own name. So a union that runs on the voluntary good faith of thirteen legislatures is leaning on the weakest form of conscience that exists: the conscience of a committee.

And there is one more force pushing in the same direction. Power itself. Anyone who holds power, Hamilton observes, develops an allergy to being told what to do with it. Every state government, simply because it is a government, will bristle at every instruction from the center — will look for reasons why this particular requisition is unfair, why this particular treaty obligation does not quite apply to them. Hamilton reaches for an image from astronomy, the science of his century: in any system built out of smaller sovereignties, there is an eccentric tendency in the lesser orbs — a constant pull in each state to fly off from the common center. He is describing a solar system whose planets hate the sun. Hold it together with nothing but goodwill, and it does not slowly drift apart. It flies apart.

So now put the whole machine in motion and watch what actually happened — because this is not a prediction, it is a history of the last six years. Congress sends a requisition to the states: here is what each of you owes for the war debt and the costs of the Union. The states closest to danger, or most committed to the cause, pay some of it. Others pay less. Some pay nothing at all. And then the paying states look around. Hamilton reconstructs their exact thoughts: why should we do more in proportion than those who are embarked with us in the same political voyage? Why should we consent to bear more than our proper share of the common burden? It is the group project problem, the one every student knows — when the grade is shared but the work is voluntary, the workers eventually go on strike against the free riders. Except here the group project is a country. Each state cuts back to match the laziest member, which lowers the bar again, which justifies the next state cutting back further. Compliance does not just decline. It spirals — each state's selfishness justified by the selfishness of the others, all the way down to zero.

By the time Hamilton is writing, the spiral is nearly complete. The requisitions of the seventeen eighties brought in a fraction of what Congress asked — in the worst years, pennies on the dollar. The national government had stopped paying even the interest on its debts. And Congress itself was dissolving in the most undramatic way imaginable: states simply stopped sending delegates. There were stretches when the Congress of the United States — the entire national government — could not gather enough members to legally do anything at all. No coup, no invasion, no dramatic collapse. Just an empty room. Here is Hamilton's description of where the spiral ends, and it is the last quote of the day.

"Each state, yielding to the persuasive voice of immediate interest or convenience, has successively withdrawn its support, till the frail and tottering edifice seems ready to fall upon our heads, and to crush us beneath its ruins."

A building, abandoned by its own inhabitants one at a time, swaying over the people still inside. That is Hamilton's picture of the United States government in December of seventeen eighty-seven. Not attacked. Not overthrown. Just walked away from — because it was built in such a way that walking away cost nothing.

Which brings us back, at the end, to the people Hamilton is actually arguing with. Because here is the strange thing about this paper — and you might have been wondering this for the whole episode. If the government's failure was this obvious, this visible to everyone, then who is on the other side? Who was defending the Articles of Confederation in seventeen eighty-seven?

Almost nobody — and that is exactly Hamilton's problem. The opponents of the Constitution did not stand up and say the system works. They admitted the system was failing, admitted something had to be done, and then opposed every change that would actually do something. They wanted Congress to be stronger, but without taking any sovereignty away from the states. They wanted the Union to have real authority, and the states to keep full independence — both at once. Hamilton has a name for this. He says these people cherish, with blind devotion, the political monster of an imperium in imperio — Latin, for a government inside a government. An empire within an empire. Two final authorities in the same territory, which is a contradiction in terms, because when they disagree, one of them turns out not to be final.

This is the choice Hamilton refuses to let his readers escape. There is no middle option. Either the national government reaches individual citizens directly — in which case it is a real government, and the states have given up a piece of their sovereignty — or it works through the states as a league of friends — in which case everything you have watched fail for the last six years keeps failing, forever, no matter how many amendments you stack on top of it. Strong union with fully sovereign states is not a compromise. It is a monster — a thing that cannot exist. Every generation since has had to relearn this, every time it designs an alliance or a league or a union of countries and watches it stall in exactly the way Hamilton said it would.

So that is Federalist Number Fifteen. The symptoms: a country in the last stage of national humiliation — broke, ignored, occupied, and unable to answer for itself. The disease: a government that addresses states instead of people, whose every law is therefore a recommendation. And the prognosis: fatal, and beyond amendment, because a law without a penalty is advice, and the only penalty you can impose on a state is war.

This is also the moment to say where the series goes from here, because Federalist Number Fifteen opens a new chapter. This paper and the seven that follow it — numbers fifteen through twenty-two — are the autopsy of the Articles of Confederation. Hamilton writes most of them, with Madison stepping in for three essays on the confederacies of history and how every one of them died the same death. Together they are going to take the old system apart piece by piece: its design, its record, and the specific ways each of its powers failed. The first movement asked you to want the Union. This one is designed to leave you no choice.

And the very next paper picks up the most dangerous thread in this one. Today, Hamilton showed that a government of states can only enforce its laws by the sword. Federalist Number Sixteen asks the question that follows: what would that actually look like? Suppose Congress did try to enforce a requisition against a state that refused to pay — try to coerce Virginia, or Massachusetts, or New York, with soldiers. Walk through what happens next. The answer is the darkest scenario in the entire Federalist Papers, and it is the final proof that the old system was not just weak but dangerous — because a union held together by force is not a union at all. It is a permanent civil war, waiting for its first battle.
