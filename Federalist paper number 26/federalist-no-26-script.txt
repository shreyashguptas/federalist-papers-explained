At the end of the last episode, Hamilton had finished tearing something down. For two papers he had been circling the same fear, the fear of a standing army kept up in peacetime, and by the close of Number Twenty-Five he had taken the safeguard that frightened people kept reaching for and shown that it was no safeguard at all. A written ban on peacetime armies, he argued, either forbids nothing, because somebody still has to decide when the danger has passed and that somebody is the government itself, or else it forbids everything, and leaves a nation shackled by its own constitution, unable to prepare for a blow until the blow has already fallen. And any ban in between, he warned, would be trampled the first time real necessity walked through the door, taking a little of the constitution's dignity with it each time.

So by the end of Twenty-Five, the comfortable answers were gone. You cannot hand the common defense to the states, and you cannot make a free people safe by writing the army out of existence on a piece of paper. Which leaves the hardest question of the whole debate just sitting there, unanswered. If a paper prohibition will not protect a free people from the very soldiers it pays to defend itself, then what will? What does a real safeguard actually look like?

That is the entire business of today's paper. And Hamilton's answer is not a wall of words. It is a leash. A leash the government is forced to re-tie every two years, held by people who have to look their own voters in the eye while they hold it.

This is Federalist Number Twenty-Six. Still Alexander Hamilton. Still signed Publius. It went out for the Independent Journal in December of seventeen eighty-seven, the argument rolling on from the last one without a pause. The title is a mouthful, but it tells you exactly where Hamilton has planted his feet. The idea of restraining the legislative authority in regard to the common defense, considered. Restraining the legislature. That is the thing on trial today. Not the army. The proposal to tie the hands of the people's own representatives so that they could never raise one.

Let me start with the instinct Hamilton is arguing against, because it is not a foolish instinct, and he is honest enough to say so. The people who wanted that ban were not villains. They loved liberty. They had just fought a war for it. And they had noticed, correctly, that armies are dangerous to free societies. Their mistake was in the cure, not in the diagnosis. Hamilton has a wonderful phrase for it. The whole idea of restraining the legislature this way, he says, is one of those refinements that owe their origin to a zeal for liberty more ardent than enlightened. A zeal more ardent than enlightened. More heat than light. They cared about the right thing, and then let that care run right past the point where it still made sense.

And here is the deep principle underneath Hamilton's objection, the one everything else in the paper grows out of. You cannot build a government on the refusal to trust anyone with anything. At some point, power has to be handed to somebody. That is not a flaw in the design. That is what it means to have a government at all. In Hamilton's words, confidence must be placed somewhere, and the necessity of doing it is implied in the very act of delegating power.

The moment you create a government and give it a job, you have already decided to trust it with whatever that job requires. You can watch it. You can limit it. You can vote it out. What you cannot do is hand it the duty of defending the nation and then forbid it the means, and still have something that works like a government. Better, Hamilton says, to risk that your confidence might occasionally be abused than to cripple the whole enterprise with restrictions that leave the country defenseless. A free people has to place its bet on trust somewhere. The only real question is where you place the bet, and how closely you watch the table.

So where did this instinct come from in the first place, this deep American reflex that a standing army in peacetime is the very shape tyranny takes? Hamilton thinks it is worth answering carefully, because the fear is inherited, and once you see where it was inherited from, you see that Americans had picked up the fear while quietly dropping the lesson that came with it. And to tell that story, he takes his readers across the ocean, and back about seven hundred years.

The story begins in England, right after the Norman Conquest, in the years after ten sixty-six. In those early centuries, Hamilton reminds his readers, the power of the English monarch was very nearly absolute. The king was, in effect, the state. And then, over a long and slow grind of history, that power got clawed back, inch by inch. First the barons forced concessions out of the crown. Then, gradually, the people did. Century after century, the most fearsome powers of the monarchy were worn down and stripped away, until the crown was a shadow of the near-total authority it had once held.

But one thing, Hamilton says, was not truly settled until near the very end of that long story. And it is the thing that matters most for us. It was not until the revolution of sixteen eighty-eight, the one that put William, the Prince of Orange, on the English throne, that English liberty was, in Hamilton's word, completely triumphant. That is the moment the modern story of English freedom reaches its climax. And the question of the army sat right at the center of it.

Here is why. One of the crown's oldest and most jealously guarded powers was the power to make war, and folded inside that was the power to raise and keep soldiers. And English kings had used it. Hamilton gives his readers the specific numbers, because the numbers tell the story. Charles the Second, on nothing but his own royal authority, kept a body of five thousand regular troops on foot in a time of peace. His successor, James the Second, took that same private power and multiplied it, building the peacetime force up to about thirty thousand men, paid for out of his own household funds. Thirty thousand soldiers, kept in peacetime, answering to the king alone, funded by the king alone, asked for by nobody but the king.

That was the thing the revolution of sixteen eighty-eight was determined to end. And here we arrive at the single most important fact in the whole paper, the one everything else swings on. When the English finally moved to kill this dangerous power once and for all, at the very high tide of their liberty, with the old king driven out and a new settlement being written, what exactly did they do?

They did not ban armies.

They wrote, into the English Bill of Rights, that the raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace, unless with the consent of Parliament, was against law.

Read that clause slowly, because Hamilton wants you to catch precisely what it forbids and what it allows. It does not say there shall be no standing army. It says there shall be no standing army raised or kept by the king on his own say-so. Unless with the consent of Parliament. The whole weight of the reform falls on those few words. What the English feared was not the army as such. It was the army in the hands of one man acting alone. So they took the power out of the hands of the one man, and they placed it in the hands of the legislature, the many, the elected. And then they stopped.

This is the part Hamilton needs his readers to feel. These were not careless people. They were, in his words, too temperate, too well-informed to go one step further and try to bind the legislature itself. They understood something the American ban-writers had forgotten. They understood that some number of troops, for guards and garrisons and the ordinary business of a country, is simply necessary. That you can never draw a precise line in advance around every danger a nation might someday face. That a power big enough to meet any possible emergency has to live somewhere in the government. And once they had moved that power from the king to the people's representatives, they judged, rightly, that they had reached the last safe point of caution. To go further, to shackle the legislature too, would not be a gain for liberty. It would be a wound to the nation's ability to survive.

That is the lesson England actually taught. Not simply fear the army. The subtler, wiser lesson underneath it. Fear the army in the hands of the one who is not accountable to you. Move it into the hands of the ones who are. And then watch them.

Now Hamilton turns the ship toward home, and delivers the quiet, devastating point the whole English detour was built for. Americans inherited the fear. They just aimed it at the wrong target.

The English had learned to be jealous of a hereditary king. That jealousy made sense, because a king is not accountable to the people below him. But when the fear crossed the ocean, some Americans took that same suspicion, the suspicion built for a monarch, and pointed it straight at their own elected representatives, sitting in their own popular assemblies. And that, Hamilton says, is an error, an injudicious excess. Because your representatives are not a king. You choose them. You can throw them out. Aiming the anti-monarchy fear at them is like installing a heavy lock to keep out a burglar, and then using that same lock to bar the front door against the owner of the house.

And it gets worse than merely misplaced, because in most cases the restriction is not just wrong, it is meaningless. Here Hamilton makes a point so clean it is almost funny. In the state constitutions, who holds the power to raise an army in the first place? The legislature. Only the legislature. There is nobody else it could possibly belong to. So when a state solemnly declares that no army shall be raised without the consent of the legislature, it has announced, with a straight face, that a thing may not be done without the permission of the only body on earth that could ever do it. It was, Hamilton says, superfluous, if not absurd. You do not need to forbid the legislature from acting without its own consent. Its consent is the only way the thing ever happens at all.

A fair question here is whether this was some fringe worry that swept through the states. It was not, and the numbers make Hamilton's case for him. Out of all the American states, only two, Pennsylvania and North Carolina, ever tried to actually restrain the legislature's power over armies. Every other state declined. And Hamilton points, with a little quiet pride, at his own. The constitution of the State of New York, celebrated on both sides of the Atlantic as one of the best in the country, says nothing on the subject whatsoever. Total silence. The most admired state charter in America looked at the idea of a paper ban on armies and simply left it out.

And here is a small, sharp detail Hamilton cannot resist, because it gives away the whole game. Look closely, he says, even at the two states that did try. Read their actual words. They did not write that standing armies shall not be kept up in peacetime. They wrote that they ought not to be kept up. Ought not. Not shall not.

That may sound like a lawyer's quibble. It is not. It is a confession. The softness of that word, Hamilton says, was the result of a conflict between jealousy and conviction. Part of them wanted to slam the door on armies forever. And another part of them knew, deep down, that slamming that door would be reckless, that someday the state would genuinely need soldiers and would raise them no matter what the parchment said. So they split the difference inside a single weak verb. Ought not. A wish dressed up as a rule. And a rule written that softly, Hamilton says, is not really a law at all. It is a mere admonition, a piece of well-meant advice that the government will set aside the very moment it decides the situation calls for it.

And we do not have to imagine that happening, because we watched it happen last time. Remember Pennsylvania from the last episode, the state whose own bill of rights declared standing armies dangerous, and which then turned right around, in a season of peace, and raised troops anyway to deal with a little local trouble. There it is. The strongest paper rule any state had, folding in real time the first moment necessity leaned on it. The parchment lost.

So that is the demolition, finished at last. A ban on peacetime armies is a bad idea, and worse, an empty one. But Hamilton is not a man who leaves a hole where an answer should be. Tearing down the false safeguard only sharpens the real question. If not a ban, then what? What does the new Constitution put in the place of that comforting, useless wall?

And this is the heart of the paper, the thing Number Twenty-Five promised was coming. The Constitution's protection against a dangerous army is not a prohibition. It is a clock.

The new Constitution says that no money to support an army may be voted for longer than two years at a stretch. That is the whole mechanism, and it sounds almost boring, which is exactly why it is so easy to miss how much power is packed into it. Compare the two approaches directly, the way Hamilton does. The paper ban, he says, by aiming at too much, is calculated to effect nothing. It tries to forbid the army outright, fails, and protects you not at all. The two-year money rule aims at far less, and precisely because it aims at less, it actually works.

Here is what it does, in plain terms. Because the army's funding runs dry every two years, the national legislature is forced, over and over, forever, to come back to the question. They cannot fund an army once and then walk away. They cannot hand the president a permanent, standing pot of army money and forget about it. At least once every two years, they are obliged, in Hamilton's words, to deliberate upon the propriety of keeping a military force on foot, to come to a new resolution on the point, and to declare their sense of the matter by a formal vote in the face of their constituents.

In the face of their constituents. Sit with that phrase, because it is the whole design in six words. The vote is not hidden. It is not permanent. It is not a decision made once by dead men long ago and then left to run on its own forever. It is a live, recurring, public act, taken by named people who have to go home afterward and answer for it. A ban is a photograph, a single frozen moment of good intentions. This is a heartbeat. It has to happen again, and again, and again, and every single time it happens, the people get to watch.

So why does forcing a repeated public vote actually protect anyone? A rule that just makes the legislature vote again every two years does not, at first, sound like much of a shield against tyranny. And here is where you see how clear-eyed Hamilton really is about the way politics actually works, because his answer does not depend on politicians being good. It depends on them being ambitious and jealous of one another, which he is quite sure they always will be.

Think about what a recurring public vote on the army actually creates. It creates a permanent occasion for a fight. In any legislature, Hamilton says, there will always be an opposition, a party out of power, hungry to attack the party in power. And of all the topics such a party could pick to make trouble over, an army is the juiciest one imaginable. Voting money to keep soldiers on foot is, in his phrase, a favorable topic for declamation. It is the perfect thing to stand up and give a speech about. So every two years, like clockwork, when the army funding comes back around for its vote, the party in opposition has every incentive in the world to rise, point at it, and shout. And that shouting, whatever its motives, does something priceless. It drags the whole subject back into the daylight and yanks the public's attention onto it at exactly the moment a decision is being made. If the majority ever really did begin building something dangerous, the alarm would already be wired into the schedule.

And the watchdogs do not stop at the walls of the national legislature. Hamilton points to another set of eyes, and for his readers this was the reassuring part. The state governments. The states, he says, will always be watching the national government, and not neutrally. They will be, in his words, not only vigilant but suspicious and jealous guardians of the people's rights against any encroachment from above. Every time the army question comes around, the state legislatures will have their attention fixed on what the national rulers are doing. And if anything looks wrong, they are perfectly placed to sound the alarm to the whole country. In one of the most memorable lines in the paper, Hamilton says the states stand ready not only to be the voice, but, if necessary, the arm of their discontent.

The voice, and if necessary the arm. That is a striking thing to put down in writing. Hamilton is telling his readers that the states are not there merely to complain. In the last resort, they are organized bodies, with their own legitimacy and their own strength, capable of standing physically between the people and a national government that has genuinely gone wrong. The recurring vote lights the alarm. The opposition party and the states are the ones standing ready to answer it.

Now, if this machinery sounds familiar, it should, because it is the direct ancestor of the way military policy is actually fought over in America to this day. We do not protect ourselves from our own military with some untouchable clause carved into the Constitution. We protect ourselves with the power of the purse, exercised on a schedule. Every single year now, tighter even than Hamilton's two, the country goes through a loud, public, argued-over, reported-on fight about how much money the military gets and what it is allowed to be spent on. The party out of power still uses it as a favorite topic for declamation, exactly as Hamilton predicted. The press still drags it into the daylight. The mechanism Hamilton described in seventeen eighty-seven, a recurring, public, on-the-record vote as the real leash on military power, is not a historical curiosity. It is the machine still running.

But you might still be uneasy, and Hamilton knows it. Fine, you might say. There are votes, and watchers, and alarms. But what if a genuinely bad government simply decided to build its tyrant's army anyway, vote after vote, and grind the whole thing through in spite of all the noise? Could all this vigilance really stop a determined conspiracy?

Hamilton's answer to that is my favorite thing in the paper, and it turns on a single word. Time.

An army big enough to actually crush the liberties of a great nation, he points out, cannot be conjured into being overnight. It has to be built up slowly, by what he calls progressive augmentations, a little larger this year, a little larger the next, growing across a long stretch of years into something genuinely menacing. And that plain fact about how armies get big carries an enormous consequence. It means a plot to enslave a country with a standing army is never a single wicked decision. It has to be, in Hamilton's words, a continued conspiracy for a series of time. Not one bad vote. A bad vote repeated, deliberately, held to for years.

Now lay the two-year clock over the top of that, and watch what happens to the conspiracy. Every two years, the whole House of Representatives is elected fresh. And the Senate is not chosen all at once, but turns over one-third at a time on that very same two-year beat, so its membership too is in constant motion. Which means the plot cannot simply be carried by one fixed group of villains sitting still. It has to survive election, after election, after election, passed like a torch from one changing cast of lawmakers to the next, every one of them freshly returned from facing the voters at home.

And this is where Hamilton simply lets the sheer absurdity of it show. For the conspiracy to work, he says, you would have to believe that every single man who takes his seat in the national legislature becomes, the very instant he sits down, a traitor to the people who just elected him. You would have to believe that across all those years, and all those elections, and all those hundreds of different representatives and senators, not one honest man, and not even one merely ambitious man hungry to make a name for himself by exposing a scandal, ever notices what is happening and blows the whistle.

And then Hamilton drives it all the way to the end of the road, and the end is devastating. If you truly believe your representatives are that uniformly, permanently corrupt, he says, then you should not be arguing about a clause in the Constitution at all. You should give up on representative government entirely. Recall every power you have ever handed to anyone. Break the country apart into as many little states as there are counties, so that every citizen can manage his own affairs in person and trust nobody but himself. That, Hamilton says, is the only honest destination for that much suspicion. The paranoia does not argue for a paper ban on armies. Followed all the way to the end, it argues for having no government at all.

And there is one more nail. Even suppose such a monstrous conspiracy somehow existed and somehow held together across all those elections. It still could not hide. You cannot quietly assemble a vast army in a country that is at peace. The very act gives itself away. What possible innocent reason, Hamilton asks, could a government offer for suddenly raising an enormous military force when there is no war and no enemy anywhere in sight? The buildup itself would be the alarm bell. The people, he says, cannot be long deceived about a thing that size. And the discovery of the plot would be followed very quickly by the destruction of the plotters.

There was one last clever objection floating around, and Hamilton bats it away on his way out. What about this, a critic says. Once a ruler has an army big enough to frighten the people into submission, he could just use that very army to seize whatever he needs, and stop bothering with the legislature's two-year votes altogether. So does not your precious money clause become worthless the moment the army is large enough?

Hamilton's answer is to notice that the objection quietly assumes the very thing in dispute. It assumes the tyrant already has a giant army standing around in peacetime. But that is the whole question. On what pretext did he ever get it? If that huge force only came into being because of a real war, or a real rebellion, then we are no longer talking about a peacetime army at all, and the objection has wandered clean off its own point. Nobody sane argues that a country should refuse to raise soldiers to beat back an invasion or put down an uprising. The fear was always specifically about armies kept in peace. And in peace, the money clock keeps ticking, and the army still cannot grow into a monster without years of loud, public, repeated votes to feed it.

Hamilton lands the plane where this whole stretch of the Federalist has been heading all along. Every safeguard he has just described works far better in a united America than in a divided one. A single great union, guarded partly by the militia as a valuable and natural auxiliary, is exactly the situation in which the danger of a runaway army is smallest, because no threat it is ever realistically likely to face would justify a force large enough to put its own people's freedom at risk. Break the union into squabbling pieces, though, and all of that changes. Frightened, rival little states, each one eyeing the others, would each build up real armies out of fear, and then the very danger Hamilton has spent the whole paper shrinking would come roaring straight back. Union is not only about strength against the outside world. It is, once again, the thing that keeps our own armies small enough to stay safe.

Step back and look at what Numbers Twenty-Five and Twenty-Six have done together, because they are really one argument told in two motions. Twenty-Five cleared the ground. It knocked down the two things frightened people most wanted to reach for, handing the army to the states, and banning it outright on paper, and showed that both were worse than useless. Twenty-Six then built something in the cleared space. It said, here is what actually protects you. Not a sentence in a document. A living machine. A legislature forced to choose, out loud, on the record, over and over, forever. An opposition party that will always have every reason in the world to howl about the army. State governments watching from below, ready to be the voice and the arm of the people's alarm. And time itself standing guard, because tyranny by army is slow, and slowness is visible, and a free people that is paying even a little attention will see it coming.

That is Hamilton's real answer to the oldest fear in republican politics. You do not keep the soldiers from turning on you by pretending you will never have any. You keep them from turning on you by never once letting the decision to keep them go quiet, or private, or permanent. Freedom, in this paper, is not a wall. It is a habit. The habit of making the government ask again, in the open, and the habit of watching while it answers. Parchment does not guard a free people. Attention does.

Next time, Hamilton keeps building. Having shown that the legislature, and not a paper ban, is the true guardian against military power, he turns to a quieter but just as important question. Why would a well-run national government need to lean on soldiers to be obeyed at all? In Federalist Number Twenty-Seven, still Alexander Hamilton, the argument moves away from the army and toward the ordinary, everyday authority of good government, and toward the idea that a union which governs its citizens well, and touches their lives directly and fairly, earns the kind of obedience that no bayonet can ever produce.
