At the end of the last episode, Hamilton was standing at the customs house. Federalist Number Twelve was his paper about revenue — the argument that a government can only tax money that moves, that cash-poor American farmers could never fill a treasury through taxes on land, and that the one tax America could actually collect was a quiet duty on imported goods, gathered at the docks before anyone felt it. But that tax came with one giant condition. Only a united America can collect it. One country has a single side to guard — the Atlantic coast — and a few armed ships can do the guarding. A divided America would have land borders running in every direction, smugglers strolling across them at night, and the only way to stop them would be a French-style army of internal tax patrols, with powers Hamilton said would be intolerable in a free country.

So the last two episodes have been about money coming in. Episode Eleven was about earning it — commerce, shipping, one big market that Europe would have to bargain with. Episode Twelve was about collecting it — the customs duty, the one tax that works. Today, Hamilton finishes the thought with the third piece, and it is the piece every household on earth already understands. It is not enough to earn money and collect it. You also have to stop pouring it away.

Federalist Number Thirteen was published in the Independent Journal on November twenty-eighth, seventeen eighty-seven. Alexander Hamilton, still signing as Publius. And here is the first thing you should know about it. It is the shortest essay in the entire Federalist Papers. Not one of the shortest. The shortest — out of all eighty-five, this is the smallest one. It runs under a thousand words. Read aloud at a normal pace, the whole paper takes about six minutes.

It is worth pausing on the calendar for a second too, because the timing tells you something about the man writing it. Federalist Number Eleven came out on Saturday, November twenty-fourth. Number Twelve came out the following Tuesday, the twenty-seventh. Number Thirteen — this paper — came out the very next day, Wednesday the twenty-eighth. And Number Fourteen, which is Madison's, came out that Friday, the thirtieth. Four essays inside a single week, three of them Hamilton's, all while he was practicing law in New York. Publius was not a research project. Publius was a sprint. And Number Thirteen reads like exactly what it is — a fast, confident note from a man in the middle of a much larger argument, tying off one loose end before racing on to the next.

But do not let the size fool you. Because folded inside this little essay is one of the most quietly chilling predictions in all eighty-five papers. Hamilton stops, looks at the map of America, and tells you exactly how the country would break apart if it ever did break. How many pieces. Where the line would run. And which state would end up as the battlefield. And he gets it eerily close to right — not in his lifetime, but seventy-four years later. We are going to spend real time on that prediction today, because it is the heart of this paper, and one of the strangest moments in the whole series.

First, though, you need to hear the objection Hamilton is answering. Because this paper is an answer — and the objection it answers was probably the single most relatable complaint anyone made against the Constitution.

Forget liberty for a moment. Forget tyranny, standing armies, the rights of states. The objection was: what is this thing going to cost me?

Picture a farmer in the Hudson Valley in the fall of seventeen eighty-seven. He already pays taxes to the state of New York. Albany has a governor, a legislature, judges, sheriffs, clerks — and his taxes pay for all of it. Now somebody hands him this new Constitution, and he reads it, or hears it read aloud at the tavern, and what does it describe? A whole second government, stacked on top of the one he already has. A Congress with two chambers. A president. Federal courts. Federal judges. Federal tax collectors. Ambassadors. A treasury full of clerks. Every one of those people will draw a salary, and every one of those salaries will come, in the end, out of pockets like his.

The writers opposing the Constitution hammered this point relentlessly, because it worked. You did not need a law degree to feel it. The new plan looked like paying for two governments where you currently pay for one. And you might be thinking — that seems like a fair worry. It was. America in seventeen eighty-seven was not a rich country with money to spare. It was a country still drowning in war debt, full of farmers who, as we saw last episode, often did not have a single coin in the house. Telling those people, in that economy, that they were about to fund an entire new layer of government — that was not a paranoid objection. That was arithmetic.

So that is the question on the table. Can America afford this Constitution?

And Hamilton's answer is one of his favorite moves in the entire series. He does not argue with the arithmetic. He argues with the comparison. You are comparing the Constitution to a fantasy, he says in effect. You are imagining that if you reject this plan, you keep your cheap, simple, one-layer world — just your state government, no national expenses, money saved. That world is not on the menu. It does not exist.

Because here is what Hamilton has been arguing for twelve papers now. If the Constitution fails, the union dissolves. And if the union dissolves, the states do not float along as thirteen happy little independent republics. Hamilton calls that idea — thirteen separate sovereignties — too extravagant to have many serious supporters, and even its supporters knew it. No, the states would clump. They would form confederacies — two or three of them — because none of them could survive alone. And every one of those confederacies would need a government. A real one. A national-scale one. With a congress, an executive, courts, diplomats, tax collectors, and an army.

In other words — reject the Constitution because one national government seems too expensive, and what you get instead is two or three national governments. The thing you were trying to avoid paying for once, you will end up paying for in duplicate, or in triplicate.

That is the whole argument of Federalist Number Thirteen, and Hamilton lays it out in the very first lines. He opens with the simplest possible thought — money a government does not waste in one place can be used in another, and every dollar saved is a dollar less drawn from the pockets of the people. Then comes the heart of it, in one sentence. If the States are united under one government, he writes, there will be but one national civil list to support; if they are divided into several confederacies, there will be as many different national civil lists to be provided for.

The civil list. That phrase needs translating, because it carries the whole paper. The term comes from Britain, where the civil list was, literally, a list — the roster of what it cost to run the civil side of the kingdom. The king's household, the ministers, the judges, the ambassadors, the customs officers, the clerks. All of them, with their salaries, written out line by line. Civil, as opposed to military — that is the whole reason for the name. The civil list was what it cost simply to have a working national government, before you bought a single cannon.

So Hamilton's point, in plain English: a country needs one of those lists, and it pays for every name on it. Stay united, and America maintains one list. Split into confederacies, and America maintains two or three complete lists — two or three congresses, two or three court systems, two or three diplomatic corps, two or three treasuries — to govern the exact same number of people.

And then Hamilton adds the clause that turns a budgeting point into a real idea. Each of those duplicate lists, he says, would be coextensive with — meaning every bit as large as — the single list needed for a government of the whole, at least in its principal departments.

At this point you might be wondering — wait, how can that be true? If a confederacy only governs four states instead of thirteen, should its government not cost roughly a third as much? Would the math not more or less wash out?

That is exactly the question Hamilton wants you to ask, because his answer is the smartest idea in the paper. No. The math does not wash out. Because government is not priced per person. Government is mostly a fixed cost.

Think about what a national government has to have, no matter how big or small the nation is. It needs a legislature to make laws. It needs an executive to carry them out. It needs courts. It needs a treasury that keeps the accounts, and a system to collect the revenue. It needs people who deal with foreign countries — ambassadors, consuls, negotiators. It needs, in short, a complete machine. And the machine is the expense. A nation of three million people needs every one of those parts. A nation of ten million people needs — the same list of parts. You cannot run a country on half a treasury or two-thirds of a court system. The machine comes whole, or it does not work at all.

Hamilton points across the ocean to make this concrete. The island of Britain, he notes, holds about eight million people. Stop and compare that to America — eight million is more than twice the population of all thirteen American states put together. And Britain is governed, fully governed, down to the last village, by one set of national institutions. One parliament. One treasury. One set of courts. Now, each of the proposed American confederacies would hold three or four million people, and would cover territory larger than Britain itself. Is the government of such a confederacy going to be meaningfully smaller, or meaningfully cheaper, than the government of Britain? Of course not. It needs the same machine. Which means dividing America into three confederacies means building something on the scale of the British government three times over, side by side, on one continent — to govern the same number of people that one government could handle.

And then Hamilton compresses the whole idea into one sentence, and it is the best sentence in the paper. Civil power, properly organized and exerted, he writes, is capable of diffusing its force to a very great extent; and can, in a manner, reproduce itself in every part of a great empire by a judicious arrangement of subordinate institutions.

Let me unpack that, because underneath the eighteenth-century wording is an idea every modern organization runs on. Civil power can reproduce itself. One national government does not need to be rebuilt from scratch in every region it governs. It extends itself — through local offices, through smaller institutions arranged underneath it, carrying its force outward. The expensive part, the top of the machine, gets built once. Everything below it is cheap by comparison. That is why a government for ten million people does not cost three times what a government for three million costs. It costs barely more at all. The bigger the country, the less its government costs per person — because the same machine spreads across more and more people.

We have a name for this now. We call it economies of scale, and it shows up everywhere once you look for it. It is the reason merged companies immediately combine their finance departments, their legal teams, their payroll offices — one set of overhead instead of two is the very first saving the accountants count. It is the reason two households cost nearly twice as much to run as one, even when the same people and the same total income are involved. Anyone who has watched a family split into two homes has felt Hamilton's civil list arithmetic personally. Same people. Same income. Two rents. Two of everything.

And you can watch Hamilton's exact argument operating in the world right now. In the eastern Caribbean today, there is a group of small island nations — Saint Lucia, Grenada, Dominica, and several of their neighbors — that share a single currency, a single central bank, and a single supreme court. Not because they are sentimental about each other. Because each island running its own central bank and its own high court would cost more than a small island can afford. They looked at the price of operating a complete national machine for a tiny population, and they did precisely what Hamilton told the thirteen states to do. Pay for the machine once, together, and share it.

So that is the economic argument. And honestly, if Federalist Number Thirteen were just that, it would be a footnote — a tidy little point about overhead, tucked between bigger papers. What makes this essay unforgettable is what Hamilton does next. Because to finish the argument, he has to answer one more question — if America did break apart, how many confederacies would there actually be? — and answering it pulls him into something close to prophecy.

Remember the standard assumption. All through these papers, when people talked about the union breaking up, they talked about three confederacies. Jay used that picture back in Federalist Number Five — a northern confederacy, a middle one, a southern one. Hamilton repeats the conventional wisdom here: the men who speculate about what he calls the dismemberment of the empire generally imagine three pieces — the four Northern states, the four Middle states, and the five Southern states. Thirteen states, three clumps. That was how nearly everyone pictured the breakup.

Hamilton looks at that conventional wisdom, then he looks at the map, and he says — no. That is not how it would go. And what follows is the most coldly realistic passage in the paper. Hamilton plays out the collapse of his own country the way a chess player works through a forced sequence of moves.

If we attend carefully to geographical and commercial considerations, he writes, in conjunction with the habits and prejudices of the different States, we shall be led to conclude that in case of disunion they will most naturally league themselves under two governments. Two. Not three.

Then he walks through it, state by state, like dominoes. Watch how each move forces the next one.

The first domino is New England — the four states people then called the Eastern states: New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Connecticut. They unite. That much, Hamilton says, is certain. They share everything — origins, religion, economy, habits. They are the most natural bloc on the continent.

The second domino is New York. And here Hamilton is talking directly to his own readers, because these essays are running in New York newspapers. Now, you might ask — why would New York not simply stand on its own, or lead a middle confederacy of its own? Hamilton's answer is brutally practical. Look at the map. New York's entire eastern border presses against New England. Standing apart from New England means standing in front of New England — alone, with what Hamilton calls a feeble and unsupported flank exposed to the weight of the strongest power on the continent. No government in Albany would ever choose that. New York joins the northern league. Not out of affection. Because geography leaves it no other sane option.

The third domino is New Jersey, and Hamilton needs only a sentence. New Jersey, he says, is too small a state to think of being a frontier — to be the border country standing between two rival powers. It would be crushed. It joins the north.

And the fourth domino is where this paper turns haunting. Pennsylvania. Hamilton treats Pennsylvania as the one genuinely open question — the state that could go either way. Its economy points north. Pennsylvania has ships, ports, and an active foreign trade of its own — which, Hamilton says, makes the commercial north its natural home. But there is a problem. If Pennsylvania joins the north, then Pennsylvania becomes the northern confederacy's southern edge — the border country, the front line, the state whose fields the armies would cross. So Pennsylvania might choose the south instead. Not because it belongs there, but on the cold calculation that if you must live next to a rival power, it is better to have your exposed side facing the weaker neighbor than the stronger one.

And then Hamilton writes the sentence this whole episode has been building toward. This would give her, he writes, the fairest chance to avoid being the Flanders of America.

The Flanders of America.

Every educated reader in seventeen eighty-seven knew exactly what that phrase meant, and it should make your blood run a little cold. Flanders is the stretch of low, flat, rich country that today sits mostly in Belgium. And for centuries before Hamilton wrote — and, though he could not know it, for centuries after — Flanders was where Europe went to fight. Not because the people of Flanders were warlike. Because of where they lived. Flanders sat between the great powers — between France and the Dutch, between France and Spain's empire, between France and England's allies — and it was flat, and rich, and in the way. So when the great powers fought, which was constantly, their armies marched into Flanders and met there. Other people's wars, fought across your fields, generation after generation — so reliably that the region would come to be called the cockpit of Europe. The fighting pit. That is what the word Flanders meant to Hamilton's readers. The country in between. The place that gets fought over, again and again, simply because it is the place where rival powers meet.

So follow what Hamilton is actually saying, in this supposedly dry little paper about government payroll. If America divides, it divides in two. And whichever state ends up on the seam between those two halves — Pennsylvania, by his reckoning — becomes the Flanders of America. The in-between country. The place where the armies meet. Somebody's farms become the fields that other people's wars get fought across.

Whatever Pennsylvania decides, Hamilton concludes, the result is the same shape. One confederacy in the north, one in the south. Two governments. One long, armed, suspicious border running across the middle of America.

A fair question here is whether this is just Hamilton doing what Hamilton always does — painting the most frightening picture available, because he is, after all, a man trying to scare New Yorkers into ratifying a Constitution. So let us hold his prediction up against what actually happened. Because America did, eventually, divide.

Eighteen sixty-one. Seventy-four years after this little essay ran in a New York newspaper, eleven southern states walked out of the union and formed a separate government — the Confederate States of America.

Count the pieces. Not three confederacies. Two. One north, one south, divided along exactly the axis Hamilton drew — the commercial, shipping, manufacturing north on one side, the agricultural south on the other. The conventional wisdom of seventeen eighty-seven said America would crack into three pieces. Hamilton said the real fault line ran one direction — north against south — and that the continent would sort itself into two governments. Seventy-four years later, that is precisely what it did.

Now, honesty requires saying what he got wrong, because it matters. Hamilton drew his line too far north. He thought the border would fall around Pennsylvania and New Jersey, with everything south of them in one confederacy. The actual line of eighteen sixty-one fell further south — Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Maryland all stayed with the north. And the force that finally broke the country was not the one Hamilton was reasoning from. He was thinking about shipping interests and commercial habits. The thing that actually split America was slavery — a fault line this paper does not touch at all. So no, Hamilton did not predict the Civil War. He was answering a budget question in seventeen eighty-seven, not prophesying eighteen sixty-one.

But here is what he did get right, and it is the part that should genuinely unsettle you. He got the structure right. Two pieces, not three. North against south. And — this is the eerie part — he got the geography of the suffering right. Hamilton said that whichever state sat on the seam between the two confederacies would become the Flanders of America, the in-between country where the armies meet. Now look at where the Civil War was actually fought. The bloodiest single day in all of American history happened at Antietam — in Maryland, a state on the seam. And the bloodiest battle of the entire war — three days of fighting that left some fifty thousand men killed, wounded, or missing — happened at Gettysburg. In Pennsylvania. The state Hamilton named. In the summer of eighteen sixty-three, Lee's army marched north across Maryland, crossed into Pennsylvania, and collided with the Union army about eight miles past the border, at a small town where ten roads happened to meet. The two halves of a broken union, meeting in battle, on the soil of the state Hamilton had pointed to seventy-six years earlier when he warned that a divided America would have to have a Flanders.

So why does that matter? Not because Hamilton was a prophet. He was not, and he would have been the first to say so. It matters because it shows you what kind of arguments these papers are. Hamilton's prediction did not come from a vision. It came from looking at a map and asking the most unsentimental questions possible. Where does the power sit? Where does the commerce flow? Who can survive being a border state, and who cannot? When you reason that way — coldly, structurally, with no flattery about how special your own people are — you can describe how a thing will break long before it breaks. That is what the Federalist Papers are underneath all the eighteenth-century prose. Not patriotic literature. Structural engineering reports, written about a country, by men trying to keep it standing.

Which brings us back to the bill. Because Hamilton, having taken his detour through the map, returns at the end of this little paper to the question he started with — what does disunion actually cost? — and he closes the ledger with two sentences.

The first is the conclusion of the economy argument. Nothing can be more evident, he writes, than that the thirteen States will be able to support a national government better than one half, or one third, or any number less than the whole. The expense objection to the Constitution gets the comparison exactly backwards. The union is not the expensive option. The union is the cheap one. It is the only arrangement on the table where America pays for the machinery of national government once.

And then comes the final sentence of the paper — and notice what Hamilton does with it. He stops adding up this paper's bill, and adds up the whole series so far. Count the cost of maintaining several civil lists instead of one — that is this paper's argument. Then add the cost of all the officers who would have to guard the inland borders between confederacies against smuggling — that is the argument of Federalist Number Twelve, the army of customs patrols we talked about last episode. Then add the standing armies that rival confederacies would inevitably build against each other — that is the argument of Federalist Number Eight, the paper about how fear slowly militarizes free societies. Add it all up, Hamilton says, and we shall clearly discover that a separation would be not less injurious to the economy, than to the tranquillity, commerce, revenue, and liberty of every part.

Economy, tranquillity, commerce, revenue, and liberty — five words at the end of the shortest paper in the series, and every one of them is an essay we have already covered. That closing line is Hamilton tying off the entire case so far in a single sentence. Disunion costs you on every line of the ledger at once. It costs you peace. It costs you trade. It costs you the ability to collect taxes. It costs you freedom. And — the modest contribution of this little paper — it also just plain costs more money.

Step back, and you can see the shape of what Hamilton has built across these three papers. Federalist Number Eleven: union is how America earns. One market, one merchant fleet, one navy — the difference between a country that sets prices and a country that takes them. Federalist Number Twelve: union is how America collects. One coastline, one customs service, the one tax that actually works. Federalist Number Thirteen: union is how America keeps what it collects. One government instead of three. One payroll instead of three. No internal borders to police, and no internal arms races to fund. Earn it, collect it, do not waste it. The economic case for the Constitution, complete, in three essays published inside of one week.

And there is one more thing worth carrying out of this paper, because it is the move that makes Hamilton Hamilton — and once you see it, you will see it everywhere, in his writing and in nearly every political argument you will ever hear.

The expense objection compared the Constitution to the world its critics already lived in. Keep things as they are, and you pay for one layer of government. Adopt the Constitution, and you pay for two. On those terms, the critics were right. The Constitution was the expensive option. Hamilton's entire answer was to attack the terms. The world you live in, he said, is not one of your choices. The Articles of Confederation are failing. The union is dissolving underneath you whether you ratify or not. So the real choice is not between the Constitution and what you have now. It is between the Constitution and what comes after what you have now collapses. And what comes after is two or three rival nations, each with its own full government, its own border patrols, and its own army — all of it billed to the same three million people.

Compare the proposal to the real alternative, not to the world you wish you could keep. That is the discipline running through every one of these papers. It is also, frankly, a discipline almost nobody has ever managed consistently — not in seventeen eighty-seven, and not since. We compare every change to an imagined version of today that stays frozen, safe, and free. Hamilton's relentless point is that today does not stay frozen. The status quo is not actually one of your options. Things are already in motion, and the only honest comparison is between futures.

That is what the shortest paper in the series is really about. Not payroll. Comparison. How to think honestly about what things cost, when one of your options is imaginary.

Madison returns for Federalist Number Fourteen, and he takes on the objection that has been hanging over this entire project from the very first essay — the claim that America is simply too big. Too big for self-government. Too big for the people to control their representatives. Too big to be a republic at all, because no republic in history has ever stretched across such a distance. Madison's answer is the paper where he draws his famous line between a democracy and a republic — and it ends with one of the most stirring passages in all eighty-five essays, a plea to his countrymen not to let the voice of the past talk them out of attempting something simply because it has never been done before.
